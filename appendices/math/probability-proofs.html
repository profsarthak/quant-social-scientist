Probability Proofs: Inequalities, the Law of Large Numbers & the CLT

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source

The three results that turn probability into statistics — Markov’s and Chebyshev’s inequalities, the Law of Large Numbers, and the Central Limit Theorem — and the short, honest proofs that connect them in a chain, each earning the next.

Four theorems, one staircase. Markov bounds a tail with a mean; Chebyshev applies Markov to the squared deviation; the Law of Large Numbers applies Chebyshev to the sample mean; and the Central Limit Theorem says how that mean settles — around a bell curve, whatever you started from. Each step is a few lines, and each uses only the one before.

Why the proofs, not just the statements

Everywhere else in this encyclopedia, math serves intuition and the derivations go to the back. This is the back. The point of collecting these four proofs is that they form a chain — you can watch each result being built from the last with nothing more than the definition of variance and one clever choice of event. Seeing the chain is what makes the Central Limit Theorem feel inevitable instead of magical, and what makes consistency a thing you can prove rather than assume. These are the proofs behind the intuition-first main-track entries on inequalities & convergence and the law of large numbers; together they are the load-bearing theorems under estimation, confidence intervals, and every large-sample argument in econometrics.

Markov’s inequality: the seed

The whole staircase starts here. For any non-negative random variable X and any a>0 (Ross 2018): \Pr(X \ge a) \le \frac{\mathrm{E}(X)}{a}. Proof (one line of honest bookkeeping). Because X\ge 0, the indicator a\,\mathbf{1}\{X\ge a\} \le X pointwise — if X<a the left side is 0\le X; if X\ge a it is a\le X. Take expectations: a\,\Pr(X\ge a) \le \mathrm{E}(X), and divide by a. That is the entire proof. It says something almost too cheap to be useful — a variable that is usually small can’t often be large — yet everything below is leverage on this one fact.

Chebyshev’s inequality: Markov, applied to the squared deviation

Point Markov at the squared distance from the mean and you get a bound that uses the variance. For any random variable X with mean \mu and any \epsilon>0 (Grinstead and Snell 1997): \Pr(|X-\mu| \ge \epsilon) \le \frac{\operatorname{Var}(X)}{\epsilon^2}. Proof. The event \{|X-\mu|\ge\epsilon\} is identical to \{(X-\mu)^2 \ge \epsilon^2\}. Apply Markov’s inequality to the non-negative variable (X-\mu)^2 with threshold \epsilon^2: \Pr\!\big((X-\mu)^2 \ge \epsilon^2\big) \le \frac{\mathrm{E}[(X-\mu)^2]}{\epsilon^2} = \frac{\operatorname{Var}(X)}{\epsilon^2}. Grinstead & Snell give the equivalent direct argument: \operatorname{Var}(X)=\sum_x (x-\mu)^2 m(x) is a sum of non-negative terms, and throwing away all terms with |x-\mu|<\epsilon only shrinks it, leaving \ge \epsilon^2 \Pr(|X-\mu|\ge\epsilon) (Grinstead and Snell 1997). Writing \epsilon=k\sigma gives the famous reading: the chance of landing more than k standard deviations from the mean is at most 1/k^2 — for any distribution (Grinstead and Snell 1997). Five sigma? At most 4%. No bell curve assumed.

The bound is deliberately loose — it must hold for every distribution at once — but it is tight: the two-point law placing mass \tfrac12 at \mu\pm\epsilon attains it with equality, so no distribution-free bound can do better (Grinstead and Snell 1997). The figure makes both facts visible.

Show the figure code 
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(3)
N = 400_000
ks = np.linspace(1.0, 4.0, 25)

def tail_fraction(x, ks):
    x = (x - x.mean()) / x.std()          # standardise to mean 0, sd 1
    return [np.mean(np.abs(x) >= k) for k in ks]

normal = rng.normal(0, 1, N)
uniform = rng.uniform(-1, 1, N)
# Two-point law at ±1 (mass 1/2 each): the extremal case that saturates Chebyshev
twopoint = rng.choice([-1.0, 1.0], size=N)

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7.4, 4.2))
ax.plot(ks, 1.0/ks**2, color="#e8531d", lw=2.6, label="Chebyshev bound  $1/k^2$")
ax.plot(ks, tail_fraction(normal, ks),  color="#161613", lw=2.0, label="Normal")
ax.plot(ks, tail_fraction(uniform, ks), color="#5e5e54", lw=2.0, label="Uniform")
ax.plot(ks, tail_fraction(twopoint, ks), color="#8a8a7e", lw=2.0,
        label="Two-point $\\pm1$ (extremal)")
ax.set_xlabel("$k$  (standard deviations from the mean)")
ax.set_ylabel(r"$\Pr(|X-\mu| \geq k\sigma)$")
ax.set_title("The distribution-free bound holds for all — and is loose for nice ones")
ax.legend(fontsize=8.5, frameon=False)
fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()

The (weak) Law of Large Numbers: Chebyshev, applied to the sample mean

Now let X_1,\dots,X_n be i.i.d. with mean \mu and finite variance \sigma^2, and let \bar X_n = \tfrac1n\sum_i X_i. Then for every \epsilon>0 (Grinstead and Snell 1997): \Pr(|\bar X_n - \mu| \ge \epsilon) \to 0 \quad\text{as } n\to\infty, which we write \operatorname{plim}\bar X_n = \mu — the sample mean converges in probability to the population mean.

Proof. Two facts about the sample mean: \mathrm{E}(\bar X_n)=\mu and, by independence, \operatorname{Var}(\bar X_n)=\sigma^2/n. Feed these straight into Chebyshev (Grinstead and Snell 1997): \Pr(|\bar X_n - \mu| \ge \epsilon) \le \frac{\operatorname{Var}(\bar X_n)}{\epsilon^2} = \frac{\sigma^2}{n\,\epsilon^2} \xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{} 0 . That is the whole proof: the variance of the average shrinks like 1/n, Chebyshev converts shrinking variance into a vanishing tail, and the average is trapped ever more tightly around \mu. This is the theorem that licenses Monte Carlo integration (average an indicator, get an area) and the empirical CDF \hat F_n(x)\to F(x) that underwrites nonparametric statistics — both are just sample means of indicators, so both inherit the LLN (Ross 2018). The strong law strengthens “in probability” to “almost surely” (the sequence itself converges, not merely its tail probabilities), a deeper result requiring more than Chebyshev (Grinstead and Snell 1997).

The Central Limit Theorem: not just whether, but how

The LLN says \bar X_n lands on \mu; the CLT says the leftover wobble, magnified by \sqrt n, is normal — no matter what distribution you drew from. For i.i.d. X_i with mean \mu and variance \sigma^2, the standardised sum (Ross 2014) Z_n = \frac{\bar X_n - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n} = \frac{\sum_i X_i - n\mu}{\sigma\sqrt n} \ \xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}\ \mathrm{N}(0,1) in distribution: \Pr(Z_n\le z)\to\Phi(z) for every z (Ross 2014). “Herein lies its power,” as Ross puts it — the limit is standard normal for any starting distribution with finite variance (Ross 2014).

Proof sketch via moment generating functions. The engine is a uniqueness theorem: an MGF, where it exists, determines the distribution, and the MGF of a sum of independents is the product of their MGFs (Ross 2014). Center and scale each variable to W_i=(X_i-\mu)/\sigma (mean 0, variance 1), so Z_n=\tfrac1{\sqrt n}\sum_i W_i. Independence gives M_{Z_n}(t) = \Big[\,M_W\!\big(t/\sqrt n\big)\Big]^n . Expand the inner MGF to second order — M_W(s)=1 + s\,\mathrm{E}(W) + \tfrac{s^2}{2}\mathrm{E}(W^2)+\cdots = 1 + \tfrac{s^2}{2} + o(s^2) since \mathrm{E}(W)=0,\ \mathrm{E}(W^2)=1. With s=t/\sqrt n, M_{Z_n}(t) = \Big[\,1 + \frac{t^2}{2n} + o\!\big(\tfrac1n\big)\Big]^{n} \xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{} e^{t^2/2}, using (1+c/n)^n\to e^{c}. But e^{t^2/2} is exactly the MGF of the standard normal (Grinstead and Snell 1997) — and by uniqueness, matching MGFs means matching distributions (Ross 2014). The bell curve is not assumed anywhere; it falls out of the second moment being all that survives the \sqrt n rescaling. (The fully rigorous version uses characteristic functions, which always exist; the flagship CLT article sketches that route.)

Common pitfalls

WarningWhere these results get misused

  • Applying Markov/Chebyshev and expecting tightness. They are worst-case, distribution-free bounds. For a roughly normal quantity, Chebyshev’s “≤ 1/k²” is wildly conservative — use it to guarantee, not to estimate (Grinstead and Snell 1997).

  • Thinking the CLT rescues a biased estimator. The CLT describes the sampling distribution of a centered statistic; it says nothing about whether the center is the right target. A consistent, asymptotically-normal estimator of the wrong parameter is still wrong.

  • Ignoring the finite-variance hypothesis. Both the LLN-via-Chebyshev proof and the classical CLT assume \sigma^2<\infty. For heavy-tailed data (infinite variance), sample means need not stabilise and the limit, if any, is not normal — a Cauchy sample mean is as variable as a single draw.

  • Reading “large n” as a fixed threshold. How large is enough depends on the population’s skew and tails; the normal approximation can be excellent by n\approx 20 for tame data and poor at n=1000 for very skewed data (Wooldridge 2019).

## Chebyshev's inequality: Markov, applied to the squared deviation

$$

$$
**Proof.** The event $\{|X-\mu|\ge\epsilon\}$ is identical to $\{(X-\mu)^2 \ge \epsilon^2\}$. Apply Markov's inequality to the non-negative variable $(X-\mu)^2$ with threshold $\epsilon^2$:
$$
\Pr\!\big((X-\mu)^2 \ge \epsilon^2\big) \le \frac{\mathrm{E}[(X-\mu)^2]}{\epsilon^2} = \frac{\operatorname{Var}(X)}{\epsilon^2}.
$$
Grinstead & Snell give the equivalent direct argument: $\operatorname{Var}(X)=\sum_x (x-\mu)^2 m(x)$ is a sum of non-negative terms, and throwing away all terms with $|x-\mu|<\epsilon$ only shrinks it, leaving $\ge \epsilon^2 \Pr(|X-\mu|\ge\epsilon)$ [@grinstead_snell]. Writing $\epsilon=k\sigma$ gives the famous reading: **the chance of landing more than $k$ standard deviations from the mean is at most $1/k^2$ — for *any* distribution** [@grinstead_snell]. ## The (weak) Law of Large Numbers: Chebyshev, applied to the sample mean

Now let $X_1,\dots,X_n$ be i.i.d. with mean $\mu$ and finite variance $\sigma^2$, and let $\bar X_n = \tfrac1n\sum_i X_i$. Then for every $\epsilon>0$ [@grinstead_snell]:
$$
\Pr(|\bar X_n - \mu| \ge \epsilon) \to 0 \quad\text{as } n\to\infty,
$$
which we write $\operatorname{plim}\bar X_n = \mu$ — the sample mean **converges in probability** to the population mean.

**Proof.** Two facts about the sample mean: $\mathrm{E}(\bar X_n)=\mu$ and, by independence, $\operatorname{Var}(\bar X_n)=\sigma^2/n$. Feed these straight into Chebyshev [@grinstead_snell]:
$$
\Pr(|\bar X_n - \mu| \ge \epsilon) \le \frac{\operatorname{Var}(\bar X_n)}{\epsilon^2} = \frac{\sigma^2}{n\,\epsilon^2} \xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{} 0 .
$$
That is the whole proof: the variance of the average shrinks like $1/n$, Chebyshev converts shrinking variance into a vanishing tail, and the average is trapped ever more tightly around $\mu$. This is the theorem that licenses **Monte Carlo integration** (average an indicator, get an area) and the **empirical CDF** $\hat F_n(x)\to F(x)$ that underwrites nonparametric statistics — both are just sample means of indicators, so both inherit the LLN [@ross_first_course]. The *strong* law strengthens "in probability" to "almost surely" (the sequence itself converges, not merely its tail probabilities), a deeper result requiring more than Chebyshev [@grinstead_snell].

## The Central Limit Theorem: not just *whether*, but *how*

The LLN says $\bar X_n$ lands on $\mu$; the CLT says the leftover wobble, magnified by $\sqrt n$, is **normal** — no matter what distribution you drew from. For i.i.d. $X_i$ with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$, the standardised sum [@ross_prob_models]
$$
Z_n = \frac{\bar X_n - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n} = \frac{\sum_i X_i - n\mu}{\sigma\sqrt n}
\ \xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}\ \mathrm{N}(0,1)
$$
in distribution: $\Pr(Z_n\le z)\to\Phi(z)$ for every $z$ [@ross_prob_models]. "Herein lies its power," as Ross puts it — the limit is standard normal *for any starting distribution with finite variance* [@ross_prob_models].

**Proof sketch via moment generating functions.** The engine is a uniqueness theorem: an MGF, where it exists, **determines the distribution**, and the MGF of a sum of independents is the product of their MGFs [@ross_prob_models]. Center and scale each variable to $W_i=(X_i-\mu)/\sigma$ (mean 0, variance 1), so $Z_n=\tfrac1{\sqrt n}\sum_i W_i$. Independence gives
$$
M_{Z_n}(t) = \Big[\,M_W\!\big(t/\sqrt n\big)\Big]^n .
$$
Expand the inner MGF to second order — $M_W(s)=1 + s\,\mathrm{E}(W) + \tfrac{s^2}{2}\mathrm{E}(W^2)+\cdots = 1 + \tfrac{s^2}{2} + o(s^2)$ since $\mathrm{E}(W)=0,\ \mathrm{E}(W^2)=1$. With $s=t/\sqrt n$,
$$
M_{Z_n}(t) = \Big[\,1 + \frac{t^2}{2n} + o\!\big(\tfrac1n\big)\Big]^{n} \xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{} e^{t^2/2},
$$
using $(1+c/n)^n\to e^{c}$. But $e^{t^2/2}$ is exactly the MGF of the standard normal [@grinstead_snell] — and by uniqueness, matching MGFs means matching distributions [@ross_prob_models]. The bell curve is not assumed anywhere; it *falls out* of the second moment being all that survives the $\sqrt n$ rescaling. (The fully rigorous version uses characteristic functions, which always exist; the flagship [CLT article](../../content/02-inference/sampling-distributions-clt.qmd) sketches that route.)

## Common pitfalls

::: {.callout-warning title="Where these results get misused"}

- **Applying Markov/Chebyshev and expecting tightness.** They are worst-case, distribution-free bounds. For a roughly normal quantity, Chebyshev's "≤ 1/k²" is wildly conservative — use it to *guarantee*, not to *estimate* [@grinstead_snell].

- **Thinking the CLT rescues a biased estimator.** The CLT describes the *sampling distribution* of a centered statistic; it says nothing about whether the center is the right target. A consistent, asymptotically-normal estimator of the *wrong* parameter is still wrong.

- **Ignoring the finite-variance hypothesis.** Both the LLN-via-Chebyshev proof and the classical CLT assume $\sigma^2<\infty$. For heavy-tailed data (infinite variance), sample means need not stabilise and the limit, if any, is not normal — a Cauchy sample mean is as variable as a single draw.

- **Reading "large $n$" as a fixed threshold.** How large is enough depends on the population's skew and tails; the normal approximation can be excellent by $n\approx 20$ for tame data and poor at $n=1000$ for very skewed data [@wooldridge_intro].

:::

## From the sources {.unnumbered}

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Who says what"}
- **Ross, *A First Course in Probability*** — Markov's inequality and its indicator-bound proof; Chebyshev as its corollary; the LLN as the justification for Monte Carlo integration and the empirical CDF ($\hat F_n \to F$).
- **Grinstead & Snell, *Introduction to Probability*** (Ch. 8) — the Chebyshev inequality with the "throw away the inner terms" proof; the extremal two-point law that saturates it; the Law of Large Numbers proved *directly* from Chebyshev via $\operatorname{Var}(\bar X_n)=\sigma^2/n$; the weak-vs-strong distinction; and $e^{t^2/2}$ as the normal MGF.
- **Ross, *Introduction to Probability Models*** — the MGF machinery (product rule for independent sums, uniqueness of the MGF↔distribution correspondence) and the CLT statement, with the "holds for any distribution" emphasis.
- **"Notes" (Goyal, after Blitzstein–Hwang / Bertsekas–Tsitsiklis)** — the compact catalogue tying the inequalities (Markov, Chebyshev, Jensen, Cauchy–Schwarz), convergence in probability, the sample mean, the WLLN, and the CLT into one sequence — the scaffold this entry follows.
:::
