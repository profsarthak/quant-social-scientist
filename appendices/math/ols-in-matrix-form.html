OLS in Matrix Form

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source

One line of linear algebra — \hat{\boldsymbol\beta}=(X'X)^{-1}X'y — replaces a page of summation notation and, once you read it, explains everything the scalar formulas only hinted at: what a coefficient is, why collinearity bites, and why OLS is the best you can do among linear estimators.

Stack the data into a matrix and the whole of least squares collapses to project y onto the columns of X. The estimator, its unbiasedness, its variance, and the Gauss–Markov optimality theorem all fall out of a single formula — and the formula tells you why, not just what.

Why bother with matrices

The scalar formulas for regression — \hat\beta_1=\sum(x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y)/\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2 and its multivariate cousins — get unwieldy fast. With k regressors you are staring at k+1 coupled normal equations, and every proof (unbiasedness, the variance formula, Gauss–Markov) turns into bookkeeping. Matrix notation is not decoration: it is the language in which the multiple regression results become short enough to see through. Wooldridge relegates it to an appendix precisely because it “proves some of the results that we either stated without proof, proved only in special cases, or proved through a more cumbersome method” (Wooldridge 2019). This is that appendix for this encyclopedia — the canonical home for the (X'X)^{-1} fragments that appear inline in regression diagnostics and heteroskedasticity & GLS.

The setup: one equation for all n observations

Write the model for observation t as y_t = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_{t1} + \dots + \beta_k x_{tk} + u_t. Now stack: let y be the n\times 1 vector of outcomes, X the n\times(k+1) matrix whose first column is all ones (the intercept) and whose other columns are the regressors, \boldsymbol\beta the (k+1)\times 1 vector of parameters, and u the n\times 1 vector of errors. Then the entire sample is (Wooldridge 2019) y = X\boldsymbol\beta + u . That one line holds all n equations at once. Everything below is what you can do with it.

The normal equations: setting the gradient to zero

OLS picks \hat{\boldsymbol\beta} to minimise the sum of squared residuals, now a clean quadratic form (Wooldridge 2019): \mathrm{SSR}(b) = (y - Xb)'(y - Xb) . Differentiate with respect to the vector b and set the gradient to zero — the multivariable version of the first-order condition. The derivative of (y_t - x_t b)^2 is -2(y_t - x_t b)x_t', so summing gives the first-order condition X'(y - X\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}) = 0, i.e. the normal equations (X'X)\,\hat{\boldsymbol\beta} = X'y . If the (k+1)\times(k+1) matrix X'X is invertible, premultiply by (X'X)^{-1} to get the formula that runs every regression on Earth (Wooldridge 2019): \boxed{\;\hat{\boldsymbol\beta} = (X'X)^{-1}X'y\;}

WarningThe tempting cancellation that is wrong

It is tempting to write (X'X)^{-1}X'y = X^{-1}(X')^{-1}X'y = X^{-1}y. The flaw: X is n\times(k+1), almost never square, so it has no inverse — you cannot split (X'X)^{-1} into X^{-1}(X')^{-1} unless n=k+1, which never happens in practice (Wooldridge 2019). The whole point of (X'X)^{-1} is that it inverts a small square matrix built from a tall rectangular one.

Invertibility is the no-collinearity assumption. X'X is invertible exactly when X has full column rank k+1 — when the regressor columns are linearly independent. This is the matrix version of “no perfect collinearity”: if one regressor is an exact linear combination of the others, X'X is singular and \hat{\boldsymbol\beta} is not unique (Wooldridge 2019). Near-collinearity doesn’t break the formula but inflates (X'X)^{-1}, which is why standard errors explode.

What the formula says: fitted values, residuals, and orthogonality

The fitted values are \hat y = X\hat{\boldsymbol\beta} and the residuals are \hat u = y - \hat y. The first-order condition X'\hat u = 0 carries real content: because the first column of X is ones, it forces the residuals to sum to zero and to be uncorrelated with every regressor in-sample (Wooldridge 2019). Substituting \hat{\boldsymbol\beta} makes \hat y = X(X'X)^{-1}X'y = Hy, where H = X(X'X)^{-1}X' is the hat matrix — the orthogonal projector onto the column space of X. Its complement M = I_n - H is the residual maker: \hat u = My. Both are symmetric and idempotent (H^2=H, M^2=M), the algebraic signature of a projection. This H is the same object whose diagonal entries are the leverages h_{tt} used in regression diagnostics — a point’s leverage is literally how much weight the projection puts on its own outcome.

NoteRegression anatomy — what a single coefficient means

The matrix formula is compact but opaque about individual coefficients. Angrist & Pischke’s regression anatomy formula reopens it (Angrist and Pischke 2009): \hat\beta_k = \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(y,\ \tilde x_k)}{\operatorname{Var}(\tilde x_k)}, where \tilde x_k is the residual from regressing x_k on all the other regressors. In words: each multiple-regression coefficient is the simple slope on the part of its regressor that is orthogonal to everything else — the variable after partialling out the others. This is the Frisch–Waugh–Lovell theorem, and the figure below shows it holding exactly.

Show the figure code 
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
n = 120
# x2 correlated with x1 so that the raw x1-slope is biased for beta1
x2 = rng.normal(0, 1, n)
x1 = 0.8 * x2 + rng.normal(0, 0.7, n)
beta = np.array([1.0, 2.0, -1.5])          # intercept, beta1, beta2
y = beta[0] + beta[1]*x1 + beta[2]*x2 + rng.normal(0, 1.0, n)

# Full OLS by the matrix formula
X = np.column_stack([np.ones(n), x1, x2])
beta_hat = np.linalg.solve(X.T @ X, X.T @ y)

# Partial x2 out of y and out of x1 (residual maker for W = [1, x2])
W = np.column_stack([np.ones(n), x2])
def resid(v):
    return v - W @ np.linalg.solve(W.T @ W, W.T @ v)
y_t, x1_t = resid(y), resid(x1)
slope_fwl = (x1_t @ y_t) / (x1_t @ x1_t)    # regression-anatomy slope

fig, (axL, axR) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(8.6, 4.0))

axL.scatter(x1, y, s=14, color="#5e5e54", alpha=0.8)
axL.set_xlabel("$x_1$ (raw)"); axL.set_ylabel("$y$ (raw)")
axL.set_title("Raw: slope contaminated by $x_2$", fontsize=10)

axR.scatter(x1_t, y_t, s=14, color="#161613", alpha=0.8)
xs = np.linspace(x1_t.min(), x1_t.max(), 50)
axR.plot(xs, slope_fwl*xs, color="#e8531d", lw=2.4,
         label=f"partialled slope = {slope_fwl:.4f}")
axR.set_xlabel(r"$\tilde x_1 = x_1$ after removing $x_2$")
axR.set_ylabel(r"$\tilde y = y$ after removing $x_2$")
axR.set_title(r"Partialled: slope $=\hat\beta_1$", fontsize=10)
axR.legend(fontsize=8.5, frameon=False, loc="upper left")

fig.suptitle(f"Multiple-regression $\\hat\\beta_1$ from $(X'X)^{{-1}}X'y$ = {beta_hat[1]:.4f}"
             f"   →   matches partialled slope {slope_fwl:.4f}", fontsize=10.5)
fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()

Unbiasedness, the variance matrix, and Gauss–Markov — in three moves

With the matrix formula, the three central OLS results are a few lines each (Wooldridge 2019).

Unbiasedness. Substitute y = X\boldsymbol\beta + u: \hat{\boldsymbol\beta} = (X'X)^{-1}X'(X\boldsymbol\beta + u) = \boldsymbol\beta + (X'X)^{-1}X'u . The estimator equals truth plus a linear function of the errors. Take expectations conditional on X; under \mathrm{E}(u\mid X)=0 the second term vanishes, so \mathrm{E}(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}\mid X)=\boldsymbol\beta.

The variance–covariance matrix. From the same \hat{\boldsymbol\beta} - \boldsymbol\beta = (X'X)^{-1}X'u, and assuming homoskedastic, serially uncorrelated errors so that \operatorname{Var}(u\mid X)=\sigma^2 I_n, \operatorname{Var}(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}\mid X) = (X'X)^{-1}X'(\sigma^2 I_n)X(X'X)^{-1} = \sigma^2 (X'X)^{-1} . The jth diagonal entry is \operatorname{Var}(\hat\beta_j\mid X); the off-diagonals are the coefficient covariances. When \operatorname{Var}(u\mid X)=\Omega\neq\sigma^2 I, the middle collapse fails and you get the sandwich \left(X'X\right)^{-1}(X'\Omega X)(X'X)^{-1} — the object robust/White standard errors estimate.

Gauss–Markov (OLS is BLUE). Under the Gauss–Markov assumptions, any linear unbiased estimator is \tilde{\boldsymbol\beta}=Ay with AX=I. Its variance minus OLS’s works out to \sigma^2 A M A' with M = I_n - X(X'X)^{-1}X' the residual maker; since M is symmetric and idempotent, AMA' is positive semi-definite, so no linear unbiased estimator beats OLS on variance (Wooldridge 2019). That is the Best Linear Unbiased Estimator theorem in three lines — a statement the scalar approach can only gesture at.

Common pitfalls

WarningWhere matrix intuition goes wrong

  • Reading (X'X)^{-1} as X^{-1}(X')^{-1}. X is rectangular; it has no inverse. The compact form exists only as the inverse of the square Gram matrix X'X (Wooldridge 2019).

  • Forgetting the assumptions attach to different results. Unbiasedness needs only \mathrm{E}(u\mid X)=0. The tidy \sigma^2(X'X)^{-1} variance and Gauss–Markov additionally need homoskedasticity and no serial correlation — drop those and the estimator is still unbiased but the reported standard errors lie (Wooldridge 2019).

  • Treating Gauss–Markov as “OLS is best, full stop.” It is best among linear, unbiased estimators under its assumptions. A biased estimator (ridge) or a nonlinear one can have smaller mean-squared error, and under non-normal errors OLS need not be efficient at all (Ashley 2012).

  • Numerically inverting X'X by hand. With near-collinear regressors, X'X is ill-conditioned and explicit inversion loses precision; software solves the normal equations by QR or Cholesky, not by forming (X'X)^{-1}. The formula is for understanding, not for computation.

NoteWho says what
  • Wooldridge, Introductory Econometrics, Appendix E (“The Linear Regression Model in Matrix Form”) — the stacked model y=X\boldsymbol\beta+u; SSR as a quadratic form; the first-order condition X'\hat u=0, the normal equations (X'X)\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}=X'y, and \hat{\boldsymbol\beta}=(X'X)^{-1}X'y; the warning against the X^{-1} cancellation; rank / no-collinearity as invertibility of X'X; Theorems E.1–E.3 (unbiasedness, \operatorname{Var}=\sigma^2(X'X)^{-1}, and the full Gauss–Markov proof via the idempotent residual maker M).
  • Angrist & Pischke, Mostly Harmless Econometrics — the regression anatomy formula \hat\beta_k=\operatorname{Cov}(y,\tilde x_k)/\operatorname{Var}(\tilde x_k) (Frisch–Waugh–Lovell): each coefficient is a simple slope on the partialled-out regressor — the reading of (X'X)^{-1}X'y used in the figure.
  • Angrist & Pischke, Mastering ’Metrics — the plain-language framing that a regression coefficient is a variance-weighted, other-things-held-fixed comparison, motivating why the matrix machinery is worth the trouble.
  • Ashley, Fundamentals of Applied Econometrics — the caution that BLUE is conditional on its assumptions, and that efficiency claims fail under heteroskedasticity / non-normal errors.

Connections

  • Builds on: simple and multiple regression (the scalar formulas this generalises), and the linear-algebra of projections.
  • Underwrites: regression diagnostics (the hat matrix H and leverage) and heteroskedasticity, robust errors & GLS (the sandwich variance is \sigma^2(X'X)^{-1} with a non-scalar middle).

Right: after partialling x₂ out of *both* y and x₁ (taking residuals from a regression on [1, x₂]), the slope of the partialled scatter equals the multiple-regression coefficient β̂₁ from (X'X)⁻¹X'y to machine precision. A coefficient in a multiple regression is a simple slope on the orthogonalised regressor — exactly what the matrix formula computes for all coefficients at once."
#| code-fold: true
#| code-summary: "Show the figure code"

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
n = 120
# x2 correlated with x1 so that the raw x1-slope is biased for beta1
x2 = rng.normal(0, 1, n)
x1 = 0.8 * x2 + rng.normal(0, 0.7, n)
beta = np.array([1.0, 2.0, -1.5])          # intercept, beta1, beta2
y = beta[0] + beta[1]*x1 + beta[2]*x2 + rng.normal(0, 1.0, n)

# Full OLS by the matrix formula
X = np.column_stack([np.ones(n), x1, x2])
beta_hat = np.linalg.solve(X.T @ X, X.T @ y)

# Partial x2 out of y and out of x1 (residual maker for W = [1, x2])
W = np.column_stack([np.ones(n), x2])
def resid(v):
    return v - W @ np.linalg.solve(W.T @ W, W.T @ v)
y_t, x1_t = resid(y), resid(x1)
slope_fwl = (x1_t @ y_t) / (x1_t @ x1_t)    # regression-anatomy slope

fig, (axL, axR) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(8.6, 4.0))

axL.scatter(x1, y, s=14, color="#5e5e54", alpha=0.8)
axL.set_xlabel("$x_1$ (raw)"); axL.set_ylabel("$y$ (raw)")
axL.set_title("Raw: slope contaminated by $x_2$", fontsize=10)

axR.scatter(x1_t, y_t, s=14, color="#161613", alpha=0.8)
xs = np.linspace(x1_t.min(), x1_t.max(), 50)
axR.plot(xs, slope_fwl*xs, color="#e8531d", lw=2.4,
         label=f"partialled slope = {slope_fwl:.4f}")
axR.set_xlabel(r"$\tilde x_1 = x_1$ after removing $x_2$")
axR.set_ylabel(r"$\tilde y = y$ after removing $x_2$")
axR.set_title(r"Partialled: slope $=\hat\beta_1$", fontsize=10)
axR.legend(fontsize=8.5, frameon=False, loc="upper left")

fig.suptitle(f"Multiple-regression $\\hat\\beta_1$ from $(X'X)^{{-1}}X'y$ = {beta_hat[1]:.4f}"
             f"   →   matches partialled slope {slope_fwl:.4f}", fontsize=10.5)
fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()

## Unbiasedness, the variance matrix, and Gauss–Markov — in three moves

With the matrix formula, the three central OLS results are a few lines each [@wooldridge_intro].

**Unbiasedness.** Substitute $y = X\boldsymbol\beta + u$:
$$
\hat{\boldsymbol\beta} = (X'X)^{-1}X'(X\boldsymbol\beta + u) = \boldsymbol\beta + (X'X)^{-1}X'u .
$$
The estimator equals truth plus a linear function of the errors. Take expectations conditional on $X$; under $\mathrm{E}(u\mid X)=0$ the second term vanishes, so $\mathrm{E}(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}\mid X)=\boldsymbol\beta$.

**The variance–covariance matrix.** From the same $\hat{\boldsymbol\beta} - \boldsymbol\beta = (X'X)^{-1}X'u$, and assuming homoskedastic, serially uncorrelated errors so that $\operatorname{Var}(u\mid X)=\sigma^2 I_n$,
$$
\operatorname{Var}(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}\mid X) = (X'X)^{-1}X'(\sigma^2 I_n)X(X'X)^{-1} = \sigma^2 (X'X)^{-1} .
$$
The $j$th diagonal entry is $\operatorname{Var}(\hat\beta_j\mid X)$; the off-diagonals are the coefficient covariances. When $\operatorname{Var}(u\mid X)=\Omega\neq\sigma^2 I$, the middle collapse fails and you get the **sandwich** $\left(X'X\right)^{-1}(X'\Omega X)(X'X)^{-1}$ — the object [robust/White standard errors](../../content/03-regression/heteroskedasticity-and-gls.qmd) estimate.

**Gauss–Markov (OLS is BLUE).** Under the [Gauss–Markov assumptions](../../content/03-regression/gauss-markov-assumptions.qmd), any linear unbiased estimator is $\tilde{\boldsymbol\beta}=Ay$ with $AX=I$. Its variance minus OLS's works out to $\sigma^2 A M A'$ with $M = I_n - X(X'X)^{-1}X'$ the residual maker; since $M$ is symmetric and idempotent, $AMA'$ is positive semi-definite, so *no* linear unbiased estimator beats OLS on variance [@wooldridge_intro]. That is the **B**est **L**inear **U**nbiased **E**stimator theorem in three lines — a statement the scalar approach can only gesture at.

## Common pitfalls

::: {.callout-warning title="Where matrix intuition goes wrong"}

- **Reading $(X'X)^{-1}$ as $X^{-1}(X')^{-1}$.** $X$ is rectangular; it has no inverse. The compact form exists *only* as the inverse of the square Gram matrix $X'X$ [@wooldridge_intro].

- **Forgetting the assumptions attach to different results.** Unbiasedness needs only $\mathrm{E}(u\mid X)=0$. The tidy $\sigma^2(X'X)^{-1}$ variance and Gauss–Markov *additionally* need homoskedasticity and no serial correlation — drop those and the estimator is still unbiased but the reported standard errors lie [@wooldridge_intro].

- **Treating Gauss–Markov as "OLS is best, full stop."** It is best among *linear, unbiased* estimators under its assumptions. A biased estimator (ridge) or a nonlinear one can have smaller mean-squared error, and under non-normal errors OLS need not be efficient at all [@ashley_econometrics].

- **Numerically inverting $X'X$ by hand.** With near-collinear regressors, $X'X$ is ill-conditioned and explicit inversion loses precision; software solves the normal equations by QR or Cholesky, not by forming $(X'X)^{-1}$. The formula is for *understanding*, not for computation.

:::

## From the sources {.unnumbered}

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Who says what"}
- **Wooldridge, *Introductory Econometrics*, Appendix E ("The Linear Regression Model in Matrix Form")** — the stacked model $y=X\boldsymbol\beta+u$; SSR as a quadratic form; the first-order condition $X'\hat u=0$, the normal equations $(X'X)\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}=X'y$, and $\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}=(X'X)^{-1}X'y$; the warning against the $X^{-1}$ cancellation; rank / no-collinearity as invertibility of $X'X$; Theorems E.1–E.3 (unbiasedness, $\operatorname{Var}=\sigma^2(X'X)^{-1}$, and the full Gauss–Markov proof via the idempotent residual maker $M$).
- **Angrist & Pischke, *Mostly Harmless Econometrics*** — the **regression anatomy** formula $\hat\beta_k=\operatorname{Cov}(y,\tilde x_k)/\operatorname{Var}(\tilde x_k)$ (Frisch–Waugh–Lovell): each coefficient is a simple slope on the partialled-out regressor — the reading of $(X'X)^{-1}X'y$ used in the figure.
- **Angrist & Pischke, *Mastering 'Metrics*** — the plain-language framing that a regression coefficient is a variance-weighted, other-things-held-fixed comparison, motivating why the matrix machinery is worth the trouble.
- **Ashley, *Fundamentals of Applied Econometrics*** — the caution that BLUE is conditional on its assumptions, and that efficiency claims fail under heteroskedasticity / non-normal errors.
:::
