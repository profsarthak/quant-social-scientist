Linear Algebra You Actually Need

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source

The few tools for handling whole tables of numbers at once — what they are, what they do, and where they turn up in regression, covariance and machine learning. No prior course needed.

You do not need a full course in linear algebra to read this encyclopedia — you need four tools and to know what each one does. A matrix is a table of numbers that can also act on other numbers; its rank says how much genuinely separate information it holds; its inverse undoes it, when undoing is possible; and a quadratic form is the one recipe behind variances, distances and the shape of a likelihood. Those four carry regression, covariance and most of machine learning.

TipWhere you probably came from — and what you need

You likely arrived from OLS in matrix form (where “X'X must be invertible” appears), covariance & correlation (where a whole grid of covariances appears), matching & propensity scores (Mahalanobis distance), the Kalman filter, or the eigenvalue arguments in the complexity entries.

You need: school algebra, and the idea of a variance and a covariance. You don’t need determinants beyond 2\times2, proofs, or any prior linear algebra — every operation is shown on numbers first.

Jump to what you came for: the idea in words · all four tools on one example · rank & collinearity · the inverse · quadratic forms & eigenvalues

Why it matters

Open any regression output with more than one variable and the numbers came from matrix arithmetic. The “no perfect collinearity” assumption in every econometrics textbook is a statement about rank. The standard errors are read off the diagonal of an inverse. The variance of a portfolio, of an index built from several survey items, or of a treatment-effect estimate that combines two coefficients is a quadratic form. And when a maximum-likelihood routine reports that “the Hessian is not positive definite”, it is telling you, in this vocabulary, that the likelihood has no clean peak. Wooldridge keeps “the working subset” an econometrician needs in a short appendix (Wooldridge 2019); Simon & Blume build it out for economists from scratch (Simon and Blume 1994). This page is that subset, with the meaning attached.

The idea in words

Think of a spreadsheet. A matrix is just that grid of numbers — rows are people, columns are variables. But a grid can also do something: feed it a list of weights and it hands back a new list (each row’s weighted total). That is the second life of a matrix — a machine that transforms lists of numbers, the way a recipe turns quantities of ingredients into a total cost.

Once you see a matrix as a machine, three questions are natural:

  1. Does it waste any inputs? If one column is just a copy (or a multiple) of another, the machine cannot tell those two inputs apart. The rank counts the columns that carry new information. In regression, a column that duplicates others is perfect collinearity: the data cannot separate the two effects.
  2. Can it be run backwards? Given the output, can you recover the input? If nothing was wasted, yes — and the backwards machine is the inverse. If something was wasted, no: two different inputs gave the same output, so there is nothing unique to recover.
  3. What shape does it make? Squaring and combining numbers through a symmetric grid gives a single number — a quadratic form. Variances, squared distances and the curvature of a hill are all built this way, and whether the result is always positive decides whether you have a bowl (one clear bottom) or a saddle.

All four tools on one small example

Two test scores — maths and reading — for a class. Each has variance 2, and they move together with covariance 1. Collect those numbers in a grid, the covariance matrix: \Sigma=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}.

Acting on a list. Multiplying a matrix by a list means: for each row, multiply across and add. So \Sigma\begin{pmatrix}1\\2\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}2\cdot1+1\cdot2\\1\cdot1+2\cdot2\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}4\\5\end{pmatrix}.

Rank. The two columns (2,1) and (1,2) point in different directions — neither is a multiple of the other — so the rank is 2: full. Contrast \begin{pmatrix}1&2\\2&4\end{pmatrix}, whose second column is exactly twice the first (income in rupees and income in half-rupees, say). Its rank is 1: only one column’s worth of information.

The inverse. For a 2\times2 grid, swap the diagonal, flip the signs off it, and divide by the determinant ad-bc (here 2\cdot2-1\cdot1=3): \Sigma^{-1}=\frac13\begin{pmatrix}2&-1\\-1&2\end{pmatrix}. Run it on the output from before and you get the input back: \Sigma^{-1}(4,5)'=(1,2)'. For the rank-1 grid the determinant is 1\cdot4-2\cdot2=0 — you would be dividing by zero. No inverse.

A quadratic form. What is the variance of the total score, maths + reading? Weight both by 1 and run the weights through \Sigma on both sides: 2+2+2\cdot1=6. The variance of the gap, maths − reading, uses weights (1,-1): 2+2-2\cdot1=2. Same grid, different weights, different variance — and because the scores move together, the total is much noisier than the gap. That “weights on both sides” calculation is the quadratic form c'\Sigma c.

Eigenvalues — the grid’s natural directions. Feed \Sigma the list (1,1) and it returns (3,3): the same direction, stretched 3 times. Feed it (1,-1) and it returns (1,-1): same direction, stretched once. Directions that come out merely stretched are eigenvectors; the stretch factors (3 and 1) are eigenvalues. Here they say: the “overall ability” direction carries three times the variance of the “maths-versus-reading” direction. Both are positive, so every weighted combination has positive variance — the matrix is positive definite. Every idea below is one of these five moves, written generally.

NoteNotation key — open if a symbol is unfamiliar
  • x, c — vectors: lists of numbers, written as a column. x_i is the ith entry.
  • A, B, \Sigma, X — matrices. A_{ij} is the entry in row i, column j. “n\times k” means n rows, k columns.
  • Ax — the matrix acting on the vector (row-by-row weighted sums). AB — do B, then A.
  • A^\top or A' — the transpose: rows become columns. Both notations are used on this site.
  • I — the identity matrix (ones on the diagonal, zeros elsewhere): the “do nothing” machine.
  • A^{-1} — the inverse: the machine that undoes A. \det A — the determinant, a single number that is zero exactly when A cannot be undone.
  • x^\top A x — a quadratic form: one number. \lambda — an eigenvalue; Q, \Lambda — the matrices of eigenvectors and of eigenvalues.

Vectors and matrices: data, and maps

A vector is an ordered list of numbers — a data point, a coefficient set, a direction. A matrix is a grid, and it plays two roles at once: it stacks data (rows = observations, columns = variables, as in the design matrix X), and it acts as a linear map x \mapsto Ax that rotates, scales, and projects vectors. (Simon and Blume 1994) The two roles meet in regression: X\beta is simultaneously “the data times the coefficients” and “the vector \beta mapped into the space of fitted values.”

Matrix multiplication AB composes maps (do B, then A); it is associative — (AB)C=A(BC), so brackets don’t matter — but not commutative (AB \neq BA in general). With A=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix} and B=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\1&1\end{pmatrix}, AB=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix} but BA=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}: order matters, as it does for “put on socks, then shoes”. The transpose A^\top flips rows and columns, and (AB)^\top = B^\top A^\top (undo in reverse order). The identity I leaves vectors unchanged. (Simon and Blume 1994)

Rank: how much independent information

The rank of a matrix is the number of linearly independent columns (equivalently, rows) — the dimension of the space its columns can reach. Columns are linearly independent when none can be built as a weighted sum of the others. A matrix has full column rank when no column is a linear combination (weighted sum) of the others. (Simon and Blume 1994; Wooldridge 2019)

In plain words: rank = the number of columns that each bring something new. In the worked example, \Sigma has rank 2; the grid with a doubled column has rank 1.

This single idea is the whole content of the “no perfect collinearity” assumption: X must have full column rank for X^\top X to be invertible and the OLS coefficients to be unique (see OLS in matrix form). Rank-deficiency is not a numerical nuisance; it means the data genuinely cannot separate two effects.

The inverse: undoing a map

A square matrix A is invertible (nonsingular) when there is an A^{-1} with A^{-1}A = AA^{-1} = I. It exists iff (“if and only if”) A has full rank, iff its determinant is nonzero. (Simon and Blume 1994)

In plain words: a machine can be run backwards exactly when it loses no information — and the determinant is the one-number test for that. (\Sigma above: determinant 3, inverse exists; the doubled-column grid: determinant 0, none.)

The inverse solves linear systems: Ax = b \Rightarrow x = A^{-1}b — though in practice you solve the system rather than form the inverse (it is faster and more stable, the lesson carried in OLS in matrix form). Only square, full-rank matrices invert; the tall design matrix X never does, which is exactly why OLS needs the Gram matrix X^\top X (the square grid of every column paired with every other column — always square, however tall X is).

Quadratic forms: variances, distances, curvature

A quadratic form is a scalar built from a symmetric matrix A (one equal to its own transpose, so A_{ij}=A_{ji}) and a vector x: Q(x) = x^\top A x = \sum_i \sum_j A_{ij}\, x_i x_j .

In plain words: multiply every pair of entries of x, weight each product by the matching cell of A, and add everything up. With two entries it is A_{11}x_1^2+2A_{12}x_1x_2+A_{22}x_2^2 — the “2+2+2\cdot1=6” of the test-score example.

This is the shape that variances and distances take. A covariance matrix \Sigma is symmetric, and the variance of a linear combination c^\top X is the quadratic form c^\top \Sigma c. (Simon and Blume 1994) A is positive definite if x^\top A x > 0 for every nonzero x — the matrix analogue of “positive,” and the condition that makes Q a genuine bowl with a unique minimum. (For a covariance matrix it says: every weighted combination of the variables has some spread.) Positive-definiteness is why covariance matrices are invertible, why X^\top X is a bowl that least squares can minimise, and (via the Hessian) why a log-likelihood has a well-defined peak.

Every symmetric A has an eigendecomposition A = Q\Lambda Q^\top: orthogonal (mutually perpendicular) eigenvectors (the Q columns) and real eigenvalues (the \Lambda diagonal). Positive-definite means all eigenvalues are positive.

In plain words: every symmetric matrix is just “stretch along a few perpendicular directions” — the eigenvectors give the directions, the eigenvalues the stretch. For the test scores: stretch 3 along “total” (1,1), stretch 1 along “gap” (1,-1), both positive, so positive definite.

Geometrically, the eigenvectors are the axes of the ellipse x^\top A x = 1, and the eigenvalues set how stretched it is — the picture below. (Its contours are also lines of equal Mahalanobis distance: distance measured in standard-deviation units that allow for correlation, the yardstick used in matching.)

Show the figure code 
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# a positive-definite covariance matrix (symmetric, eigenvalues > 0)
Sigma = np.array([[2.0, 1.1],
                  [1.1, 1.0]])
vals, vecs = np.linalg.eigh(Sigma)          # symmetric eigendecomposition
Sinv = np.linalg.inv(Sigma)

# grid of the quadratic form x^T Sigma^{-1} x
g = np.linspace(-4, 4, 300)
XX, YY = np.meshgrid(g, g)
Q = Sinv[0,0]*XX**2 + 2*Sinv[0,1]*XX*YY + Sinv[1,1]*YY**2

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(5.6, 5.2))
cs = ax.contour(XX, YY, Q, levels=[1, 4, 9], colors="#161613", linewidths=1.6)
ax.clabel(cs, fmt={1:"c=1", 4:"c=4", 9:"c=9"}, fontsize=8)
# eigenvectors, scaled by sqrt(eigenvalue) = the ellipse axes
for i in range(2):
    v = vecs[:, i] * np.sqrt(vals[i])
    ax.annotate("", xy=v, xytext=(0, 0),
                arrowprops=dict(arrowstyle="-|>", color="#e8531d", lw=2.6))
    ax.text(v[0]*1.15, v[1]*1.15, f"λ={vals[i]:.1f}", color="#cf4718", fontsize=9,
            ha="center", va="center")
ax.axhline(0, color="#b7b6a8", lw=0.8); ax.axvline(0, color="#b7b6a8", lw=0.8)
ax.set_aspect("equal"); ax.set_xlim(-4, 4); ax.set_ylim(-4, 4)
ax.set_title(r"Level sets of  $x^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}x$  — eigenvectors are the axes")
fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()

Common pitfalls

WarningWhere linear-algebra intuition slips

  • Assuming AB = BA. Matrix multiplication does not commute. Order is the composition order of maps; swapping it changes the answer (or fails to conform). (Simon and Blume 1994)

  • Treating a rectangular matrix as invertible. Only square, full-rank matrices have inverses. The tall design matrix X has none — which is the whole reason OLS works through X^\top X.

  • Forgetting “symmetric” before “eigenvalues are real.” The clean picture (real eigenvalues, orthogonal eigenvectors, the ellipse) is guaranteed for symmetric matrices — covariance matrices, X^\top X, Hessians. General matrices can have complex eigenvalues.

  • Confusing positive with positive-definite. A matrix of positive entries need not be positive definite; the condition is x^\top A x > 0 for all x \neq 0 — equivalently, all eigenvalues positive. This is what distinguishes a bowl (unique minimum) from a saddle. (Simon and Blume 1994)

  • Inverting when you should solve. Forming A^{-1} to compute A^{-1}b is slower and less stable than solving Ax=b directly — mathematically identical, numerically not.

NoteWho says what

Connections

  • Powers: OLS in matrix form (rank → invertibility of X^\top X; the Gram matrix and the projection) and the Gauss–Markov variance \sigma^2(X^\top X)^{-1} (a quadratic-form / covariance object).
  • Pairs with: calculus for optimisation & MLE — the Hessian is a symmetric matrix, and its positive-definiteness is the second-order condition for a minimum.
  • Shows up as: covariance matrices and the geometry of correlated data, and the multivariate normal (whose density is \exp(-\tfrac12 x^\top\Sigma^{-1}x) — the ellipse above).

Think of a spreadsheet. A **matrix** is just that grid of numbers — rows are people, columns are variables.
But a grid can also *do* something: feed it a list of weights and it hands back a new list (each row's
weighted total). That is the second life of a matrix — a **machine that transforms lists of numbers**, the
way a recipe turns quantities of ingredients into a total cost.

Once you see a matrix as a machine, three questions are natural:

1. **Does it waste any inputs?** If one column is just a copy (or a multiple) of another, the machine cannot
   tell those two inputs apart. The **rank** counts the columns that carry *new* information. In regression, a
   column that duplicates others is perfect collinearity: the data cannot separate the two effects.
2. **Can it be run backwards?** Given the output, can you recover the input? If nothing was wasted, yes — and
   the backwards machine is the **inverse**. If something was wasted, no: two different inputs gave the same
   output, so there is nothing unique to recover.
3. **What shape does it make?** Squaring and combining numbers through a symmetric grid gives a single number —
   a **quadratic form**. Variances, squared distances and the curvature of a hill are all built this way, and
   whether the result is always positive decides whether you have a bowl (one clear bottom) or a saddle.

## All four tools on one small example {#sec-by-hand}

Two test scores — maths and reading — for a class. Each has variance 2, and they move together with
covariance 1. Collect those numbers in a grid, the **covariance matrix**:
$$
\Sigma=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}.
$$

**Acting on a list.** Multiplying a matrix by a list means: for each row, multiply across and add. So
$\Sigma\begin{pmatrix}1\\2\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}2\cdot1+1\cdot2\\1\cdot1+2\cdot2\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}4\\5\end{pmatrix}$.

**Rank.** The two columns $(2,1)$ and $(1,2)$ point in different directions — neither is a multiple of the
other — so the rank is 2: full. Contrast $\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\2&4\end{pmatrix}$, whose second column is
exactly twice the first (income in rupees and income in *half*-rupees, say). Its rank is 1: only one column's
worth of information.

**The inverse.** For a $2\times2$ grid, swap the diagonal, flip the signs off it, and divide by the
**determinant** $ad-bc$ (here $2\cdot2-1\cdot1=3$):
$$
\Sigma^{-1}=\frac13\begin{pmatrix}2&-1\\-1&2\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Run it on the output from before and you get the input back: $\Sigma^{-1}(4,5)'=(1,2)'$. For the rank-1 grid
the determinant is $1\cdot4-2\cdot2=0$ — you would be dividing by zero. No inverse.

**A quadratic form.** What is the variance of the *total* score, maths + reading? Weight both by 1 and run the
weights through $\Sigma$ on both sides: $2+2+2\cdot1=6$. The variance of the *gap*, maths − reading, uses
weights $(1,-1)$: $2+2-2\cdot1=2$. Same grid, different weights, different variance — and because the scores
move together, the total is much noisier than the gap. That "weights on both sides" calculation is the
quadratic form $c'\Sigma c$.

**Eigenvalues — the grid's natural directions.** Feed $\Sigma$ the list $(1,1)$ and it returns $(3,3)$: the
same direction, stretched 3 times. Feed it $(1,-1)$ and it returns $(1,-1)$: same direction, stretched once.
Directions that come out merely stretched are **eigenvectors**; the stretch factors (3 and 1) are
**eigenvalues**. Here they say: the "overall ability" direction carries three times the variance of the
"maths-versus-reading" direction. Both are positive, so every weighted combination has positive variance —
the matrix is **positive definite**. Every idea below is one of these five moves, written generally.

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Notation key — open if a symbol is unfamiliar"}
- $x$, $c$ — vectors: lists of numbers, written as a column. $x_i$ is the $i$th entry.
- $A$, $B$, $\Sigma$, $X$ — matrices. $A_{ij}$ is the entry in row $i$, column $j$. "$n\times k$" means $n$ rows, $k$ columns.
- $Ax$ — the matrix acting on the vector (row-by-row weighted sums). $AB$ — do $B$, then $A$.
- $A^\top$ or $A'$ — the transpose: rows become columns. Both notations are used on this site.
- $I$ — the identity matrix (ones on the diagonal, zeros elsewhere): the "do nothing" machine.
- $A^{-1}$ — the inverse: the machine that undoes $A$. $\det A$ — the determinant, a single number that is
  zero exactly when $A$ cannot be undone.
- $x^\top A x$ — a quadratic form: one number. $\lambda$ — an eigenvalue; $Q$, $\Lambda$ — the matrices of
  eigenvectors and of eigenvalues.
:::

## Vectors and matrices: data, and maps

A **vector** is an ordered list of numbers — a data point, a coefficient set, a direction. A **matrix** is a
grid, and it plays two roles at once: it **stacks** data (rows = observations, columns = variables, as in the
design matrix $X$), and it **acts** as a linear map $x \mapsto Ax$ that rotates, scales, and projects vectors.
[@simon_blume] The two roles meet in regression: $X\beta$ is simultaneously "the data times the coefficients"
and "the vector $\beta$ mapped into the space of fitted values."

Matrix multiplication $AB$ composes maps (do $B$, then $A$); it is **associative** — $(AB)C=A(BC)$, so
brackets don't matter — but **not commutative** ($AB \neq BA$ in general). With
$A=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$ and $B=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\1&1\end{pmatrix}$, $AB=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}$
but $BA=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}$: order matters, as it does for "put on socks, then shoes". The
**transpose** $A^\top$ flips rows and columns, and $(AB)^\top = B^\top A^\top$ (undo in reverse order).
The **identity** $I$ leaves vectors unchanged. [@simon_blume]

## Rank: how much independent information {#sec-rank}

The **rank** of a matrix is the number of linearly independent columns (equivalently, rows) — the dimension of
the space its columns can reach. Columns are **linearly independent** when none can be built as a weighted sum
of the others. A matrix has **full column rank** when no column is a linear combination (weighted sum) of the
others. [@simon_blume; @wooldridge_intro]

> **In plain words:** rank = the number of columns that each bring something new. In the worked example,
> $\Sigma$ has rank 2; the grid with a doubled column has rank 1.

This single idea is the whole content of the "no perfect
collinearity" assumption: $X$ must have full column rank for $X^\top X$ to be invertible and the OLS
coefficients to be unique (see [OLS in matrix form](ols-in-matrix-form.qmd)). Rank-deficiency is not a numerical
nuisance; it means the data genuinely cannot separate two effects.

## The inverse: undoing a map {#sec-inverse}

A **square** matrix $A$ is **invertible** (nonsingular) when there is an $A^{-1}$ with $A^{-1}A = AA^{-1} = I$.
It exists **iff** ("if and only if") $A$ has full rank, iff its **determinant** is nonzero. [@simon_blume]

> **In plain words:** a machine can be run backwards exactly when it loses no information — and the
> determinant is the one-number test for that. ($\Sigma$ above: determinant 3, inverse exists; the
> doubled-column grid: determinant 0, none.)

The inverse solves linear
systems: $Ax = b \Rightarrow x = A^{-1}b$ — though in practice you **solve** the system rather than form the
inverse (it is faster and more stable, the lesson carried in [OLS in matrix form](ols-in-matrix-form.qmd)). Only
square, full-rank matrices invert; the tall design matrix $X$ never does, which is exactly why OLS needs the
*Gram* matrix $X^\top X$ (the square grid of every column paired with every other column — always square,
however tall $X$ is).

## Quadratic forms: variances, distances, curvature {#sec-quadratic}

A **quadratic form** is a scalar built from a symmetric matrix $A$ (one equal to its own transpose, so
$A_{ij}=A_{ji}$) and a vector $x$:
$$
Q(x) = x^\top A x = \sum_i \sum_j A_{ij}\, x_i x_j .
$$

> **In plain words:** multiply every pair of entries of $x$, weight each product by the matching cell of $A$,
> and add everything up. With two entries it is $A_{11}x_1^2+2A_{12}x_1x_2+A_{22}x_2^2$ — the "$2+2+2\cdot1=6$"
> of the test-score example.

This is the shape that variances and distances take. A **covariance matrix** $\Sigma$ is symmetric, and the
variance of a linear combination $c^\top X$ is the quadratic form $c^\top \Sigma c$. [@simon_blume] $A$ is
**positive definite** if $x^\top A x > 0$ for every nonzero $x$ — the matrix analogue of "positive," and the
condition that makes $Q$ a genuine bowl with a unique minimum. (For a covariance matrix it says: every
weighted combination of the variables has some spread.) Positive-definiteness is why covariance matrices
are invertible, why $X^\top X$ is a bowl that least squares can minimise, and (via the **Hessian**) why a
[log-likelihood has a well-defined peak](calculus-for-optimisation.qmd).

Every symmetric $A$ has an **eigendecomposition** $A = Q\Lambda Q^\top$: orthogonal (mutually perpendicular)
eigenvectors (the $Q$ columns) and real eigenvalues (the $\Lambda$ diagonal). Positive-definite means **all
eigenvalues are positive.**

> **In plain words:** every symmetric matrix is just "stretch along a few perpendicular directions" — the
> eigenvectors give the directions, the eigenvalues the stretch. For the test scores: stretch 3 along
> "total" $(1,1)$, stretch 1 along "gap" $(1,-1)$, both positive, so positive definite.

Geometrically, the eigenvectors are the axes of the ellipse $x^\top A x = 1$, and the eigenvalues
set how stretched it is — the picture below. (Its contours are also lines of equal **Mahalanobis distance**:
distance measured in standard-deviation units that allow for correlation, the yardstick used in
[matching](../../content/05-causal-inference/matching-and-propensity-scores.qmd).)

```{python}
#| label: fig-quadratic-form
#| fig-cap: "A quadratic form is an ellipse, and its eigenvectors are the axes. The curves are level sets xᵀΣ⁻¹x = c of the quadratic form built from a covariance matrix Σ — exactly the contours of a bivariate normal, or lines of equal Mahalanobis distance. The eigenvectors of Σ (orange) point along the ellipse's axes; each axis half-length scales with √eigenvalue, so a larger eigenvalue means more spread in that direction. Positive-definiteness is what guarantees closed elliptical contours (a genuine bowl) rather than a saddle. This one picture underlies covariance, the geometry of correlated data, and the curvature of a likelihood."
#| code-fold: true
#| code-summary: "Show the figure code"

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# a positive-definite covariance matrix (symmetric, eigenvalues > 0)
Sigma = np.array([[2.0, 1.1],
                  [1.1, 1.0]])
vals, vecs = np.linalg.eigh(Sigma)          # symmetric eigendecomposition
Sinv = np.linalg.inv(Sigma)

# grid of the quadratic form x^T Sigma^{-1} x
g = np.linspace(-4, 4, 300)
XX, YY = np.meshgrid(g, g)
Q = Sinv[0,0]*XX**2 + 2*Sinv[0,1]*XX*YY + Sinv[1,1]*YY**2

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(5.6, 5.2))
cs = ax.contour(XX, YY, Q, levels=[1, 4, 9], colors="#161613", linewidths=1.6)
ax.clabel(cs, fmt={1:"c=1", 4:"c=4", 9:"c=9"}, fontsize=8)
# eigenvectors, scaled by sqrt(eigenvalue) = the ellipse axes
for i in range(2):
    v = vecs[:, i] * np.sqrt(vals[i])
    ax.annotate("", xy=v, xytext=(0, 0),
                arrowprops=dict(arrowstyle="-|>", color="#e8531d", lw=2.6))
    ax.text(v[0]*1.15, v[1]*1.15, f"λ={vals[i]:.1f}", color="#cf4718", fontsize=9,
            ha="center", va="center")
ax.axhline(0, color="#b7b6a8", lw=0.8); ax.axvline(0, color="#b7b6a8", lw=0.8)
ax.set_aspect("equal"); ax.set_xlim(-4, 4); ax.set_ylim(-4, 4)
ax.set_title(r"Level sets of  $x^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}x$  — eigenvectors are the axes")
fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

## Common pitfalls

::: {.callout-warning title="Where linear-algebra intuition slips"}

- **Assuming $AB = BA$.** Matrix multiplication does not commute. Order is the composition order of maps;
  swapping it changes the answer (or fails to conform). [@simon_blume]

- **Treating a rectangular matrix as invertible.** Only square, full-rank matrices have inverses. The tall
  design matrix $X$ has none — which is the whole reason OLS works through $X^\top X$.

- **Forgetting "symmetric" before "eigenvalues are real."** The clean picture (real eigenvalues, orthogonal
  eigenvectors, the ellipse) is guaranteed for **symmetric** matrices — covariance matrices, $X^\top X$,
  Hessians. General matrices can have complex eigenvalues.

- **Confusing positive with positive-definite.** A matrix of positive *entries* need not be positive
  *definite*; the condition is $x^\top A x > 0$ for all $x \neq 0$ — equivalently, all eigenvalues positive.
  This is what distinguishes a bowl (unique minimum) from a saddle. [@simon_blume]

- **Inverting when you should solve.** Forming $A^{-1}$ to compute $A^{-1}b$ is slower and less stable than
  solving $Ax=b$ directly — mathematically identical, numerically not.

:::
