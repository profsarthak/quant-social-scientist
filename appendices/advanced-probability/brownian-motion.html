Brownian Motion

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source

What a coin-flip random walk becomes when the steps get tiny and fast: a continuous, jagged path whose spread grows with the square root of time. The bell curve, turned into a process, and the basic model of diffusion and of prices.

Flip a coin and step left or right; keep going. Make the steps smaller and faster in just the right proportion, and the zig-zag becomes a continuous, jagged path: Brownian motion. Wherever you stand on it, the next stretch is a fresh bell-curve shock, independent of the past. Its typical distance from the start grows with the square root of time, not with time itself. That one fact is why the bell curve shows up as the limit of so many accumulating small shocks.

TipWhere you probably came from — and what you need

You likely arrived from the random walk in the law of large numbers, the CLT proof (“why \sqrt n?”), the random-walk model in unit roots & stationarity, or the neighbouring Poisson process entry (the other basic continuous-time process).

You need: the normal distribution and its standard deviation, and the idea that variances of independent pieces add. You don’t need calculus or stochastic calculus. The worked example uses a normal table (or any calculator’s normal CDF).

Why it matters

Social scientists rarely study pollen grains, but they constantly meet quantities built from many small independent shocks: a price, an exchange rate, a running vote margin, the cumulative error in a measurement. Brownian motion is the baseline model for all of them, in the same way the Poisson process is the baseline for counts. Ross lists its uses as running from the physics of a particle in a liquid to goodness-of-fit testing and stock-market prices (Ross 2014). Its key lesson for applied work is the square-root law. If yesterday’s shocks are independent of today’s, uncertainty about the level two years out is not twice the one-year uncertainty but only about 1.4 times it, and an apparent “trend” over a short window can be pure noise.

The idea in words

Start with the coin-flip walk: each second, step one unit left or right. After 100 steps the total is a sum of 100 independent \pm1s, so its variance is 100 and its standard deviation is \sqrt{100}=10, not 100. To wander twice as far on average, you need four times as many steps. That is the square-root law.

Now speed the film up: take many more steps per second, each one smaller. If you shrink the step length too fast the walker never leaves the origin; too slowly and it flies off to infinity. Shrink it in proportion to the square root of the time between steps and the picture settles down to a continuous, endlessly jagged path. By the CLT, the position at any time is then normal. That limit is Brownian motion.

Three properties come along for free:

  1. Fresh shocks. What the walk does next is independent of what it did before (independent increments).
  2. Same rules all the time. A stretch of length t behaves the same whenever it starts (stationary increments).
  3. Normal, with variance growing like time. The displacement over a stretch of length t is normal with mean 0 and variance proportional to t.

A bicycle race, by hand

Ross’s Example 10.1 (Ross 2014): two cyclists race, and Y(t) is the number of seconds by which the inside rider leads when a fraction t of the race is done. Model Y as a Brownian motion with variance parameter \sigma^2. To put numbers on it, take \sigma=2 seconds: over the whole race the lead wanders with a standard deviation of 2 seconds.

Step 1 — leading at halfway. The inside rider leads by 1 second at the midpoint. What happens in the second half is independent of the first, and it is normal with mean 0 and variance \sigma^2\times\tfrac12=2 (standard deviation \approx1.41). She wins if the second half does not cost her more than 1 second: \Pr\{\text{win}\} = \Pr\{N(0,2) > -1\} = \Phi\!\left(\tfrac{1}{1.41}\right) = \Phi(0.71)\approx 0.76 .

Step 2 — looking back from the finish. Now suppose she won by 1 second. Was she ahead at halfway? Given the end point, the midpoint is normal with mean \tfrac12 of the final lead (0.5 s) and variance \sigma^2/4=1 (Ross 2014). So \Pr\{\text{ahead at halfway}\mid\text{won by 1 s}\}=\Pr\{N(0.5,1)>0\}=\Phi(0.5)\approx0.69 . Knowing the end point pulls the middle towards a straight line from start to finish.

Step 3 — ever ahead by 2 seconds? For standard Brownian motion over one unit of time, the chance of ending above 1 is 1-\Phi(1)\approx0.16. The chance of ever touching 1 along the way is exactly twice that, about 0.32 (Ross 2014). (Any path that touched 1 is equally likely to finish above or below it.) In the race, with \sigma=2, that is the chance the inside rider is ever 2 seconds ahead.

Step 4 — which comes first? Starting at 0, the chance of rising by A before falling by B is B/(A+B) (Ross 2014). Up 1 before down 2: 2/3. This is the gambler’s-ruin answer from the random walk, carried over to the limit.

NoteNotation key — open if a symbol is unfamiliar
  • t — time; W(t) (Ross writes X(t) or B(t)) — the position of standard Brownian motion at time t.
  • \sigma^2 — the variance parameter: variance accumulated per unit of time. Standard means \sigma=1.
  • N(\mu,\sigma^2) — a normal distribution with mean \mu and variance \sigma^2.
  • \Phi(x) — the standard normal CDF, \Pr\{N(0,1)\le x\}. \Phi(1)\approx0.84, \Phi(2)\approx0.98.
  • Increment — the change over a stretch of time, W(t)-W(s).
  • \xi_i — the i-th coin-flip step, +1 or -1; S_n — the walk’s position after n steps.
  • T_a — the first time the path reaches level a (a hitting time).

From random walk to a continuous path

Let S_n = \xi_1 + \dots + \xi_n be a symmetric random walk (each \xi_i = \pm 1). Rescale space by 1/\sqrt{n} and time by 1/n; as n\to\infty the rescaled walk converges to a limiting process W(t). (Ross 2014) The \sqrt{n} space-scaling is the same \sqrt{n} that appears in the CLT, and for the same reason: a sum of n independent steps has standard deviation \propto\sqrt{n}, so to keep the limit finite you must shrink space by exactly that factor. Brownian motion is what that limit is. Ross makes the point by contrast: shrink the step size in proportion to the time step instead, and the mean and variance both go to zero, so the limit is a walker that never moves (Ross 2014).

In plain words: the step length must shrink like the square root of the time step. Any faster and nothing happens; any slower and everything blows up.

The defining properties

Standard Brownian motion W(t) is characterised by four properties: (Ross 2014)

  • W(0) = 0;
  • independent increments — W(t)-W(s) is independent of the past up to s;
  • stationary Gaussian increments — W(t)-W(s)\sim\mathrm{N}(0,\,t-s), depending only on the elapsed time;
  • continuous paths — t\mapsto W(t) is continuous.

From these, \mathrm{E}[W(t)]=0 and \operatorname{Var}(W(t))=t: the variance grows linearly in time, so the typical displacement grows like \sqrt{t}.

In plain words: the cloud of possible paths stays centred on zero but widens like the square root of time. Step 1 of the race used exactly this: half the race, half the variance.

That single scaling, spread \propto\sqrt{t}, is the signature of diffusion, and the figure below is built around it. Ross notes that continuity of the paths is a deep fact, proved with probability one, though he does not prove it (Ross 2014).

Show the figure code 
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(2)
n_paths, n_steps, T = 40, 500, 1.0
dt = T / n_steps
t = np.linspace(0, T, n_steps + 1)

# increments ~ N(0, dt); cumulative sum = Brownian paths
incr = rng.normal(0, np.sqrt(dt), size=(n_paths, n_steps))
W = np.concatenate([np.zeros((n_paths, 1)), np.cumsum(incr, axis=1)], axis=1)

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7.8, 4.4))
for i in range(n_paths):
    ax.plot(t, W[i], color="#b7b6a8", lw=0.7, alpha=0.8)
# +/- sqrt(t) and +/- 2 sqrt(t) envelopes
ax.plot(t, np.sqrt(t), color="#e8531d", lw=2.2, label=r"$\pm\sqrt{t}$  (1 sd)")
ax.plot(t, -np.sqrt(t), color="#e8531d", lw=2.2)
ax.plot(t, 2*np.sqrt(t), color="#161613", lw=1.8, ls=(0, (5, 3)), label=r"$\pm 2\sqrt{t}$  (2 sd)")
ax.plot(t, -2*np.sqrt(t), color="#161613", lw=1.8, ls=(0, (5, 3)))
ax.axhline(0, color="#5e5e54", lw=0.8)
ax.set_xlim(0, T)
ax.set_xlabel("time  t"); ax.set_ylabel("W(t)")
ax.set_title(r"Brownian paths spread like $\sqrt{t}$  (variance grows linearly)")
ax.legend(fontsize=9, frameon=False, loc="upper left")
fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()

Hitting levels and the maximum

Let T_a be the first time the path reaches level a>0. Ross’s argument (Ross 2014): if the path has reached a by time t, then from that moment it is equally likely to be above or below a at time t. So \Pr\{T_a\le t\} = 2\,\Pr\{W(t)\ge a\}, \qquad \Pr\Big\{\max_{0\le s\le t} W(s)\ge a\Big\} = 2\,\Pr\{W(t)\ge a\}.

In plain words: the chance of having ever reached a level is twice the chance of being above it now. Step 3 of the race is this with a=1, t=1: 2\times0.16=0.32.

For the chance of hitting A before -B (A,B>0), Ross takes the gambler’s-ruin result for the random walk and lets the step size shrink: (Ross 2014) \Pr\{\text{up } A \text{ before down } B\} = \frac{B}{A+B}.

In plain words: the nearer barrier is the likelier to be hit first, in exact proportion to the distances.

Continuous but nowhere differentiable

Brownian motion’s paths are continuous, yet at no point do they have a derivative (a well-defined slope). Zoom in and the wiggle never smooths out. A heuristic shows why: over a tiny interval h the increment is of size about \sqrt{h}, so the slope \sqrt{h}/h = 1/\sqrt{h} blows up as h shrinks. “Velocity” is undefined everywhere. This is why stochastic calculus (Itô’s calculus) exists: you cannot differentiate a Brownian path, so integration against it needs its own rules.

Brownian motion with drift, and geometric Brownian motion

Adding a steady trend gives Brownian motion with drift: X(t) is normal with mean \mu t and variance \sigma^2 t (Ross 2014). Exponentiating it gives geometric Brownian motion, X(t)=e^{Y(t)}, which Ross recommends for stock prices “when one feels that the percentage changes are independent and identically distributed” (Ross 2014). The reasoning is short: if each period’s price ratio X_n/X_{n-1} is i.i.d., then the log price is a sum of i.i.d. terms, so by the CLT it is approximately a Brownian motion with drift, and the price itself is approximately geometric Brownian motion (Ross 2014). This model is the basis of the Black–Scholes option-pricing formula (Ross 2014).

In plain words: prices multiply rather than add, so it is the log of the price that wanders like Brownian motion. That keeps the price positive and makes the percentage shocks, not the shocks in money terms, the ones with a fixed distribution.

Where it goes

  • Diffusion. Brownian motion is the mathematical model of a particle buffeted by countless molecular collisions (its physical origin, explained by Einstein in 1905 and formalised by Wiener from 1918), and of any quantity driven by many small independent shocks (Ross 2014).
  • Finance. Geometric Brownian motion is the standard model for a stock price, and the foundation of the Black–Scholes option-pricing theory (Ross 2014).
  • The CLT, continuously. Partial-sum processes of i.i.d. shocks converge to Brownian motion (Donsker’s “functional CLT”), which is why it appears as the limit in so many places: it is the CLT’s process-level twin.

Common pitfalls

WarningWhere Brownian intuition slips

  • Expecting a smooth trend. A Brownian path has no drift and no velocity; apparent “trends” over a finite window are noise, and the process is equally likely to reverse. Do not read momentum into \sqrt{t} spreading.

  • Confusing spread with drift. \operatorname{Var}(W(t))=t grows without bound, but \mathrm{E}[W(t)]=0 always: the cloud widens around zero; it does not march off in a direction (that requires added drift). (Ross 2014)

  • Scaling uncertainty linearly. The standard deviation over a horizon of t is \sigma\sqrt t, not \sigma t. Four times the horizon means twice the uncertainty.

  • Treating paths as differentiable. They are continuous but nowhere differentiable; ordinary calculus does not apply, which is the whole reason for Itô calculus.

  • Modelling prices as Brownian, not geometric Brownian. Plain Brownian motion can go negative and has additive (not multiplicative) shocks; asset prices use geometric Brownian motion so that log-returns, not levels, are Gaussian. (Ross 2014)

NoteWho says what

Connections

  • Is the process form of: the Central Limit Theorem (rescaled sums of i.i.d. shocks): the same \sqrt{n} scaling, traced through time.
  • Grows from: the random walk and, alongside the Poisson process, is one of the two canonical continuous-time processes (one continuous, one jump).
  • Echoes in: the random-walk and unit-root models of time series, the discrete-time cousins of Brownian motion, where shocks also accumulate without fading.
  • Underlies: diffusion models and continuous-time finance (Black–Scholes).

fast the walker never leaves the origin; too slowly and it flies off to infinity. Shrink it in proportion to
the *square root* of the time between steps and the picture settles down to a continuous, endlessly jagged
path. By the CLT, the position at any time is then normal. That limit is Brownian motion.

Three properties come along for free:

1. **Fresh shocks.** What the walk does next is independent of what it did before (independent increments).
2. **Same rules all the time.** A stretch of length $t$ behaves the same whenever it starts (stationary
   increments).
3. **Normal, with variance growing like time.** The displacement over a stretch of length $t$ is normal with
   mean 0 and variance proportional to $t$.

## A bicycle race, by hand {#sec-by-hand}

Ross's Example 10.1 [@ross_prob_models]: two cyclists race, and $Y(t)$ is the number of seconds by which the
inside rider leads when a fraction $t$ of the race is done. Model $Y$ as a Brownian motion with variance
parameter $\sigma^2$. To put numbers on it, take $\sigma=2$ seconds: over the whole race the lead wanders with
a standard deviation of 2 seconds.

**Step 1 — leading at halfway.** The inside rider leads by 1 second at the midpoint. What happens in the
second half is independent of the first, and it is normal with mean 0 and variance $\sigma^2\times\tfrac12=2$
(standard deviation $\approx1.41$). She wins if the second half does not cost her more than 1 second:
$$
\Pr\{\text{win}\} = \Pr\{N(0,2) > -1\} = \Phi\!\left(\tfrac{1}{1.41}\right) = \Phi(0.71)\approx 0.76 .
$$

**Step 2 — looking back from the finish.** Now suppose she *won* by 1 second. Was she ahead at halfway? Given
the end point, the midpoint is normal with mean $\tfrac12$ of the final lead (0.5 s) and variance
$\sigma^2/4=1$ [@ross_prob_models]. So
$$
\Pr\{\text{ahead at halfway}\mid\text{won by 1 s}\}=\Pr\{N(0.5,1)>0\}=\Phi(0.5)\approx0.69 .
$$
Knowing the end point pulls the middle towards a straight line from start to finish.

**Step 3 — ever ahead by 2 seconds?** For standard Brownian motion over one unit of time, the chance of
*ending* above 1 is $1-\Phi(1)\approx0.16$. The chance of *ever touching* 1 along the way is exactly twice that,
about 0.32 [@ross_prob_models]. (Any path that touched 1 is equally likely to finish above or below it.) In the
race, with $\sigma=2$, that is the chance the inside rider is ever 2 seconds ahead.

**Step 4 — which comes first?** Starting at 0, the chance of rising by $A$ before falling by $B$ is
$B/(A+B)$ [@ross_prob_models]. Up 1 before down 2: $2/3$. This is the gambler's-ruin answer from the random
walk, carried over to the limit.

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Notation key — open if a symbol is unfamiliar"}
- $t$ — time; $W(t)$ (Ross writes $X(t)$ or $B(t)$) — the position of standard Brownian motion at time $t$.
- $\sigma^2$ — the variance parameter: variance accumulated per unit of time. *Standard* means $\sigma=1$.
- $N(\mu,\sigma^2)$ — a normal distribution with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$.
- $\Phi(x)$ — the standard normal CDF, $\Pr\{N(0,1)\le x\}$. $\Phi(1)\approx0.84$, $\Phi(2)\approx0.98$.
- *Increment* — the change over a stretch of time, $W(t)-W(s)$.
- $\xi_i$ — the $i$-th coin-flip step, $+1$ or $-1$; $S_n$ — the walk's position after $n$ steps.
- $T_a$ — the first time the path reaches level $a$ (a *hitting time*).
:::

## From random walk to a continuous path {#sec-limit}

Let $S_n = \xi_1 + \dots + \xi_n$ be a symmetric random walk (each $\xi_i = \pm 1$). Rescale space by
$1/\sqrt{n}$ and time by $1/n$; as $n\to\infty$ the rescaled walk converges to a limiting process $W(t)$.
[@ross_prob_models] The $\sqrt{n}$ space-scaling is the same $\sqrt{n}$ that appears in the CLT, and for the
same reason: a sum of $n$ independent steps has standard deviation $\propto\sqrt{n}$, so to keep the limit finite
you must shrink space by exactly that factor. Brownian motion is what that limit *is*. Ross makes the point by
contrast: shrink the step size in proportion to the time step instead, and the mean and variance both go to
zero, so the limit is a walker that never moves [@ross_prob_models].

> **In plain words:** the step length must shrink like the square root of the time step. Any faster and
> nothing happens; any slower and everything blows up.

## The defining properties {#sec-properties}

Standard Brownian motion $W(t)$ is characterised by four properties: [@ross_prob_models]

- $W(0) = 0$;
- **independent increments** — $W(t)-W(s)$ is independent of the past up to $s$;
- **stationary Gaussian increments** — $W(t)-W(s)\sim\mathrm{N}(0,\,t-s)$, depending only on the elapsed time;
- **continuous paths** — $t\mapsto W(t)$ is continuous.

From these, $\mathrm{E}[W(t)]=0$ and $\operatorname{Var}(W(t))=t$: the variance grows **linearly in time**, so
the typical displacement grows like $\sqrt{t}$.

> **In plain words:** the cloud of possible paths stays centred on zero but widens like the square root of
> time. Step 1 of the race used exactly this: half the race, half the variance.

That single scaling, spread $\propto\sqrt{t}$, is the signature of diffusion, and the figure below is built
around it. Ross notes that continuity of the paths is a deep fact, proved with probability one, though he does
not prove it [@ross_prob_models].

```{python}
#| label: fig-brownian
#| fig-cap: "Brownian motion spreads like √t. Forty independent sample paths of standard Brownian motion, each a continuous random trajectory with W(0)=0 and independent Gaussian increments. The envelopes are ±√t (orange) and ±2√t (dashed ink), the 1- and 2-standard-deviation bands, since W(t) ~ N(0, t): at any moment about two-thirds of paths sit inside the inner band and about 95% inside the outer. The cloud widens as √t, not linearly: the same √n scaling as the Central Limit Theorem, now traced through time."
#| code-fold: true
#| code-summary: "Show the figure code"

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(2)
n_paths, n_steps, T = 40, 500, 1.0
dt = T / n_steps
t = np.linspace(0, T, n_steps + 1)

# increments ~ N(0, dt); cumulative sum = Brownian paths
incr = rng.normal(0, np.sqrt(dt), size=(n_paths, n_steps))
W = np.concatenate([np.zeros((n_paths, 1)), np.cumsum(incr, axis=1)], axis=1)

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7.8, 4.4))
for i in range(n_paths):
    ax.plot(t, W[i], color="#b7b6a8", lw=0.7, alpha=0.8)
# +/- sqrt(t) and +/- 2 sqrt(t) envelopes
ax.plot(t, np.sqrt(t), color="#e8531d", lw=2.2, label=r"$\pm\sqrt{t}$  (1 sd)")
ax.plot(t, -np.sqrt(t), color="#e8531d", lw=2.2)
ax.plot(t, 2*np.sqrt(t), color="#161613", lw=1.8, ls=(0, (5, 3)), label=r"$\pm 2\sqrt{t}$  (2 sd)")
ax.plot(t, -2*np.sqrt(t), color="#161613", lw=1.8, ls=(0, (5, 3)))
ax.axhline(0, color="#5e5e54", lw=0.8)
ax.set_xlim(0, T)
ax.set_xlabel("time  t"); ax.set_ylabel("W(t)")
ax.set_title(r"Brownian paths spread like $\sqrt{t}$  (variance grows linearly)")
ax.legend(fontsize=9, frameon=False, loc="upper left")
fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

## Hitting levels and the maximum {#sec-hitting}

Let $T_a$ be the first time the path reaches level $a>0$. Ross's argument [@ross_prob_models]: if the path has
reached $a$ by time $t$, then from that moment it is equally likely to be above or below $a$ at time $t$. So
$$
\Pr\{T_a\le t\} = 2\,\Pr\{W(t)\ge a\}, \qquad
\Pr\Big\{\max_{0\le s\le t} W(s)\ge a\Big\} = 2\,\Pr\{W(t)\ge a\}.
$$

> **In plain words:** the chance of having *ever* reached a level is twice the chance of being above it *now*.
> Step 3 of the race is this with $a=1$, $t=1$: $2\times0.16=0.32$.

For the chance of hitting $A$ before $-B$ ($A,B>0$), Ross takes the gambler's-ruin result for the random walk
and lets the step size shrink: [@ross_prob_models]
$$
\Pr\{\text{up } A \text{ before down } B\} = \frac{B}{A+B}.
$$

> **In plain words:** the nearer barrier is the likelier to be hit first, in exact proportion to the distances.

## Continuous but nowhere differentiable

Brownian motion's paths are continuous, yet at no point do they have a derivative (a well-defined slope). Zoom
in and the wiggle never smooths out. A heuristic shows why: over a tiny interval $h$ the increment is of size
about $\sqrt{h}$, so the slope $\sqrt{h}/h = 1/\sqrt{h}$ blows up as $h$ shrinks. "Velocity" is undefined
everywhere. This is why stochastic calculus (Itô's calculus) exists: you cannot differentiate a Brownian path,
so integration against it needs its own rules.

## Brownian motion with drift, and geometric Brownian motion {#sec-geometric}

Adding a steady trend gives **Brownian motion with drift**: $X(t)$ is normal with mean $\mu t$ and variance
$\sigma^2 t$ [@ross_prob_models]. Exponentiating it gives **geometric Brownian motion**, $X(t)=e^{Y(t)}$, which
Ross recommends for stock prices "when one feels that the percentage changes are independent and identically
distributed" [@ross_prob_models]. The reasoning is short: if each period's price ratio $X_n/X_{n-1}$ is i.i.d.,
then the log price is a sum of i.i.d. terms, so by the CLT it is approximately a Brownian motion with drift, and
the price itself is approximately geometric Brownian motion [@ross_prob_models]. This model is the basis of
the Black–Scholes option-pricing formula [@ross_prob_models].

> **In plain words:** prices multiply rather than add, so it is the *log* of the price that wanders like
> Brownian motion. That keeps the price positive and makes the percentage shocks, not the shocks in money
> terms, the ones with a fixed distribution.

## Where it goes

- **Diffusion.** Brownian motion is the mathematical model of a particle buffeted by countless molecular
  collisions (its physical origin, explained by Einstein in 1905 and formalised by Wiener from 1918), and of
  any quantity driven by many small independent shocks [@ross_prob_models].
- **Finance.** **Geometric** Brownian motion is the standard model for a stock price, and the foundation of
  the Black–Scholes option-pricing theory [@ross_prob_models].
- **The CLT, continuously.** Partial-sum processes of i.i.d. shocks converge to Brownian motion (Donsker's
  "functional CLT"), which is why it appears as the limit in so many places: it is the CLT's process-level
  twin.

## Common pitfalls

::: {.callout-warning title="Where Brownian intuition slips"}

- **Expecting a smooth trend.** A Brownian path has no drift and no velocity; apparent "trends" over a finite
  window are noise, and the process is equally likely to reverse. Do not read momentum into $\sqrt{t}$ spreading.

- **Confusing spread with drift.** $\operatorname{Var}(W(t))=t$ grows without bound, but $\mathrm{E}[W(t)]=0$
  always: the cloud widens around zero; it does not march off in a direction (that requires added drift).
  [@ross_prob_models]

- **Scaling uncertainty linearly.** The standard deviation over a horizon of $t$ is $\sigma\sqrt t$, not
  $\sigma t$. Four times the horizon means twice the uncertainty.

- **Treating paths as differentiable.** They are continuous but nowhere differentiable; ordinary calculus does
  not apply, which is the whole reason for Itô calculus.

- **Modelling prices as Brownian, not geometric Brownian.** Plain Brownian motion can go negative and has
  additive (not multiplicative) shocks; asset prices use *geometric* Brownian motion so that log-returns, not
  levels, are Gaussian. [@ross_prob_models]

:::
