Branching Processes

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source

One ancestor, a random number of offspring each generation, repeated. Whether the line survives or dies out turns on a single number — the mean number of offspring — and the exact chance of extinction is the fixed point of one generating function.

Start with one individual who has a random number of offspring; each offspring independently does the same; repeat. This Galton–Watson branching process is the simplest model of anything that reproduces itself — family names, infections, rumours, neutrons. Its whole fate hinges on the mean number of offspring m: if m \le 1 the line is certain to die out, if m > 1 it has a positive chance of exploding forever. And the exact extinction probability is the smallest solution of h(z)=z, where h is the offspring generating function — one of the cleanest pay-offs in all of applied probability.

Why it matters

The branching process is the probability behind every “does it fizzle or does it take off?” question a social scientist meets. In an epidemic the offspring are the people one infected person infects, and the mean m is exactly the basic reproduction number R_0: the sharp threshold at m=1 is why “keep R_0 below one” is the entire logic of containment. In the diffusion of a rumour, a product, or a protest, the offspring are the people each adopter converts, and the same threshold decides whether an idea dies in a handful of shares or cascades across a population. Galton and Watson invented the model in 1873 to ask whether aristocratic surnames were doomed to extinction. (Grinstead and Snell 1997) The same mathematics is the microscopic engine under the giant-component transition in random graphs and the cascades that sweep through networks.

The model: count the next generation

Fix an offspring distribution \{p_k\}: p_k is the probability that any given individual has exactly k children, independently of everyone else. Start with a single progenitor, Z_0 = 1. Generation n+1 is the pooled offspring of the Z_n individuals alive in generation n: Z_{n+1} = \sum_{i=1}^{Z_n} X_i, where each X_i is an independent draw from \{p_k\}. (Ross 2014) The population Z_n is a Markov chain on \{0,1,2,\dots\} whose only absorbing state is 0: once a generation is empty, the line is gone forever. Everything reduces to a race between that absorbing 0 and unbounded growth.

The mean tells you the drift

Let m = \mathbb E[X] = \sum_k k\,p_k be the mean number of offspring per individual. Because generations compound multiplicatively, the expected population size is just m raised to the generation number: (Ross 2014) \mathbb E[Z_n] = m^{\,n}. That one line already splits the world into three regimes, named for how they sit against the knife-edge m=1:

  • Subcritical (m < 1): expected size decays geometrically to zero — the line withers.
  • Critical (m = 1): expected size stays exactly 1 forever — perfectly balanced on average.
  • Supercritical (m > 1): expected size explodes geometrically — room to survive.

But the expectation is only the drift, not the fate of any single family. A supercritical process can still go extinct by bad luck in the first few generations; a critical one, astonishingly, dies out with certainty even though its expected size never shrinks. To get the actual chance of extinction you need the generating function, not just the mean.

Extinction is the fixed point of the generating function

Encode the offspring distribution in its probability generating function h(z) = \sum_k p_k z^k = \mathbb E[z^X] — the integer-valued sibling of the moment generating function. Let q be the extinction probability, the chance that the population eventually hits zero. The self-similar structure of the process pins q down exactly: condition on the progenitor’s own number of children. If the founder has k children, each child independently launches a fresh, identical copy of the whole process, and the line dies out only if all k of those independent sub-lines die out — probability q^k. Averaging over k: (Ross 2014; Grinstead and Snell 1997) q = \sum_{k} p_k\, q^{k} = h(q). The extinction probability is a fixed point of the offspring generating function — specifically, the smallest non-negative root of h(z)=z. Since h is increasing and convex on [0,1] with h(1)=1, the geometry is decisive (see the figure): its slope at z=1 is exactly h'(1)=m, so

  • if m \le 1, the curve h stays above the diagonal until they meet only at z=1, forcing q=1 — certain extinction;
  • if m > 1, the convex curve dips below the diagonal and crosses it at some q < 1 before reaching 1 — a genuine chance 1-q of surviving forever.

This is the sharp m=1 threshold made exact. The critical case m=1 lands in the first branch: it goes extinct with probability one, the model’s most famous counter-intuitive fact — expected size constant, yet survival impossible.

Show the figure code 
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

z = np.linspace(0.0, 1.0, 400)

def pgf(m, z):                    # Poisson(m) offspring: h(z) = exp(m(z-1))
    return np.exp(m * (z - 1.0))

def extinction_prob(m):           # smallest fixed point of h(z)=z, by iterating from 0
    q = 0.0
    for _ in range(500):
        q = np.exp(m * (q - 1.0))
    return q

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(9.4, 4.4), sharey=True)
for ax, m, label in zip(axes, [0.8, 1.6],
                        ["subcritical   m = 0.8", "supercritical   m = 1.6"]):
    ax.plot(z, z, color="#161613", lw=1.4)                  # diagonal y = z
    ax.plot(z, pgf(m, z), color="#5e5e54", lw=2.0)          # offspring PGF h(z)
    q = extinction_prob(m)
    ax.vlines(q, 0, q, color="#e8531d", lw=1.0, ls=(0, (3, 3)))
    ax.plot([q], [q], "o", color="#e8531d", ms=9, zorder=5)  # the extinction probability
    ax.text(q, -0.05, f"q = {q:.2f}", color="#e8531d", ha="center", va="top", fontsize=9)
    ax.set_title(label, fontsize=10)
    ax.set_xlim(0, 1); ax.set_ylim(0, 1); ax.set_aspect("equal")
    ax.set_xlabel("z")
axes[0].set_ylabel("h(z)  and  y = z")
fig.suptitle("Extinction probability is the smallest solution of h(z) = z", fontsize=11)
fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()

Common pitfalls

WarningWhere branching intuition misleads

  • Reading the mean as the whole story. \mathbb E[Z_n]=m^n gives the drift, not the destiny. A supercritical process (m>1) still goes extinct with probability q>0; the expected size exploding does not mean your line survives. (Ross 2014)

  • Missing the critical catastrophe. At m=1 the expected size is constant at 1 forever, yet extinction is certain (whenever there is any variability in offspring). Balanced on average is not the same as safe. (Grinstead and Snell 1997)

  • Grabbing the wrong root. h(z)=z always has z=1 as a solution; the extinction probability is the smallest non-negative root, which is <1 only in the supercritical case. Solve by iterating q \leftarrow h(q) from 0 and it converges to the correct one.

  • Assuming independence you don’t have. The clean q=h(q) needs offspring counts to be independent and identically distributed across individuals and generations. Finite populations, saturation, or contagion that depletes the susceptible pool break the model — real epidemics bend below the branching prediction as they run out of people to infect.

From the sources

NoteWho says what
  • Grinstead & Snell, Introduction to Probability (Ch 10, Generating Functions) — the Galton–Watson surname-extinction problem as the historical motivation; the offspring generating function h(z)=\sum p_k z^k; extinction probability as the smallest fixed point of h(z)=z; and the threshold result that extinction is certain iff the mean offspring number is \le 1.
  • Ross, Introduction to Probability Models (Branching Processes) — the process Z_{n+1}=\sum_{i=1}^{Z_n} X_i as a Markov chain with absorbing state 0; \mathbb E[Z_n]=m^n; and the derivation of the extinction equation q=\sum_k p_k q^k by conditioning on the first generation.

Appendix · the mathematics

Expected size. Condition on Z_n and use that each of its members has mean m offspring: \mathbb E[Z_{n+1}\mid Z_n] = m\,Z_n, so \mathbb E[Z_{n+1}] = m\,\mathbb E[Z_n]. With Z_0=1 this iterates to \mathbb E[Z_n]=m^n. (Ross 2014)

The extinction equation. Let h(z)=\mathbb E[z^{X}] be the offspring PGF and h_n(z)=\mathbb E[z^{Z_n}] the PGF of the n-th generation. Because generation n+1 is a sum of Z_n independent offspring counts, the generating functions compose: h_{n+1}(z)=h_n(h(z))=h(h(z)) iterated — the PGF of the whole line is h applied to itself n times. The extinction probability is q=\lim_n \mathbb P(Z_n=0)=\lim_n h_n(0), and taking the limit in h_{n+1}(0)=h(h_n(0)) gives the fixed-point equation q = h(q), \qquad q = \text{smallest root in } [0,1]. (Grinstead and Snell 1997)

Why the smallest root, and the m=1 knife-edge. On [0,1], h is continuous, non-decreasing, and convex (its second derivative \sum_k k(k-1)p_k z^{k-2}\ge 0), with h(0)=p_0\ge 0 and h(1)=1. A convex curve meets the straight line y=z in at most two points on [0,1]. The slope at the top end is h'(1)=\sum_k k\,p_k = m. If m\le 1 the curve approaches z=1 from above and the only intersection is z=1, so q=1. If m>1 the curve crosses the diagonal from above at a unique interior point q<1; that smaller root is the extinction probability, and the iteration q_{k+1}=h(q_k) started at q_0=0 increases to it. (The critical case m=1 falls in the first branch, hence certain extinction unless every individual has exactly one child, p_1=1.) (Ross 2014; Grinstead and Snell 1997)

Connections

  • Rests on: generating functions (the offspring PGF h(z)=\mathbb E[z^X] whose fixed point is the answer) and Markov chains (the population size is a chain absorbed at 0).
  • Twin of: the giant-component transition in Erdős–Rényi graphs — the local branching approximation is why a giant component appears exactly when the mean degree passes 1 — and the cascades and spreading processes that use the same fizzle-or-explode threshold.
  • Applies to: epidemic modelling (m=R_0 and the containment threshold), the diffusion of behaviour and information, and any self-reproducing count.

$$

## Common pitfalls

::: {.callout-warning title="Where branching intuition misleads"}

- **Reading the mean as the whole story.** $\mathbb E[Z_n]=m^n$ gives the drift, not the destiny. A
  supercritical process ($m>1$) still goes extinct with probability $q>0$; the *expected* size exploding does
  not mean *your* line survives. [@ross_prob_models]

  is **certain** (whenever there is any variability in offspring). Balanced on average is not the same as
  safe. [@grinstead_snell]

- **Grabbing the wrong root.** $h(z)=z$ always has $z=1$ as a solution; the extinction probability is the
  **smallest** non-negative root, which is $<1$ only in the supercritical case. Solve by iterating
  $q \leftarrow h(q)$ from $0$ and it converges to the correct one.

- **Assuming independence you don't have.** The clean $q=h(q)$ needs offspring counts to be **independent and
  identically distributed** across individuals and generations. Finite populations, saturation, or contagion
  that depletes the susceptible pool break the model — real epidemics bend below the branching prediction as
  they run out of people to infect.

:::

## From the sources {.unnumbered}

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Who says what"}
- **Grinstead & Snell, *Introduction to Probability* (Ch 10, Generating Functions)** — the Galton–Watson
  **surname-extinction** problem as the historical motivation; the offspring **generating function**
  $h(z)=\sum p_k z^k$; extinction probability as the **smallest fixed point of $h(z)=z$**; and the
  threshold result that extinction is certain iff the mean offspring number is $\le 1$.
- **Ross, *Introduction to Probability Models* (Branching Processes)** — the process $Z_{n+1}=\sum_{i=1}^{Z_n}
  X_i$ as a Markov chain with absorbing state $0$; $\mathbb E[Z_n]=m^n$; and the derivation of the extinction
  equation $q=\sum_k p_k q^k$ by conditioning on the first generation.
:::

## Appendix · the mathematics {.unnumbered}

::: {.math-appendix}

**Expected size.** Condition on $Z_n$ and use that each of its members has mean $m$ offspring:
$\mathbb E[Z_{n+1}\mid Z_n] = m\,Z_n$, so $\mathbb E[Z_{n+1}] = m\,\mathbb E[Z_n]$. With $Z_0=1$ this iterates
to $\mathbb E[Z_n]=m^n$. [@ross_prob_models]

**The extinction equation.** Let $h(z)=\mathbb E[z^{X}]$ be the offspring PGF and $h_n(z)=\mathbb E[z^{Z_n}]$
the PGF of the $n$-th generation. Because generation $n+1$ is a sum of $Z_n$ independent offspring counts, the
generating functions **compose**: $h_{n+1}(z)=h_n(h(z))=h(h(z))$ iterated — the PGF of the whole line is $h$
applied to itself $n$ times. The extinction probability is $q=\lim_n \mathbb P(Z_n=0)=\lim_n h_n(0)$, and
taking the limit in $h_{n+1}(0)=h(h_n(0))$ gives the fixed-point equation
$$
q = h(q), \qquad q = \text{smallest root in } [0,1].
$$
[@grinstead_snell]

**Why the smallest root, and the $m=1$ knife-edge.** On $[0,1]$, $h$ is continuous, non-decreasing, and
**convex** (its second derivative $\sum_k k(k-1)p_k z^{k-2}\ge 0$), with $h(0)=p_0\ge 0$ and $h(1)=1$. A
convex curve meets the straight line $y=z$ in at most two points on $[0,1]$. The slope at the top end is
$h'(1)=\sum_k k\,p_k = m$. If $m\le 1$ the curve approaches $z=1$ from above and the only intersection is
$z=1$, so $q=1$. If $m>1$ the curve crosses the diagonal from above at a unique interior point $q<1$; that
smaller root is the extinction probability, and the iteration $q_{k+1}=h(q_k)$ started at $q_0=0$ increases to
it. (The critical case $m=1$ falls in the first branch, hence certain extinction unless every individual has
exactly one child, $p_1=1$.) [@ross_prob_models; @grinstead_snell]

:::
