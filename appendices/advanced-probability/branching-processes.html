Branching Processes

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source

One ancestor, a random number of children each generation, repeated. Whether the family line survives or dies out turns on a single number, the average number of children, and the exact chance of dying out can be found by repeating one simple calculation.

One person has a random number of children, each child does the same, and so on down the generations. This is the simplest model of anything that copies itself: family names, infections, rumours, chain reactions. Its fate hinges on the average number of children per person. At one or below, the line is certain to die out eventually. Above one, it has a real chance of growing forever, but it can still die out by bad luck, and that chance can be computed exactly.

TipWhere you probably came from — and what you need

You likely arrived from generating functions (“what are they actually good for?”), the giant-component threshold in Erdős–Rényi random graphs, spreading on networks (“why is R_0=1 the epidemic threshold?”), or the Markov chains entry (the population is a chain that can be absorbed at zero).

You need: what a probability distribution is, the expected value (a long-run average), and the rule that independent events multiply. You don’t need calculus for the main ideas: the worked example is arithmetic you can do on a calculator, repeated.

Jump to what you came for: the idea in words · a family line by hand · the model · the mean and its three regimes · the extinction probability

Why it matters

The branching process is the probability behind every “does it fizzle or does it take off?” question a social scientist meets. In an epidemic the offspring are the people one infected person infects, and the mean m is exactly the basic reproduction number R_0: the sharp threshold at m=1 is why “keep R_0 below one” is the entire logic of containment. In the diffusion of a rumour, a product, or a protest, the offspring are the people each adopter converts, and the same threshold decides whether an idea dies in a handful of shares or cascades across a population. Galton and Watson invented the model in 1873 to ask whether aristocratic surnames were doomed to extinction. (Grinstead and Snell 1997) The same mathematics is the microscopic engine under the giant-component transition in random graphs and the cascades that sweep through networks.

The idea in words

Think of a family tree drawn downwards from one founder. Each person, independently of everyone else, rolls the same die to decide how many children to have. The tree either stops (a whole generation has no children) or keeps going forever.

Two ideas carry the whole entry:

  1. The average multiplies. If each person has m children on average, each generation is on average m times the last. Above one the average explodes; below one it shrinks to nothing.
  2. The line dies only if every branch dies. The founder’s children each start an independent copy of the same tree. So “the whole line dies out” means “every one of those sub-trees dies out”, and independent chances multiply. That self-similarity gives a single equation for the chance of extinction.

The surprise is that the average and the fate can disagree. A line whose average size grows every generation can still be more likely than not to vanish.

A family line, by hand

Grinstead and Snell’s running example (Grinstead and Snell 1997): each person has 0 children with probability 0.2, 1 child with probability 0.5, and 2 children with probability 0.3.

Step 1 — the average. The mean number of children is 0\times0.2+1\times0.5+2\times0.3=1.1, just above one. On average the line grows by 10% a generation: 1.1^{12}\approx3.1 people expected in generation 12.

Step 2 — dying out by generation 1. The founder has no children with probability 0.2.

Step 3 — dying out by generation 2. Condition on the founder’s children. With 0 children the line is already dead. With 1 child, that child’s line must die within one generation (chance 0.2). With 2 children, both must (chance 0.2^2). So 0.2 + 0.5\times0.2 + 0.3\times0.2^2 = 0.312 .

Step 4 — repeat. Each generation’s answer comes from the last one by the same recipe: take the previous value d and compute 0.2+0.5d+0.3d^2.

by generation 1 2 3 4 6 8 10 12
chance the line has died out 0.200 0.312 0.385 0.437 0.506 0.549 0.578 0.599

These match Grinstead and Snell’s Table 10.1 (Grinstead and Snell 1997).

Step 5 — the limit. The numbers creep up towards a value d that the recipe leaves unchanged: d = 0.2+0.5d+0.3d^2. This quadratic has two roots, d=1 and d=0.2/0.3=\tfrac23. The chance of eventual extinction is the smaller one, 2/3, even though the expected size keeps growing (Grinstead and Snell 1997). Twelve generations (0.599) are still well short of it.

A real-data version: Keyfitz’s estimates of the number of daughters born to Japanese women in 1960 give a mean of about 1.84 daughters, and an extinction probability for a female line of only about 0.32 (Grinstead and Snell 1997).

NoteNotation key — open if a symbol is unfamiliar
  • p_k — the probability that one person has exactly k children (the offspring distribution).
  • X, X_i — the number of children of one person, a random draw from \{p_k\}.
  • Z_n — the number of people in generation n; Z_0=1 is the founder.
  • m=\mathbb E[X] — the mean number of children (1.1 above). \mathbb E[\cdot] is the expected value.
  • h(z)=\sum_k p_k z^k — the probability generating function: a polynomial whose coefficients are the p_k. Above, h(z)=0.2+0.5z+0.3z^2. Step 4’s recipe is h.
  • q (Grinstead and Snell write d) — the probability that the line eventually dies out.
  • Fixed point — a value the function leaves unchanged, h(q)=q.
  • Absorbing state — a state a Markov chain can enter but never leave (here, zero people).

The model: count the next generation

Fix an offspring distribution \{p_k\}: p_k is the probability that any given individual has exactly k children, independently of everyone else. Start with a single progenitor, Z_0 = 1. Generation n+1 is the pooled offspring of the Z_n individuals alive in generation n: Z_{n+1} = \sum_{i=1}^{Z_n} X_i, where each X_i is an independent draw from \{p_k\}. (Ross 2014)

In plain words: next generation’s size is the total number of children of everyone alive now.

The population Z_n is a Markov chain on \{0,1,2,\dots\} whose only absorbing state is 0: once a generation is empty, the line is gone forever. Everything reduces to a race between that absorbing 0 and unbounded growth.

The mean tells you the drift

Let m = \mathbb E[X] = \sum_k k\,p_k be the mean number of offspring per individual. Because generations compound multiplicatively, the expected population size is just m raised to the generation number: (Ross 2014; Grinstead and Snell 1997) \mathbb E[Z_n] = m^{\,n}.

In plain words: on average, each generation is m times the size of the one before. With m=1.1, twelve generations multiply the founder by about 3.1.

That one line already splits the world into three regimes, named for how they sit against the knife-edge m=1:

  • Subcritical (m < 1): expected size decays geometrically to zero — the line withers.
  • Critical (m = 1): expected size stays exactly 1 forever — perfectly balanced on average.
  • Supercritical (m > 1): expected size explodes geometrically — room to survive.

But the expectation is only the drift, not the fate of any single family. A supercritical process can still go extinct by bad luck in the first few generations; a critical one, astonishingly, dies out with certainty even though its expected size never shrinks. To get the actual chance of extinction you need the generating function, not just the mean. The family line above is the example: mean 1.1, yet a 2/3 chance of dying out.

Extinction is the fixed point of the generating function

Encode the offspring distribution in its probability generating function h(z) = \sum_k p_k z^k = \mathbb E[z^X] — the integer-valued sibling of the moment generating function. Let q be the extinction probability, the chance that the population eventually hits zero. The self-similar structure of the process pins q down exactly: condition on the progenitor’s own number of children. If the founder has k children, each child independently launches a fresh, identical copy of the whole process, and the line dies out only if all k of those independent sub-lines die out — probability q^k. Averaging over k: (Ross 2014; Grinstead and Snell 1997) q = \sum_{k} p_k\, q^{k} = h(q).

In plain words: the chance of dying out equals the average, over the founder’s number of children, of the chance that all those children’s lines die out. Step 5 solved exactly this equation, with h(q)=0.2+0.5q+0.3q^2.

The extinction probability is a fixed point of the offspring generating function — specifically, the smallest non-negative root of h(z)=z. Since h is increasing and convex (curving upwards, like a bowl) on [0,1] with h(1)=1, the geometry is decisive (see the figure): its slope at z=1 is exactly h'(1)=m, so

  • if m \le 1, the curve h stays above the diagonal until they meet only at z=1, forcing q=1 — certain extinction;
  • if m > 1, the convex curve dips below the diagonal and crosses it at some q < 1 before reaching 1 — a genuine chance 1-q of surviving forever.

This is the sharp m=1 threshold made exact. The critical case m=1 lands in the first branch: it goes extinct with probability one, the model’s most famous counter-intuitive fact — expected size constant, yet survival impossible.

Show the figure code 
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

z = np.linspace(0.0, 1.0, 400)

def pgf(m, z):                    # Poisson(m) offspring: h(z) = exp(m(z-1))
    return np.exp(m * (z - 1.0))

def extinction_prob(m):           # smallest fixed point of h(z)=z, by iterating from 0
    q = 0.0
    for _ in range(500):
        q = np.exp(m * (q - 1.0))
    return q

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(9.4, 4.4), sharey=True)
for ax, m, label in zip(axes, [0.8, 1.6],
                        ["subcritical   m = 0.8", "supercritical   m = 1.6"]):
    ax.plot(z, z, color="#161613", lw=1.4)                  # diagonal y = z
    ax.plot(z, pgf(m, z), color="#5e5e54", lw=2.0)          # offspring PGF h(z)
    q = extinction_prob(m)
    ax.vlines(q, 0, q, color="#e8531d", lw=1.0, ls=(0, (3, 3)))
    ax.plot([q], [q], "o", color="#e8531d", ms=9, zorder=5)  # the extinction probability
    ax.text(q, -0.05, f"q = {q:.2f}", color="#e8531d", ha="center", va="top", fontsize=9)
    ax.set_title(label, fontsize=10)
    ax.set_xlim(0, 1); ax.set_ylim(0, 1); ax.set_aspect("equal")
    ax.set_xlabel("z")
axes[0].set_ylabel("h(z)  and  y = z")
fig.suptitle("Extinction probability is the smallest solution of h(z) = z", fontsize=11)
fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()

Common pitfalls

WarningWhere branching intuition misleads

  • Reading the mean as the whole story. \mathbb E[Z_n]=m^n gives the drift, not the destiny. A supercritical process (m>1) still goes extinct with probability q>0; the expected size exploding does not mean your line survives. (Ross 2014)

  • Missing the critical catastrophe. At m=1 the expected size is constant at 1 forever, yet extinction is certain (whenever there is any variability in offspring). Balanced on average is not the same as safe. (Grinstead and Snell 1997)

  • Grabbing the wrong root. h(z)=z always has z=1 as a solution; the extinction probability is the smallest non-negative root, which is <1 only in the supercritical case. This is the very mistake Watson made in the first attempt at Galton’s problem: he assumed z=1 was the only root, and so concluded that every surname dies out with certainty. (Bienaymé had the right answer in 1845.) (Grinstead and Snell 1997) Solve by iterating q \leftarrow h(q) from 0 (the table in Step 4) and it converges to the correct one.

  • Assuming independence you don’t have. The clean q=h(q) needs offspring counts to be independent and identically distributed across individuals and generations. Finite populations, saturation, or contagion that depletes the susceptible pool break the model — real epidemics bend below the branching prediction as they run out of people to infect.

Appendix · the mathematics

Expected size. Condition on Z_n and use that each of its members has mean m offspring: \mathbb E[Z_{n+1}\mid Z_n] = m\,Z_n, so \mathbb E[Z_{n+1}] = m\,\mathbb E[Z_n]. With Z_0=1 this iterates to \mathbb E[Z_n]=m^n. (Ross 2014)

The extinction equation. Let h(z)=\mathbb E[z^{X}] be the offspring PGF and h_n(z)=\mathbb E[z^{Z_n}] the PGF of the n-th generation. Because generation n+1 is a sum of Z_n independent offspring counts, the generating functions compose: h_{n+1}(z)=h_n(h(z))=h(h(z)) iterated — the PGF of the whole line is h applied to itself n times. The extinction probability is q=\lim_n \mathbb P(Z_n=0)=\lim_n h_n(0), and taking the limit in h_{n+1}(0)=h(h_n(0)) gives the fixed-point equation q = h(q), \qquad q = \text{smallest root in } [0,1]. (Grinstead and Snell 1997)

Why the smallest root, and the m=1 knife-edge. On [0,1], h is continuous, non-decreasing, and convex (its second derivative \sum_k k(k-1)p_k z^{k-2}\ge 0), with h(0)=p_0\ge 0 and h(1)=1. A convex curve meets the straight line y=z in at most two points on [0,1]. The slope at the top end is h'(1)=\sum_k k\,p_k = m. If m\le 1 the curve approaches z=1 from above and the only intersection is z=1, so q=1. If m>1 the curve crosses the diagonal from above at a unique interior point q<1; that smaller root is the extinction probability, and the iteration q_{k+1}=h(q_k) started at q_0=0 increases to it. (The critical case m=1 falls in the first branch, hence certain extinction unless every individual has exactly one child, p_1=1.) (Ross 2014; Grinstead and Snell 1997)

Connections

  • Rests on: generating functions (the offspring PGF h(z)=\mathbb E[z^X] whose fixed point is the answer) and Markov chains (the population size is a chain absorbed at 0).
  • Twin of: the giant-component transition in Erdős–Rényi graphs — the local branching approximation is why a giant component appears exactly when the mean degree passes 1 — and the cascades and spreading processes that use the same fizzle-or-explode threshold.
  • Applies to: epidemic modelling (m=R_0 and the containment threshold), the diffusion of behaviour and information, and any self-reproducing count.

## Why it matters

The branching process is the probability behind every "does it fizzle or does it take off?" question a social
scientist meets. In an epidemic the offspring are the people one infected person infects, and the mean $m$ is
exactly the **basic reproduction number $R_0$**: the sharp threshold at $m=1$ is why "keep $R_0$ below one" is
the entire logic of containment. In the diffusion of a rumour, a product, or a protest, the offspring are the
people each adopter converts, and the same threshold decides whether an idea dies in a handful of shares or
cascades across a population. Galton and Watson invented the model in 1873 to ask whether aristocratic
**surnames** were doomed to extinction. [@grinstead_snell] The same mathematics is the microscopic engine
under the [giant-component transition](../../content/complexity/random-graphs-erdos-renyi.qmd) in random
graphs and the [cascades](../../content/complexity/information-cascades.qmd) that sweep through networks.

## The idea in words {#sec-idea}

Think of a family tree drawn downwards from one founder. Each person, independently of everyone else, rolls
the same die to decide how many children to have. The tree either stops (a whole generation has no children)
or keeps going forever.

Two ideas carry the whole entry:

1. **The average multiplies.** If each person has $m$ children on average, each generation is on average $m$
   times the last. Above one the average explodes; below one it shrinks to nothing.
2. **The line dies only if every branch dies.** The founder's children each start an independent copy of the
   same tree. So "the whole line dies out" means "every one of those sub-trees dies out", and independent
   chances multiply. That self-similarity gives a single equation for the chance of extinction.

The surprise is that the average and the fate can disagree. A line whose average size grows every generation
can still be more likely than not to vanish.

## A family line, by hand {#sec-by-hand}

Grinstead and Snell's running example [@grinstead_snell]: each person has 0 children with probability 0.2,
1 child with probability 0.5, and 2 children with probability 0.3.

**Step 1 — the average.** The mean number of children is $0\times0.2+1\times0.5+2\times0.3=1.1$, just above
one. On average the line grows by 10% a generation: $1.1^{12}\approx3.1$ people expected in generation 12.

**Step 2 — dying out by generation 1.** The founder has no children with probability $0.2$.

**Step 3 — dying out by generation 2.** Condition on the founder's children. With 0 children the line is
already dead. With 1 child, that child's line must die within one generation (chance 0.2). With 2 children,
both must (chance $0.2^2$). So
$$
0.2 + 0.5\times0.2 + 0.3\times0.2^2 = 0.312 .
$$

**Step 4 — repeat.** Each generation's answer comes from the last one by the same recipe: take the previous
value $d$ and compute $0.2+0.5d+0.3d^2$.

| by generation | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 6 | 8 | 10 | 12 |
|:-:|:-:|:-:|:-:|:-:|:-:|:-:|:-:|:-:|
| chance the line has died out | 0.200 | 0.312 | 0.385 | 0.437 | 0.506 | 0.549 | 0.578 | 0.599 |

These match Grinstead and Snell's Table 10.1 [@grinstead_snell].

**Step 5 — the limit.** The numbers creep up towards a value $d$ that the recipe leaves unchanged:
$d = 0.2+0.5d+0.3d^2$. This quadratic has two roots, $d=1$ and $d=0.2/0.3=\tfrac23$. The chance of eventual
extinction is the smaller one, **2/3**, even though the expected size keeps growing [@grinstead_snell]. Twelve
generations (0.599) are still well short of it.

A real-data version: Keyfitz's estimates of the number of daughters born to Japanese women in 1960 give a mean
of about 1.84 daughters, and an extinction probability for a female line of only about 0.32
[@grinstead_snell].

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Notation key — open if a symbol is unfamiliar"}
- $p_k$ — the probability that one person has exactly $k$ children (the **offspring distribution**).
- $X, X_i$ — the number of children of one person, a random draw from $\{p_k\}$.
- $Z_n$ — the number of people in generation $n$; $Z_0=1$ is the founder.
- $m=\mathbb E[X]$ — the mean number of children (1.1 above). $\mathbb E[\cdot]$ is the expected value.
- $h(z)=\sum_k p_k z^k$ — the **probability generating function**: a polynomial whose coefficients are the
  $p_k$. Above, $h(z)=0.2+0.5z+0.3z^2$. Step 4's recipe *is* $h$.
- $q$ (Grinstead and Snell write $d$) — the probability that the line eventually dies out.
- *Fixed point* — a value the function leaves unchanged, $h(q)=q$.
- *Absorbing state* — a state a Markov chain can enter but never leave (here, zero people).
:::

## The model: count the next generation {#sec-model}

Fix an **offspring distribution** $\{p_k\}$: $p_k$ is the probability that any given individual has exactly
$k$ children, independently of everyone else. Start with a single progenitor, $Z_0 = 1$. Generation $n+1$ is
the pooled offspring of the $Z_n$ individuals alive in generation $n$:
$$
Z_{n+1} = \sum_{i=1}^{Z_n} X_i,
$$
where each $X_i$ is an independent draw from $\{p_k\}$. [@ross_prob_models]

> **In plain words:** next generation's size is the total number of children of everyone alive now.

The population $Z_n$ is a Markov chain on $\{0,1,2,\dots\}$ whose only absorbing state is $0$: once a
generation is empty, the line is gone forever. Everything reduces to a race between that absorbing $0$ and
unbounded growth.

## The mean tells you the drift {#sec-mean}

Let $m = \mathbb E[X] = \sum_k k\,p_k$ be the **mean number of offspring** per individual. Because generations
compound multiplicatively, the expected population size is just $m$ raised to the generation number:
[@ross_prob_models; @grinstead_snell]
$$
\mathbb E[Z_n] = m^{\,n}.
$$

> **In plain words:** on average, each generation is $m$ times the size of the one before. With $m=1.1$,
> twelve generations multiply the founder by about 3.1.

That one line already splits the world into three regimes, named for how they sit against the knife-edge
$m=1$:

- **Subcritical ($m < 1$):** expected size decays geometrically to zero — the line withers.
- **Critical ($m = 1$):** expected size stays exactly $1$ forever — perfectly balanced on average.
- **Supercritical ($m > 1$):** expected size explodes geometrically — room to survive.

But the *expectation* is only the drift, not the fate of any single family. A supercritical process can still go
extinct by bad luck in the first few generations; a critical one, astonishingly, dies out with certainty even
though its expected size never shrinks. To get the actual chance of extinction you need the generating
function, not just the mean. The family line above is the example: mean 1.1, yet a 2/3 chance of dying out.

## Extinction is the fixed point of the generating function {#sec-extinction}

Encode the offspring distribution in its **probability generating function**
$h(z) = \sum_k p_k z^k = \mathbb E[z^X]$ — the integer-valued sibling of the
[moment generating function](../../content/01-foundations/moment-generating-functions.qmd). Let $q$ be the
**extinction probability**, the chance that the population eventually hits zero. The self-similar structure of
the process pins $q$ down exactly: condition on the progenitor's own number of children. If the founder has
$k$ children, each child independently launches a fresh, identical copy of the *whole* process, and the line
dies out only if **all $k$** of those independent sub-lines die out — probability $q^k$. Averaging over $k$:
[@ross_prob_models; @grinstead_snell]
$$
q = \sum_{k} p_k\, q^{k} = h(q).
$$

> **In plain words:** the chance of dying out equals the average, over the founder's number of children, of
> the chance that *all* those children's lines die out. Step 5 solved exactly this equation, with
> $h(q)=0.2+0.5q+0.3q^2$.

**The extinction probability is a fixed point of the offspring generating function** — specifically, the
*smallest* non-negative root of $h(z)=z$. Since $h$ is increasing and **convex** (curving upwards, like a
bowl) on $[0,1]$ with $h(1)=1$, the
geometry is decisive (see the figure): its slope at $z=1$ is exactly $h'(1)=m$, so

- if $m \le 1$, the curve $h$ stays above the diagonal until they meet only at $z=1$, forcing $q=1$ —
  **certain extinction**;
- if $m > 1$, the convex curve dips below the diagonal and crosses it at some $q < 1$ before reaching $1$ —
  a genuine chance $1-q$ of surviving forever.

This is the sharp $m=1$ threshold made exact. The critical case $m=1$ lands in the first branch: it goes
extinct with probability one, the model's most famous counter-intuitive fact — expected size constant, yet
survival impossible.

```{python}
#| label: fig-branching-extinction
#| fig-cap: "Extinction probability = the smallest fixed point of h(z) = z. Each panel plots the offspring generating function h(z) (grey, here Poisson: h(z)=e^{m(z-1)}) against the diagonal y = z (ink). The extinction probability q is where they first meet (orange). Left, subcritical (mean m = 0.8): the curve stays above the line and only touches it at z = 1, so q = 1 — the line is certain to die out. Right, supercritical (m = 1.6): the convex curve crosses the diagonal early, at q ≈ 0.36, leaving a 1 − q ≈ 0.64 chance of surviving forever. The slope of h at z = 1 is exactly m, which is why m = 1 is the knife-edge."
#| code-fold: true
#| code-summary: "Show the figure code"

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

z = np.linspace(0.0, 1.0, 400)

def pgf(m, z):                    # Poisson(m) offspring: h(z) = exp(m(z-1))
    return np.exp(m * (z - 1.0))

def extinction_prob(m):           # smallest fixed point of h(z)=z, by iterating from 0
    q = 0.0
    for _ in range(500):
        q = np.exp(m * (q - 1.0))
    return q

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(9.4, 4.4), sharey=True)
for ax, m, label in zip(axes, [0.8, 1.6],
                        ["subcritical   m = 0.8", "supercritical   m = 1.6"]):
    ax.plot(z, z, color="#161613", lw=1.4)                  # diagonal y = z
    ax.plot(z, pgf(m, z), color="#5e5e54", lw=2.0)          # offspring PGF h(z)
    q = extinction_prob(m)
    ax.vlines(q, 0, q, color="#e8531d", lw=1.0, ls=(0, (3, 3)))
    ax.plot([q], [q], "o", color="#e8531d", ms=9, zorder=5)  # the extinction probability
    ax.text(q, -0.05, f"q = {q:.2f}", color="#e8531d", ha="center", va="top", fontsize=9)
    ax.set_title(label, fontsize=10)
    ax.set_xlim(0, 1); ax.set_ylim(0, 1); ax.set_aspect("equal")
    ax.set_xlabel("z")
axes[0].set_ylabel("h(z)  and  y = z")
fig.suptitle("Extinction probability is the smallest solution of h(z) = z", fontsize=11)
fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

## Common pitfalls

::: {.callout-warning title="Where branching intuition misleads"}

- **Reading the mean as the whole story.** $\mathbb E[Z_n]=m^n$ gives the drift, not the destiny. A
  supercritical process ($m>1$) still goes extinct with probability $q>0$; the *expected* size exploding does
  not mean *your* line survives. [@ross_prob_models]

- **Missing the critical catastrophe.** At $m=1$ the expected size is constant at $1$ forever, yet extinction
  is **certain** (whenever there is any variability in offspring). Balanced on average is not the same as
  safe. [@grinstead_snell]

- **Grabbing the wrong root.** $h(z)=z$ always has $z=1$ as a solution; the extinction probability is the
  **smallest** non-negative root, which is $<1$ only in the supercritical case. This is the very mistake
  Watson made in the first attempt at Galton's problem: he assumed $z=1$ was the only root, and so concluded
  that every surname dies out with certainty. (Bienaymé had the right answer in 1845.)
  [@grinstead_snell] Solve by iterating $q \leftarrow h(q)$ from $0$ (the table in Step 4) and it converges
  to the correct one.

- **Assuming independence you don't have.** The clean $q=h(q)$ needs offspring counts to be **independent and
  identically distributed** across individuals and generations. Finite populations, saturation, or contagion
  that depletes the susceptible pool break the model — real epidemics bend below the branching prediction as
  they run out of people to infect.

:::

## Appendix · the mathematics {.unnumbered}

::: {.math-appendix}

**Expected size.** Condition on $Z_n$ and use that each of its members has mean $m$ offspring:
$\mathbb E[Z_{n+1}\mid Z_n] = m\,Z_n$, so $\mathbb E[Z_{n+1}] = m\,\mathbb E[Z_n]$. With $Z_0=1$ this iterates
to $\mathbb E[Z_n]=m^n$. [@ross_prob_models]

**The extinction equation.** Let $h(z)=\mathbb E[z^{X}]$ be the offspring PGF and $h_n(z)=\mathbb E[z^{Z_n}]$
the PGF of the $n$-th generation. Because generation $n+1$ is a sum of $Z_n$ independent offspring counts, the
generating functions **compose**: $h_{n+1}(z)=h_n(h(z))=h(h(z))$ iterated — the PGF of the whole line is $h$
applied to itself $n$ times. The extinction probability is $q=\lim_n \mathbb P(Z_n=0)=\lim_n h_n(0)$, and
taking the limit in $h_{n+1}(0)=h(h_n(0))$ gives the fixed-point equation
$$
q = h(q), \qquad q = \text{smallest root in } [0,1].
$$
[@grinstead_snell]

**Why the smallest root, and the $m=1$ knife-edge.** On $[0,1]$, $h$ is continuous, non-decreasing, and
**convex** (its second derivative $\sum_k k(k-1)p_k z^{k-2}\ge 0$), with $h(0)=p_0\ge 0$ and $h(1)=1$. A
convex curve meets the straight line $y=z$ in at most two points on $[0,1]$. The slope at the top end is
$h'(1)=\sum_k k\,p_k = m$. If $m\le 1$ the curve approaches $z=1$ from above and the only intersection is
$z=1$, so $q=1$. If $m>1$ the curve crosses the diagonal from above at a unique interior point $q<1$; that
smaller root is the extinction probability, and the iteration $q_{k+1}=h(q_k)$ started at $q_0=0$ increases to
it. (The critical case $m=1$ falls in the first branch, hence certain extinction unless every individual has
exactly one child, $p_1=1$.) [@ross_prob_models; @grinstead_snell]

:::
