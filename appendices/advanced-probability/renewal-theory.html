Renewal Theory

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source

Events that repeat at irregular intervals — batteries replaced, buses arriving, machines repaired — and why, in the long run, only the average gap matters.

Something happens, then the clock restarts and you wait a random time for it to happen again. Whatever pattern the waits follow, the long-run rate of events depends on just one number: the average wait. Attach a cost or a reward to each cycle, and the long-run rate of reward is the average reward per cycle divided by the average cycle length. There is one famous trap: the gap you happen to land in is longer than a typical gap.

TipWhere you probably came from — and what you need

You likely arrived from the Poisson process (“what if the gaps are not exponential?”), queueing (“why does Little’s law hold so generally?”), the warning about waiting times that aren’t memoryless in common distributions, or the friendship paradox, which is the same biased-sampling trap seen on a network.

You need: what an average (expected value) is, and the idea that averages settle down in long runs (the law of large numbers). You don’t need calculus. Every worked number below is a division.

Why it matters

Plenty of social and operational processes repeat: a patient returns to a clinic, a tenant moves, a government falls and a new one forms, a machine breaks and is repaired. The gaps are rarely exponential. People age, schedules exist, and wear accumulates. So the Poisson process, with its memoryless gaps, is often the wrong model. Renewal theory says you can still answer the long-run questions (how often, at what cost, what fraction of the time) without knowing the shape of the gaps. It also warns about a sampling trap that turns up well beyond probability: whenever you sample “whatever is going on right now”, long episodes are over-represented (Ross 2014).

The idea in words

Picture a lamp with one bulb. When the bulb dies you replace it at once, and the new one lasts a random time. Over ten years, how many bulbs do you buy? If bulbs last 1,000 hours on average, you buy about one per 1,000 hours. It does not matter whether every bulb lasts almost exactly 1,000 hours, or some die in a week and others last for years. Over a long horizon the lifetimes add up to (number of bulbs) × (average life), so the count is roughly the horizon divided by the average life.

Now give each cycle a price, for example the cost of the bulb plus a penalty if it dies in the dark. The long-run cost per hour is the average cost of one cycle divided by the average length of one cycle. You only ever need to think about one cycle.

The trap: walk into the room at a random moment and ask how long the current bulb will last in total. Long-lived bulbs occupy more of the timeline, so a random moment is more likely to fall inside one. The bulb you inspect is, on average, a long one.

Batteries, trains and buses, by hand

Ross’s batteries (Examples 7.5–7.6). Beverly’s radio runs on one battery. Its life is uniform between 30 and 60 hours, so the mean life is 45 hours. In the long run she changes batteries at a rate of 1 per 45 hours (Ross 2014). The answer uses only the mean. A battery that always lasted exactly 45 hours would give the same rate.

Now suppose she keeps no spares, and each trip to buy one takes a uniform time between 0 and 1 hour, so half an hour on average. A cycle is now “battery life + trip”, with mean 45+0.5=45.5 hours. The rate becomes 1 per 45.5 hours, or 2 batteries every 91 hours (Ross 2014).

Ross’s train depot (Example 7.12), a renewal-reward calculation. Passengers arrive on average one per unit of time. A train leaves as soon as N are waiting. Each departure costs 6, and each waiting passenger costs 2 per unit of time. A cycle runs from one departure to the next, so it lasts N units on average. The expected waiting cost in a cycle is 2\times(1+2+\dots+(N-1))=N(N-1): one passenger waits through the second gap, two through the third, and so on. Divide cost per cycle by cycle length:

train leaves when N = 1 2 3 4 5
cost per unit time, \frac{6}{N}+(N-1) 6 4 4 4.5 5.2

Either N=2 or N=3 is best, which is Ross’s answer (Ross 2014). The arithmetic of one cycle settles the long-run policy.

Buses and the inspection paradox (our numbers). Buses come at gaps that alternate between 5 and 25 minutes, so the average gap is 15 minutes. Arrive at a random moment. The 25-minute gaps cover 25 of every 30 minutes, so you land in a long gap with probability 5/6. The average length of the gap you land in is \frac{5\cdot5+25\cdot25}{30}\approx21.7 minutes, not 15. Your average wait is about 10.8 minutes, not the 7.5 that “half the average gap” suggests. (A simulation of 200,000 random arrivals gives 10.8.)

NoteNotation key — open if a symbol is unfamiliar
  • X_1, X_2,\dots — the gaps (interarrival times) between successive events. i.i.d. means independent and all drawn from the same distribution.
  • \mu=\mathrm{E}[X] — the mean gap (45 hours for Beverly’s batteries).
  • S_n=X_1+\dots+X_n — the time of the nth event.
  • N(t) — the number of events by time t; m(t)=\mathrm{E}[N(t)] — its expected value, the renewal function.
  • R_n — the reward (or cost) earned in cycle n.
  • \xrightarrow[t\to\infty]{} — “gets closer and closer to, as t grows without limit”.
  • “with probability 1” — happens on essentially every run of the process, not just on average.

From Poisson to renewal

Let X_1, X_2, \dots be i.i.d. positive interarrival times with mean \mu = \mathrm{E}[X] < \infty, and let S_n = X_1 + \dots + X_n be the time of the n-th event. The renewal counting process is N(t) = \max\{n : S_n \le t\}, the number of events by time t. (Ross 2014)

In plain words: N(t) is the largest number of gaps that fit, end to end, before time t.

When the X_i are exponential this is exactly the Poisson process. Renewal theory keeps the i.i.d. gaps but frees their distribution: light bulbs replaced when they fail, a machine repaired on each breakdown, a bus arriving on an irregular schedule.

The elementary renewal theorem: only the mean survives

The central fact is that the long-run rate of events is 1/\mu, whatever the gap distribution: (Ross 2014) \frac{N(t)}{t} \xrightarrow[t\to\infty]{} \frac{1}{\mu} \quad\text{(with probability 1)}, \qquad \frac{\mathrm{E}[N(t)]}{t} \xrightarrow[t\to\infty]{} \frac{1}{\mu}.

In plain words: in the long run you get one event per average gap. Beverly’s 1-per-45-hours is this formula with \mu=45.

The first is the strong law for renewals; the second is the elementary renewal theorem, about the expected count m(t)=\mathrm{E}[N(t)] (the renewal function). Both say the same intuitive thing: if the average gap is \mu, then over a long horizon you fit about t/\mu of them. The variance, skewness, everything else about the gap distribution washes out of the rate. (Ross notes that the second does not follow automatically from the first. A sequence can converge to 0 on every run while its expected value stays at 1, which is his Example 7.4 (Ross 2014).) The figure shows three very different gap laws with the same mean converging to the identical rate.

Show the figure code 
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(4)
T = 400
def running_rate(gaps):
    S = np.cumsum(gaps)
    t = np.arange(1, int(S[-1]))
    N = np.searchsorted(S, t, side="right")
    return t, N / t

# three interarrival laws, all with mean 1
gens = {
    "exponential (var 1)":  lambda m: rng.exponential(1.0, m),
    "gamma (var 0.2)":      lambda m: rng.gamma(5.0, 1/5.0, m),
    "lognormal (heavy)":    lambda m: rng.lognormal(mean=-0.5*np.log(2), sigma=np.sqrt(np.log(2)), size=m),
}
colors = {"exponential (var 1)": "#161613", "gamma (var 0.2)": "#5e5e54", "lognormal (heavy)": "#e8531d"}

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7.6, 4.3))
for name, gen in gens.items():
    gaps = gen(2 * T)
    t, r = running_rate(gaps)
    ax.plot(t, r, color=colors[name], lw=1.7, label=name)
ax.axhline(1.0, color="#8a8a7e", lw=1.2, ls=(0, (4, 3)))
ax.text(T, 1.0, r"  $1/\mu = 1$", color="#5e5e54", fontsize=9, va="bottom", ha="right")
ax.set_xlim(0, T); ax.set_ylim(0.6, 1.5)
ax.set_xlabel("time  t"); ax.set_ylabel("running event rate  N(t)/t")
ax.set_title("Same mean gap → same long-run rate, whatever the distribution")
ax.legend(fontsize=8.5, frameon=False, title="interarrival law")
fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()

The renewal-reward theorem

The real workhorse attaches a reward to each cycle. If cycle n earns R_n (i.i.d., possibly correlated with its own length X_n), the long-run reward rate is (Ross 2014) \frac{\text{total reward by }t}{t} \xrightarrow[t\to\infty]{} \frac{\mathrm{E}[R]}{\mathrm{E}[X]} = \frac{\text{expected reward per cycle}}{\text{expected cycle length}}.

In plain words: work out what one cycle earns and how long it lasts, on average, and divide. The train table above is exactly this, with costs in place of rewards.

This one identity answers a startling range of questions by defining a “cycle” and a “reward”: the long-run cost rate of a replacement policy (reward = −cost), the long-run fraction of time a machine is broken (reward = downtime per cycle), the average inventory held, the utilisation of a server. You compute one cycle’s expectations and divide — no need to track the whole process. It is also the engine behind queueing results like Little’s law.

The inspection paradox

A famous renewal surprise: the gap that happens to contain a fixed inspection time is longer on average than a typical gap. (Ross 2014) Ross’s setting is a battery in use at a fixed time t. If you try to learn how long batteries last by checking the one in use at time t, you will over-estimate their lifetimes. Long intervals cover more of the timeline, so a randomly chosen instant is more likely to fall inside one. This is length-biased sampling: each interval is caught with probability proportional to its length. It is why the wait for the next bus can average more than half the mean headway (10.8 versus 7.5 minutes in the bus example), and a caution whenever you sample “the interval you happen to be in.”

For a Poisson process the effect is exact and large. For large t, the interval containing t is made of two independent exponential pieces, the time back to the last event and the time forward to the next, so its expected length is about twice an ordinary gap (Ross 2014). The same logic is behind the friendship paradox: following a link samples people in proportion to how many friends they have.

In plain words: if you sample “whatever is happening right now”, long things are over-represented in proportion to their length. Correct for it, or ask about a typical cycle instead.

Common pitfalls

WarningWhere renewal reasoning misleads

  • Assuming the rate needs the exponential. It does not — N(t)/t \to 1/\mu holds for any gap distribution with finite mean. The memoryless property is special to Poisson; the long-run rate is universal. (Ross 2014)

  • Forgetting finite mean. If \mathrm{E}[X]=\infty (very heavy-tailed gaps), the rate degenerates to zero and the clean theorems fail — the same finite-moment caveat as the law of large numbers.

  • Reading the reward rate as an instantaneous rate. \mathrm{E}[R]/\mathrm{E}[X] is a long-run average, not what happens in any single cycle; short horizons can look nothing like it.

  • Ignoring the inspection/length bias. The interval covering a fixed time is length-biased and longer than average; sampling “the current gap” over-weights long gaps. (Ross 2014) The bus example shows a 44% longer wait than the naive guess.

Connections

  • Generalises: the Poisson process (exponential gaps → any gaps).
  • Rests on: the law of large numbers (the long-run-average machinery) and finite-mean conditions from asymptotics.

expected waiting cost in a cycle is $2\times(1+2+\dots+(N-1))=N(N-1)$: one passenger waits through the second
gap, two through the third, and so on. Divide cost per cycle by cycle length:

| train leaves when $N$ = | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
|:-:|:-:|:-:|:-:|:-:|:-:|
| cost per unit time, $\frac{6}{N}+(N-1)$ | 6 | **4** | **4** | 4.5 | 5.2 |

Either $N=2$ or $N=3$ is best, which is Ross's answer [@ross_prob_models]. The arithmetic of one cycle settles
the long-run policy.

**Buses and the inspection paradox (our numbers).** Buses come at gaps that alternate between 5 and 25
minutes, so the average gap is 15 minutes. Arrive at a random moment. The 25-minute gaps cover 25 of every 30
minutes, so you land in a long gap with probability $5/6$. The average length of the gap you land in is
$\frac{5\cdot5+25\cdot25}{30}\approx21.7$ minutes, not 15. Your average wait is about **10.8 minutes**, not the
7.5 that "half the average gap" suggests. (A simulation of 200,000 random arrivals gives 10.8.)

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Notation key — open if a symbol is unfamiliar"}
- $X_1, X_2,\dots$ — the gaps (*interarrival times*) between successive events. *i.i.d.* means independent
  and all drawn from the same distribution.
- $\mu=\mathrm{E}[X]$ — the mean gap (45 hours for Beverly's batteries).
- $S_n=X_1+\dots+X_n$ — the time of the $n$th event.
- $N(t)$ — the number of events by time $t$; $m(t)=\mathrm{E}[N(t)]$ — its expected value, the *renewal
  function*.
- $R_n$ — the reward (or cost) earned in cycle $n$.
- $\xrightarrow[t\to\infty]{}$ — "gets closer and closer to, as $t$ grows without limit".
- "with probability 1" — happens on essentially every run of the process, not just on average.
:::

## From Poisson to renewal

Let $X_1, X_2, \dots$ be i.i.d. positive **interarrival times** with mean $\mu = \mathrm{E}[X] < \infty$, and
let $S_n = X_1 + \dots + X_n$ be the time of the $n$-th event. The **renewal counting process** is
$$
N(t) = \max\{n : S_n \le t\},
$$
the number of events by time $t$. [@ross_prob_models]

> **In plain words:** $N(t)$ is the largest number of gaps that fit, end to end, before time $t$.

When the $X_i$ are exponential this is exactly the Poisson process. Renewal theory keeps the i.i.d. gaps but
frees their distribution: light bulbs replaced when they fail, a machine repaired on each breakdown, a bus
arriving on an irregular schedule.

## The elementary renewal theorem: only the mean survives {#sec-rate}

The central fact is that the **long-run rate of events is $1/\mu$**, whatever the gap distribution: [@ross_prob_models]
$$
\frac{N(t)}{t} \xrightarrow[t\to\infty]{} \frac{1}{\mu} \quad\text{(with probability 1)},
\qquad
\frac{\mathrm{E}[N(t)]}{t} \xrightarrow[t\to\infty]{} \frac{1}{\mu}.
$$

> **In plain words:** in the long run you get one event per average gap. Beverly's 1-per-45-hours is this
> formula with $\mu=45$.

The first is the strong law for renewals; the second is the **elementary renewal theorem**, about the *expected*
count $m(t)=\mathrm{E}[N(t)]$ (the **renewal function**). Both say the same intuitive thing: if the average gap
is $\mu$, then over a long horizon you fit about $t/\mu$ of them. The variance, skewness, everything else about
the gap distribution washes out of the rate. (Ross notes that the second does not follow automatically from the
first. A sequence can converge to 0 on every run while its expected value stays at 1, which is his
Example 7.4 [@ross_prob_models].) The figure shows three very different gap laws with the same mean converging
to the identical rate.

```{python}
#| label: fig-renewal-rate
#| fig-cap: "The elementary renewal theorem: the long-run event rate is 1/μ, whatever the gap distribution. Three renewal processes, each with mean gap μ = 1 but wildly different interarrival laws — exponential (high variance), a tight gamma (low variance), and a heavy-tailed lognormal — have their running rate N(t)/t all converge to the same 1/μ = 1 (dashed). The shape of the gaps controls the early wobble, not the destination. This distribution-free rate is what renewal-reward arguments exploit."
#| code-fold: true
#| code-summary: "Show the figure code"

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(4)
T = 400
def running_rate(gaps):
    S = np.cumsum(gaps)
    t = np.arange(1, int(S[-1]))
    N = np.searchsorted(S, t, side="right")
    return t, N / t

# three interarrival laws, all with mean 1
gens = {
    "exponential (var 1)":  lambda m: rng.exponential(1.0, m),
    "gamma (var 0.2)":      lambda m: rng.gamma(5.0, 1/5.0, m),
    "lognormal (heavy)":    lambda m: rng.lognormal(mean=-0.5*np.log(2), sigma=np.sqrt(np.log(2)), size=m),
}
colors = {"exponential (var 1)": "#161613", "gamma (var 0.2)": "#5e5e54", "lognormal (heavy)": "#e8531d"}

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7.6, 4.3))
for name, gen in gens.items():
    gaps = gen(2 * T)
    t, r = running_rate(gaps)
    ax.plot(t, r, color=colors[name], lw=1.7, label=name)
ax.axhline(1.0, color="#8a8a7e", lw=1.2, ls=(0, (4, 3)))
ax.text(T, 1.0, r"  $1/\mu = 1$", color="#5e5e54", fontsize=9, va="bottom", ha="right")
ax.set_xlim(0, T); ax.set_ylim(0.6, 1.5)
ax.set_xlabel("time  t"); ax.set_ylabel("running event rate  N(t)/t")
ax.set_title("Same mean gap → same long-run rate, whatever the distribution")
ax.legend(fontsize=8.5, frameon=False, title="interarrival law")
fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

## The renewal-reward theorem {#sec-reward}

The real workhorse attaches a **reward** to each cycle. If cycle $n$ earns $R_n$ (i.i.d., possibly correlated
with its own length $X_n$), the **long-run reward rate** is [@ross_prob_models]
$$
\frac{\text{total reward by }t}{t} \xrightarrow[t\to\infty]{} \frac{\mathrm{E}[R]}{\mathrm{E}[X]}
= \frac{\text{expected reward per cycle}}{\text{expected cycle length}}.
$$

> **In plain words:** work out what one cycle earns and how long it lasts, on average, and divide. The train
> table above is exactly this, with costs in place of rewards.

This one identity answers a startling range of questions by defining a "cycle" and a "reward": the long-run
cost rate of a replacement policy (reward = −cost), the long-run fraction of time a machine is broken
(reward = downtime per cycle), the average inventory held, the utilisation of a server. You compute *one
cycle's* expectations and divide — no need to track the whole process. It is also the engine behind
[queueing](queueing.qmd) results like Little's law.

## The inspection paradox {#sec-inspection}

A famous renewal surprise: the gap that happens to **contain a fixed inspection time** is *longer* on average
than a typical gap. [@ross_prob_models] Ross's setting is a battery in use at a fixed time $t$. If you try to
learn how long batteries last by checking the one in use at time $t$, you will over-estimate their lifetimes.
Long intervals cover more of the timeline, so a randomly chosen instant is more likely to fall inside one. This
is *length-biased sampling*: each interval is caught with probability proportional to its length. It is why
the wait for the next bus can average more than half the mean headway (10.8 versus 7.5 minutes in the bus
example), and a caution whenever you sample "the interval you happen to be in."

For a Poisson process the effect is exact and large. For large $t$, the interval containing $t$ is made of two
independent exponential pieces, the time back to the last event and the time forward to the next, so its
expected length is about **twice** an ordinary gap [@ross_prob_models]. The same logic is behind the
[friendship paradox](../../content/complexity/degree-correlations-and-the-friendship-paradox.qmd): following a
link samples people in proportion to how many friends they have.

> **In plain words:** if you sample "whatever is happening right now", long things are over-represented in
> proportion to their length. Correct for it, or ask about a typical cycle instead.

## Common pitfalls

::: {.callout-warning title="Where renewal reasoning misleads"}

- **Assuming the rate needs the exponential.** It does not — $N(t)/t \to 1/\mu$ holds for *any* gap distribution
  with finite mean. The memoryless property is special to Poisson; the long-run rate is universal. [@ross_prob_models]

- **Forgetting finite mean.** If $\mathrm{E}[X]=\infty$ (very heavy-tailed gaps), the rate degenerates to zero
  and the clean theorems fail — the same finite-moment caveat as the [law of large numbers](../math/probability-proofs.qmd).

- **Reading the reward rate as an instantaneous rate.** $\mathrm{E}[R]/\mathrm{E}[X]$ is a *long-run average*,
  not what happens in any single cycle; short horizons can look nothing like it.

- **Ignoring the inspection/length bias.** The interval covering a fixed time is length-biased and longer than
  average; sampling "the current gap" over-weights long gaps. [@ross_prob_models] The bus example shows a 44%
  longer wait than the naive guess.

:::
