Queueing: The M/M/1 Queue

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source

People arrive, one server works through them, and a line forms. The simplest model already teaches the lesson every capacity decision needs: as the server gets close to fully busy, waiting does not creep up, it explodes.

A queue is arrivals, a server, and a line. When the server is idle half the time, the line is short. When the server is busy nine-tenths of the time, the average line is nine people long, and a little more demand makes it far longer. Waiting grows slowly at first and then very fast as the server approaches full use. That one fact governs call centres, clinics, courts and computer servers alike.

TipWhere you probably came from — and what you need

You likely arrived from the Poisson process (arrivals at random times), the exponential distribution in common distributions, the continuous-time chains in Markov chains, or renewal theory, whose renewal-reward idea is behind Little’s law. Or you are trying to understand why a service that runs “at full capacity” has such long waits.

You need: rates (“3 per hour”), averages, and the idea of a long-run proportion of time. You don’t need calculus or Markov-chain theory for the main ideas. The worked example is fractions.

Why it matters

Public services are queues: emergency departments, benefits offices, court dockets, visa processing. So are many of the systems researchers study, such as call-centre staffing and hospital bed occupancy. Managers are often rewarded for keeping staff “fully utilised”. Queueing theory shows why that target makes waits very long, and it gives the arithmetic for how much slack a service needs. The simplest model, the M/M/1 queue, is enough to see the shape of the problem (Ross 2014).

The idea in words

Think of a single clerk at a desk. People turn up at random. Each takes a random amount of time to serve. Sometimes several arrive close together and a line builds. The clerk then needs idle time to clear it.

If the clerk is busy, say, half the time, there is plenty of spare time to clear the occasional burst, so lines stay short. If the clerk is busy 95% of the time, a burst takes a very long time to clear, because there is almost no spare capacity. Before it clears, the next burst often arrives. Lines then pile up on top of lines. That is why waiting rises steeply as the clerk approaches full use. It also explains why “busy 100% of the time” is not efficiency but collapse: there is no spare time at all to recover from bad luck.

A second idea links the length of the line to the wait. If 6 people arrive per hour and each spends 20 minutes in the system, then on average 6\times\frac13=2 people are in the system at any moment. That is Little’s law, and it holds for almost any queue.

A service desk, by hand

This is Ross’s Example 8.2 (Ross 2014). Customers arrive at random, on average one every 12 minutes. Service times are random, averaging 8 minutes.

Step 1 — how busy is the server? The utilisation is (average service time) ÷ (average gap between arrivals) =8/12=2/3. The server is busy two-thirds of the time and idle one-third of the time.

Step 2 — how many are in the system? The M/M/1 formula below gives the chance of finding n people present:

people in the system 0 1 2 3 …
proportion of time 0.333 0.222 0.148 0.099 …

Each entry is two-thirds of the one before. The average works out to 2 people.

Step 3 — how long does a customer spend? By Little’s law, (average number present) = (arrival rate) × (average time in system). So the time in system is 2\div\frac1{12}= 24 minutes: 8 in service and 16 waiting in line.

Step 4 — add 20% more customers. Now they arrive every 10 minutes. Utilisation rises from 2/3 to 0.8. The average number present doubles from 2 to 4, and the time in system rises from 24 to 40 minutes. The wait in line doubles from 16 to 32 minutes. A 20% rise in demand doubled the line (Ross 2014).

NoteNotation key — open if a symbol is unfamiliar
  • M/M/1 — a shorthand: Markovian (memoryless, i.e. Poisson) arrivals / Markovian (exponential) service times / 1 server.
  • \lambda — the arrival rate (customers per unit time; 1/12 per minute above).
  • \mu — the service rate (customers one busy server can finish per unit time; 1/8 per minute above), so 1/\mu is the mean service time.
  • \rho=\lambda/\mu — the utilisation, or traffic intensity: the fraction of time the server is busy.
  • P_n — the long-run proportion of time with exactly n customers in the system.
  • L, W — the average number in the system and the average time a customer spends in it (waiting plus service). L_Q, W_Q — the same, counting only the line, not the person being served.
  • \mathrm{E}[S], \mathrm{E}[S^2] — the mean of the service time and the mean of its square.

The model and its utilisation

Customers arrive as a Poisson process at rate \lambda (interarrivals exponential, mean 1/\lambda); each takes an exponential service time with rate \mu (mean 1/\mu); there is one server and an unbounded waiting room served first-come-first-served. (Ross 2014) The single most important quantity is the traffic intensity or utilisation: \rho = \frac{\lambda}{\mu} = \frac{\text{arrival rate}}{\text{service rate}}, the fraction of time the server is busy.

In plain words: utilisation is the share of the clock the server spends working. In the worked example it is 8/12=2/3.

Because both processes are memoryless, the number in the system is a continuous-time Markov chain — a birth–death chain, meaning it can only move one step at a time: up by one on an arrival and down by one on a service completion.

Stability: the line is stable only if \rho < 1

If customers arrive faster than they can be served (\lambda \ge \mu, i.e. \rho \ge 1), the queue grows without bound — there is no equilibrium. (Ross 2014) A stable queue requires \rho < 1: the server must, on average, have spare capacity. Ross’s way of putting it is that the mean service time must be less than the mean time between arrivals (Ross 2014). This is already the first surprise for newcomers — a server that is busy exactly 100% of the time is not maximally efficient, it is unstable.

The stationary distribution and the queue length

Ross finds the long-run probabilities with a picture. Imagine a person who walks into “room n” whenever there are n customers in the system. Over a long time, they must enter each room as often as they leave it. Writing “rate in = rate out” for every room gives the balance equations (Ross 2014). For room 0, the process leaves only by an arrival and enters only by a service completion from room 1, so \lambda P_0=\mu P_1.

When \rho < 1 the chain settles to a geometric stationary distribution (stationary means the proportions no longer change over time). The probability of finding n customers in the system is (Ross 2014) P_n = (1-\rho)\,\rho^{\,n}, \qquad n = 0, 1, 2, \dots

In plain words: the server is idle a fraction 1-\rho of the time, and each extra person in the system is \rho times as likely as the one before. The table in the worked example is this with \rho=2/3.

From it, the expected number in the system is L = \frac{\rho}{1-\rho}.

In plain words: the line length depends only on how busy the server is, and the denominator 1-\rho is the spare capacity. As spare capacity shrinks toward zero, the line grows without limit.

This is the formula that matters. At \rho = 0.5, L = 1; at \rho = 0.9, L = 9; at \rho = 0.99, L = 99. Halving the idle capacity from 10% to 1% multiplies the line by more than ten. The relationship is not linear — it is a wall, plotted below.

Show the figure code 
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# analytic curve
rho = np.linspace(0.01, 0.97, 300)
L = rho / (1 - rho)

# a quick event-driven M/M/1 simulation to confirm L = rho/(1-rho)
def simulate_L(rho, mu=1.0, n_events=200_000, seed=0):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    lam = rho * mu
    t = 0.0; n = 0; area = 0.0; last = 0.0
    # next arrival / next departure times
    t_arr = rng.exponential(1/lam)
    t_dep = np.inf
    for _ in range(n_events):
        t_next = min(t_arr, t_dep)
        area += n * (t_next - last); last = t_next; t = t_next
        if t_arr <= t_dep:                      # arrival
            n += 1
            t_arr = t + rng.exponential(1/lam)
            if n == 1: t_dep = t + rng.exponential(1/mu)
        else:                                   # departure
            n -= 1
            t_dep = t + rng.exponential(1/mu) if n > 0 else np.inf
    return area / t

sim_rhos = np.array([0.3, 0.5, 0.7, 0.85, 0.92])
sim_L = [simulate_L(r, seed=i) for i, r in enumerate(sim_rhos)]

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7.4, 4.4))
ax.plot(rho, L, color="#161613", lw=2.4, label=r"$L = \rho/(1-\rho)$")
ax.scatter(sim_rhos, sim_L, color="#e8531d", s=55, zorder=5, edgecolor="#161613",
           linewidth=1.0, label="simulation")
ax.axvline(1.0, color="#8a8a7e", lw=1.0, ls=(0, (3, 3)))
ax.set_xlim(0, 1.02); ax.set_ylim(0, 30)
ax.set_xlabel(r"utilisation  $\rho = \lambda/\mu$")
ax.set_ylabel("expected number in system  L")
ax.set_title("Waiting explodes as the server approaches full utilisation")
ax.legend(fontsize=9, frameon=False, loc="upper left")
fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()

Little’s law: a bridge that needs no assumptions

The expected number in the system L, the arrival rate \lambda, and the expected time a customer spends W are tied by Little’s law: (Ross 2014) L = \lambda W.

In plain words: the average crowd equals the arrival rate times how long each person stays. Two people present on average, with one arrival every 12 minutes, means each stays 24 minutes.

Its power is its generality — it holds for essentially any stable queue, not just M/M/1. Ross’s argument is a cost identity: make every customer pay $1 per minute while in the system. The system then earns L per minute, and each customer pays W, so L=\lambda W (Ross 2014). The argument is made rigorous through the regenerative-process version of renewal-reward. Charge only for time in the line and the same argument gives L_Q=\lambda W_Q. So once you know L, you know the average wait W = L/\lambda, and vice versa. For M/M/1, W = \frac{1}{\mu - \lambda} — which, like L, diverges as \lambda \to \mu.

One more general fact makes these formulas usable. Because Poisson arrivals come at moments that carry no information about the past, the state an arriving customer finds has the same distribution as the state at a random moment. Ross’s phrase is that Poisson arrivals see time averages (Ross 2014). With scheduled, evenly spaced arrivals this fails, and what customers experience can differ from the time average.

Why variability matters

M/M/1 assumes exponential service times, which vary a lot. Ross’s M/G/1 model allows any service-time distribution (the G is for “general”) and gives the average wait in line, the Pollaczek–Khintchine formula (Ross 2014): W_Q = \frac{\lambda\,\mathrm{E}[S^2]}{2\,(1-\lambda\,\mathrm{E}[S])}.

In plain words: waiting depends on how busy the server is (the denominator again) and on the mean square of the service time, which grows with its variance. Keep the mean fixed and make service more variable, and everyone waits longer.

Since \mathrm{E}[S^2]=\mathrm{Var}(S)+(\mathrm{E}[S])^2, Ross notes that for a fixed mean, all of L, L_Q, W and W_Q increase with the variance of service times (Ross 2014). On the worked example’s numbers (arrivals every 12 minutes, 8-minute mean service): exponential service has \mathrm{E}[S^2]=128 and gives W_Q=16 minutes, as before. Service that always takes exactly 8 minutes has \mathrm{E}[S^2]=64 and gives W_Q= 8 minutes, half the wait at the same utilisation.

Common pitfalls

WarningWhere queue intuition fails

  • Aiming for 100% utilisation. Maximal throughput is not maximal efficiency. At \rho = 1 the queue is unstable; the sweet spot leaves slack, and how much depends on how much delay you can tolerate. (Ross 2014)

  • Assuming waiting scales linearly with load. L = \rho/(1-\rho) is convex and explodes near 1 — the last 10% of utilisation costs far more than the first 10%. Planning capacity on an average load ignores the wall. The worked example shows it: 20% more customers doubled the line.

  • Forgetting variability is baked in. M/M/1’s exponential service is highly variable; the same utilisation with steadier service queues less (half the wait for fixed-length service, by the Pollaczek–Khintchine formula). Bursty arrivals or more variable service than exponential mean more queueing than M/M/1 predicts. (Ross 2014)

  • Misapplying Little’s law across an unstable or non-stationary system. L=\lambda W needs a stable, long-run-average regime; it is not a snapshot identity for a queue that is still filling up.

From the sources

NoteWho says what
  • Ross, Introduction to Probability Models (Queueing Theory chapter) — the cost identity and Little’s law L=\lambda W (and L_Q=\lambda W_Q) as general results, rigorous for regenerative queues (§8.2.1); Poisson arrivals see time averages (Proposition 8.2); the M/M/1 queue, its “rooms” argument and balance equations, the stability condition \rho<1, the geometric stationary distribution P_n=(1-\rho)\rho^n, L=\rho/(1-\rho) and W=1/(\mu-\lambda) (§8.3.1); Example 8.2, the worked example used here (L = 2 and W = 24 minutes, then L = 4 and W = 40 after a 20% rise in arrivals); and the M/G/1 Pollaczek–Khintchine formula with the remark that waits increase with service-time variance (§8.5).
  • The single-clerk picture and the fixed-length-service comparison (16 versus 8 minutes) are this encyclopedia’s own illustrations of Ross’s results.

Connections

  • Built from: the Poisson process (arrivals) and Markov chains (the number-in-system is a continuous-time birth–death chain).
  • Uses: renewal-reward — Little’s law is a renewal-reward identity, which is why it holds far beyond M/M/1.
  • Applies to: capacity and staffing decisions, server/network provisioning, and any first-come-first-served system where load meets finite service.

arrivals) $=8/12=2/3$. The server is busy two-thirds of the time and idle one-third of the time.

**Step 2 — how many are in the system?** The M/M/1 formula below gives the chance of finding $n$ people
present:

| people in the system | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | … |
|:-:|:-:|:-:|:-:|:-:|:-:|
| proportion of time | 0.333 | 0.222 | 0.148 | 0.099 | … |

Each entry is two-thirds of the one before. The average works out to **2 people**.

**Step 3 — how long does a customer spend?** By Little's law, (average number present) = (arrival rate) ×
(average time in system). So the time in system is $2\div\frac1{12}=$ **24 minutes**: 8 in service and 16
waiting in line.

**Step 4 — add 20% more customers.** Now they arrive every 10 minutes. Utilisation rises from $2/3$ to $0.8$.
The average number present **doubles from 2 to 4**, and the time in system rises from 24 to **40 minutes**.
The wait in line doubles from 16 to 32 minutes. A 20% rise in demand doubled the line [@ross_prob_models].

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Notation key — open if a symbol is unfamiliar"}
- M/M/1 — a shorthand: **M**arkovian (memoryless, i.e. Poisson) arrivals / **M**arkovian (exponential)
  service times / **1** server.
- $\lambda$ — the arrival rate (customers per unit time; $1/12$ per minute above).
- $\mu$ — the service rate (customers one busy server can finish per unit time; $1/8$ per minute above), so
  $1/\mu$ is the mean service time.
- $\rho=\lambda/\mu$ — the utilisation, or *traffic intensity*: the fraction of time the server is busy.
- $P_n$ — the long-run proportion of time with exactly $n$ customers in the system.
- $L$, $W$ — the average number in the system and the average time a customer spends in it (waiting plus
  service). $L_Q$, $W_Q$ — the same, counting only the line, not the person being served.
- $\mathrm{E}[S]$, $\mathrm{E}[S^2]$ — the mean of the service time and the mean of its square.
:::

## The model and its utilisation

Customers arrive as a [Poisson process](poisson-process.qmd) at rate $\lambda$ (interarrivals exponential,
mean $1/\lambda$); each takes an **exponential** service time with rate $\mu$ (mean $1/\mu$); there is one
server and an unbounded waiting room served first-come-first-served. [@ross_prob_models] The single most
important quantity is the **traffic intensity** or **utilisation**:
$$
\rho = \frac{\lambda}{\mu} = \frac{\text{arrival rate}}{\text{service rate}},
$$
the fraction of time the server is busy.

> **In plain words:** utilisation is the share of the clock the server spends working. In the worked example
> it is $8/12=2/3$.

Because both processes are memoryless, the number in the system is a continuous-time
[Markov chain](markov-chains.qmd) — a *birth–death chain*, meaning it can only move one step at a time: up by
one on an arrival and down by one on a service completion.

## Stability: the line is stable only if $\rho < 1$ {#sec-stability}

If customers arrive faster than they can be served ($\lambda \ge \mu$, i.e. $\rho \ge 1$), the queue grows
without bound — there is no equilibrium. [@ross_prob_models] A stable queue **requires** $\rho < 1$: the server
must, on average, have spare capacity. Ross's way of putting it is that the mean service time must be less than
the mean time between arrivals [@ross_prob_models]. This is already the first surprise for newcomers — a
server that is busy *exactly* 100% of the time is not maximally efficient, it is unstable.

## The stationary distribution and the queue length {#sec-length}

Ross finds the long-run probabilities with a picture. Imagine a person who walks into "room $n$" whenever
there are $n$ customers in the system. Over a long time, they must enter each room as often as they leave it.
Writing "rate in = rate out" for every room gives the **balance equations** [@ross_prob_models]. For room 0,
the process leaves only by an arrival and enters only by a service completion from room 1, so
$\lambda P_0=\mu P_1$.

When $\rho < 1$ the chain settles to a **geometric** stationary distribution (*stationary* means the
proportions no longer change over time). The probability of finding $n$ customers in the system is
[@ross_prob_models]
$$
P_n = (1-\rho)\,\rho^{\,n}, \qquad n = 0, 1, 2, \dots
$$

> **In plain words:** the server is idle a fraction $1-\rho$ of the time, and each extra person in the system
> is $\rho$ times as likely as the one before. The table in the worked example is this with $\rho=2/3$.

From it, the **expected number in the system** is
$$
L = \frac{\rho}{1-\rho}.
$$

> **In plain words:** the line length depends only on how busy the server is, and the denominator $1-\rho$ is
> the spare capacity. As spare capacity shrinks toward zero, the line grows without limit.

This is the formula that matters. At $\rho = 0.5$, $L = 1$; at $\rho = 0.9$, $L = 9$; at $\rho = 0.99$,
$L = 99$. Halving the *idle* capacity from 10% to 1% multiplies the line by more than ten. The relationship is
not linear — it is a wall, plotted below.

```{python}
#| label: fig-mm1-congestion
#| fig-cap: "The congestion catastrophe. The expected number in an M/M/1 system, L = ρ/(1−ρ), against utilisation ρ. Below ~70% the line is short and grows slowly; past 90% it turns near-vertical — small increases in load produce huge increases in waiting. The orange markers are averages from a direct event-driven simulation of the queue, confirming the formula. The lesson every capacity plan needs: the cost of the last few percent of utilisation is not a few percent of waiting — it is unbounded."
#| code-fold: true
#| code-summary: "Show the figure code"

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# analytic curve
rho = np.linspace(0.01, 0.97, 300)
L = rho / (1 - rho)

# a quick event-driven M/M/1 simulation to confirm L = rho/(1-rho)
def simulate_L(rho, mu=1.0, n_events=200_000, seed=0):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    lam = rho * mu
    t = 0.0; n = 0; area = 0.0; last = 0.0
    # next arrival / next departure times
    t_arr = rng.exponential(1/lam)
    t_dep = np.inf
    for _ in range(n_events):
        t_next = min(t_arr, t_dep)
        area += n * (t_next - last); last = t_next; t = t_next
        if t_arr <= t_dep:                      # arrival
            n += 1
            t_arr = t + rng.exponential(1/lam)
            if n == 1: t_dep = t + rng.exponential(1/mu)
        else:                                   # departure
            n -= 1
            t_dep = t + rng.exponential(1/mu) if n > 0 else np.inf
    return area / t

sim_rhos = np.array([0.3, 0.5, 0.7, 0.85, 0.92])
sim_L = [simulate_L(r, seed=i) for i, r in enumerate(sim_rhos)]

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7.4, 4.4))
ax.plot(rho, L, color="#161613", lw=2.4, label=r"$L = \rho/(1-\rho)$")
ax.scatter(sim_rhos, sim_L, color="#e8531d", s=55, zorder=5, edgecolor="#161613",
           linewidth=1.0, label="simulation")
ax.axvline(1.0, color="#8a8a7e", lw=1.0, ls=(0, (3, 3)))
ax.set_xlim(0, 1.02); ax.set_ylim(0, 30)
ax.set_xlabel(r"utilisation  $\rho = \lambda/\mu$")
ax.set_ylabel("expected number in system  L")
ax.set_title("Waiting explodes as the server approaches full utilisation")
ax.legend(fontsize=9, frameon=False, loc="upper left")
fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

## Little's law: a bridge that needs no assumptions {#sec-little}

The expected number in the system $L$, the arrival rate $\lambda$, and the expected time a customer spends
$W$ are tied by **Little's law**: [@ross_prob_models]
$$
L = \lambda W.
$$

> **In plain words:** the average crowd equals the arrival rate times how long each person stays. Two people
> present on average, with one arrival every 12 minutes, means each stays 24 minutes.

Its power is its generality — it holds for essentially *any* stable queue, not just M/M/1. Ross's argument is
a cost identity: make every customer pay \$1 per minute while in the system. The system then earns $L$ per
minute, and each customer pays $W$, so $L=\lambda W$ [@ross_prob_models]. The argument is made rigorous through
the regenerative-process version of [renewal-reward](renewal-theory.qmd). Charge only for time in the line and
the same argument gives $L_Q=\lambda W_Q$. So once you know $L$, you know the average wait $W = L/\lambda$, and
vice versa. For M/M/1, $W = \frac{1}{\mu - \lambda}$ — which, like $L$, diverges as $\lambda \to \mu$.

One more general fact makes these formulas usable. Because Poisson arrivals come at moments that carry no
information about the past, the state an arriving customer finds has the same distribution as the state at a
random moment. Ross's phrase is that **Poisson arrivals see time averages** [@ross_prob_models]. With
scheduled, evenly spaced arrivals this fails, and what customers experience can differ from the time average.

## Why variability matters {#sec-variability}

M/M/1 assumes exponential service times, which vary a lot. Ross's **M/G/1** model allows any service-time
distribution (the G is for "general") and gives the average wait in line, the Pollaczek–Khintchine formula
[@ross_prob_models]:
$$
W_Q = \frac{\lambda\,\mathrm{E}[S^2]}{2\,(1-\lambda\,\mathrm{E}[S])}.
$$

> **In plain words:** waiting depends on how busy the server is (the denominator again) and on the mean
> *square* of the service time, which grows with its variance. Keep the mean fixed and make service more
> variable, and everyone waits longer.

Since $\mathrm{E}[S^2]=\mathrm{Var}(S)+(\mathrm{E}[S])^2$, Ross notes that for a fixed mean, all of $L$, $L_Q$,
$W$ and $W_Q$ increase with the variance of service times [@ross_prob_models]. On the worked example's
numbers (arrivals every 12 minutes, 8-minute mean service): exponential service has $\mathrm{E}[S^2]=128$ and
gives $W_Q=16$ minutes, as before. Service that always takes exactly 8 minutes has $\mathrm{E}[S^2]=64$ and
gives $W_Q=$ **8 minutes**, half the wait at the same utilisation.

## Common pitfalls

::: {.callout-warning title="Where queue intuition fails"}

- **Aiming for 100% utilisation.** Maximal throughput is *not* maximal efficiency. At $\rho = 1$ the queue is
  unstable; the sweet spot leaves slack, and how much depends on how much delay you can tolerate. [@ross_prob_models]

- **Assuming waiting scales linearly with load.** $L = \rho/(1-\rho)$ is convex and explodes near 1 — the last
  10% of utilisation costs far more than the first 10%. Planning capacity on an average load ignores the wall.
  The worked example shows it: 20% more customers doubled the line.

- **Forgetting variability is baked in.** M/M/1's exponential service is *highly* variable; the same utilisation
  with steadier service queues less (half the wait for fixed-length service, by the Pollaczek–Khintchine
  formula). Bursty arrivals or more variable service than exponential mean *more* queueing than M/M/1 predicts.
  [@ross_prob_models]

- **Misapplying Little's law across an unstable or non-stationary system.** $L=\lambda W$ needs a stable,
  long-run-average regime; it is not a snapshot identity for a queue that is still filling up.

:::
