Factorial ANOVA & ANCOVA: Interactions and Covariates

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source

Two classic designs from the experimental tradition, and why both are really regressions: one with a product term, one with a control.

One-way ANOVA asks whether one factor moves the outcome. Real designs ask more. Factorial ANOVA crosses two factors and asks whether their effects simply add up or whether one factor’s effect depends on the other, which is an interaction. ANCOVA adds a measured covariate and compares groups as if they had started equal on it. Both are the ANOVA you already know, rebuilt as a regression with a product term or a control variable.

Why it matters

A great deal of applied work in psychology, education, health and evaluation is reported in the language of ANOVA: “a 2 × 2 between-subjects design”, “a significant condition × status interaction”, “adjusted means controlling for age”. If you learned regression first, these read like a foreign dialect. They aren’t. A factorial ANOVA is a regression with dummy variables and their product, and an ANCOVA is a regression with a group dummy and a continuous control. (Martin and Bridgmon 2012) Knowing both dialects lets you read either literature. It also shows you the questions each design is really asking: does the treatment work for everyone, or only for some? and how much of this group difference is just a head start?

The sources treat these as the natural next step after comparing groups. Martin & Bridgmon give each design a full chapter, with a complete worked study, assumption checks, hand calculations and a written-up result. (Martin and Bridgmon 2012) This entry follows their two studies closely, both simulated on a real contingency-management trial for substance use.

Two factors at once: main effects and interactions

A factorial design crosses every level of one factor with every level of another. With two treatments and two patient types you get four cells, and one experiment answers three questions: (Martin and Bridgmon 2012)

  • Main effect of A. Averaging over B, does A change the outcome?
  • Main effect of B. Averaging over A, does B change the outcome?
  • The A × B interaction. Is A’s effect the same at every level of B, or does it depend on B?

The interaction is the prize. On a profile plot, which shows cell means with one line per level of B, no interaction means parallel lines: A shifts everyone by the same amount. An interaction means the lines are not parallel. They might fan out (A helps one group more) or even cross (A helps one group and does nothing, or harm, to another). (Martin and Bridgmon 2012) The number of hypotheses grows quickly. A 2 × 3 × 2 design has 12 cells and seven null hypotheses: three main effects, three two-way interactions and one three-way. (Martin and Bridgmon 2012)

Worked example: who does contingency management help?

Martin & Bridgmon’s factorial study follows 120 cocaine users in outpatient treatment, 30 per cell. (Martin and Bridgmon 2012)

  • Factor A, condition (randomised): standard care (SC) vs standard care plus contingency management (SC+CM), which pays monetary rewards for drug-free samples and for completing goal activities.
  • Factor B, treatment history (observed, not assigned): 0–1 prior treatment attempts vs 2 or more.
  • Outcome: retention, meaning the number of weeks out of 12 the participant stayed in treatment.

The cell means (weeks retained):

0–1 prior attempts ≥ 2 prior attempts Row mean
SC 5.567 4.800 5.183
SC + CM 5.700 7.533 6.617
Column mean 5.633 6.167 5.900

And the two-way ANOVA (α = .01): (Martin and Bridgmon 2012)

Source SS df F p partial η²
Condition 61.633 1 28.605 < .001 .198
History 8.533 1 3.961 .049 .033
Condition × History 50.700 1 23.531 < .001 .169
Error 249.933 116

Read naively, the table says “CM works, history barely matters”. The row means agree: 6.617 weeks with CM against 5.183 without. But look at the cells. For people with 0–1 prior attempts, CM moves retention from 5.567 to 5.700, which is nothing. For people with two or more prior attempts it moves retention from 4.800 to 7.533. The lines on the profile plot cross. The “main effect” of CM is an average of a large effect and a zero effect.

Simple effects: unpacking the interaction

When the interaction is significant, the next step is simple effects: the effect of one factor at each single level of the other, tested against the full model’s error term. (Martin and Bridgmon 2012)

Simple effect F(1, 116) p
CM vs SC, among 0–1 prior attempts 0.12 .726
CM vs SC, among ≥ 2 prior attempts 52.01 < .001
History, within SC 4.09 .045
History, within SC+CM 23.40 < .001

The conclusion changes shape. Contingency management helps the people with a history of failed treatment, and has no detectable effect on the others. (Martin and Bridgmon 2012) That is a finding about for whom the treatment works, which a one-way comparison of CM against SC would have hidden. Note also that the history factor was observed, not randomised. The interaction is a well-measured moderation, but it is not proof that prior treatment causes people to respond to CM. Anything that travels with treatment history (severity, age, motivation) could be doing the work. (This is the same caution the heterogeneous-effects entry applies to subgroup findings in experiments.)

It’s a regression with a product term

Code condition as a 0/1 dummy \text{CM} and history as a 0/1 dummy \text{H} (1 = two or more prior attempts). Then the four cell means are reproduced exactly by

\text{weeks} = \beta_0 + \beta_1\,\text{CM} + \beta_2\,\text{H} + \beta_3\,(\text{CM}\times\text{H}) + u,

which is the interaction model from the functional-form entry. Solving from the table (our arithmetic on the book’s cell means): \beta_0 = 5.567 (SC, 0–1 attempts), \beta_1 = 0.133 (CM’s effect among 0–1), \beta_2 = -0.767 (history’s effect under SC), and

\beta_3 = (7.533 - 4.800) - (5.700 - 5.567) = 2.733 - 0.133 = 2.600 .

The interaction coefficient is a difference in differences: CM’s effect among the repeat-treatment group minus CM’s effect among the others. It is the same logic as difference-in-differences, applied to two factors instead of group and time. With equal cell sizes, the ANOVA’s interaction F test and the t-test on \beta_3 are the same test (F = t^2).

ANCOVA: comparing groups as if they started equal

A covariate is a measured variable that predicts the outcome but is not the factor you are studying. Martin & Bridgmon describe it as an extraneous variable you choose to measure and account for, rather than leave in the error term. (Martin and Bridgmon 2012) Analysis of covariance combines ANOVA with regression. It removes the part of the outcome the covariate predicts, and then compares the groups. It does two jobs: (Martin and Bridgmon 2012)

  1. Precision. Variation the covariate explains no longer counts as noise, so the error term shrinks and the test gets sharper.
  2. Mean adjustment. Each group’s mean is moved to what it would be if every group had the same covariate value, the grand mean. These are the adjusted means.

The adjustment is simple. Fit one common within-group slope b_W of outcome on covariate, then slide each group’s mean along it to the grand mean of the covariate:

\bar Y_{g}^{\text{adj}} = \bar Y_g - b_W\,(\bar X_g - \bar X).

A group that happened to have a higher covariate value (and so, via the slope, a higher outcome) gets marked down; a group that started lower gets marked up. (Martin and Bridgmon 2012)

Worked example: abstinence, adjusted for age

Martin & Bridgmon’s ANCOVA study randomises 114 treatment-seeking substance users to standard care or standard care plus contingency management (57 each). (Martin and Bridgmon 2012) The outcome is the longest duration of verified abstinence (weeks, 0–12). The covariate is age, on the reasoning that older participants bring more prior treatment experience and coping skills.

Mean age Raw mean abstinence Adjusted mean (age = 33.29)
SC 31.82 4.79 4.950
SC + CM 34.75 6.07 5.910

The pooled within-group slope is about 0.11 weeks of abstinence per year of age. The CM group was, by chance, about three years older, so part of its raw 1.28-week advantage is what age alone would predict. After adjustment the gap is 0.96 weeks (99% CI 0.09 to 1.83), and the ANCOVA gives F(1, 111) = 8.332, p = .005, partial η² = .07. Age itself is a strong predictor (F = 34.72, partial η² = .24). (Martin and Bridgmon 2012)

The book also works the whole calculation by hand. It adjusts the total and within-group sums of squares by removing the part each shares with age, and takes the adjusted between-groups SS as the difference: 25.54 on 1 df against an adjusted error of 340.62 on 111 df, giving F ≈ 8.3. (Martin and Bridgmon 2012) The appendix below sets this out.

The lesson hiding in the numbers: ANCOVA is not a power button

A common belief is that adding a covariate always makes your effect “more significant”. This example says otherwise. From the book’s own printed sums of squares (SS between 46.74, SS within 447.20), a plain one-way ANOVA on abstinence would give F(1,112) \approx 46.74 / (447.20/112) \approx 11.7 (our arithmetic). The ANCOVA F is smaller, at 8.33.

Both of ANCOVA’s jobs happened, and they pulled in opposite directions:

  • Precision went up. The error mean square fell from about 3.99 to 3.07. Age soaked up roughly a quarter of the within-group variation (the within-group r^2 is .238).
  • The effect went down. Randomisation happened to put older, and so longer-abstaining, participants in the CM arm. The adjustment took that head start back.

This is ANCOVA working correctly. In a randomised trial, the adjusted estimate corrects a chance imbalance and is more precise. It is not a reliably larger number, and choosing covariates because they make the result bigger is a researcher degree of freedom. It is also the precision role that the experiments entry means by “regression adjustment”: covariates in a randomised trial are there to sharpen the estimate, not to rescue it.

The homogeneity-of-slopes assumption

ANCOVA assumes the covariate–outcome slope is the same in every group: the lines are parallel, so the adjusted difference is the same whatever covariate value you evaluate it at. (Martin and Bridgmon 2012) You test this by adding the group × covariate interaction; a significant interaction means the slopes differ.

In the book’s example that test gives F(1, 110) = 5.377, p = .022. (Martin and Bridgmon 2012) The authors proceed because they set α = .01. At the conventional .05, the assumption would have failed. If the slopes truly differ, “the” adjusted effect no longer exists: the treatment effect depends on age, which is an interaction question, and belongs in the factorial framework above, not a single adjusted mean.

Effect sizes for these designs

Both designs are reported with partial η², the share of variance an effect explains once the other effects are set aside: \text{SS}_{\text{effect}} / (\text{SS}_{\text{effect}} + \text{SS}_{\text{error}}). Martin & Bridgmon also report ω², which corrects for the fact that sample η² overstates the population effect. For the factorial study, ω² is .190 for condition, .024 for history and .160 for the interaction, each a little below its partial η². (Martin and Bridgmon 2012) The book plans its sample sizes around these quantities too. For the ANCOVA study, an expected partial η² of .11 at α = .01 and power .80 called for 98 participants. (Martin and Bridgmon 2012) See effect sizes and statistical power for the general machinery.

Common pitfalls

WarningWhere this goes wrong

  • Reporting a main effect when the interaction crosses. “CM increased retention” is true on average and false for half the sample. When the interaction is significant, lead with the simple effects. (Martin and Bridgmon 2012) It is the ANOVA form of the functional-form warning about reading a main effect alone.

  • Treating an observed factor as if it were randomised. Treatment history was measured, not assigned, so the design is only partly experimental. Its main effect and the interaction describe who responds, not what history causes.

  • Misreading the covariate’s F test. The covariate row tests whether the covariate predicts the outcome within groups. It does not test whether the groups differ on the covariate. (Martin & Bridgmon’s own write-up slips here, reading a significant age row as evidence the groups differed in age. (Martin and Bridgmon 2012))

  • Letting α decide the parallel-slopes assumption. A slopes test at p = .022 passes at α = .01 and fails at .05. If the slopes plausibly differ, report the interaction, not one adjusted effect.

  • Adjusting for something the treatment can change. Covariates should be measured before treatment or be fixed traits like age. Adjusting for a post-treatment variable removes part of the effect you are trying to measure, the bad-control problem.

  • Expecting ANCOVA to boost significance, or shopping for covariates that do. Adjustment can shrink an effect that a chance imbalance had inflated, as it did here. Pre-specify covariates.

  • Leaning on “observed power”. The book reports post-hoc observed power (.605 for the ANCOVA treatment effect). It is a transformation of the p-value and adds nothing (why). (Martin and Bridgmon 2012)

  • Forgetting multiplicity in follow-ups. Four simple-effects tests after an omnibus F are four more chances for a false positive (multiple testing).

Appendix · the mathematics

Two-way ANOVA decomposition (balanced, n per cell). With a levels of A, b levels of B, cell means \bar Y_{ij}, marginal means \bar Y_{i\cdot}, \bar Y_{\cdot j} and grand mean \bar Y:

\text{SS}_A = nb\sum_i(\bar Y_{i\cdot}-\bar Y)^2,\qquad \text{SS}_B = na\sum_j(\bar Y_{\cdot j}-\bar Y)^2, \text{SS}_{AB} = n\sum_{i,j}(\bar Y_{ij}-\bar Y_{i\cdot}-\bar Y_{\cdot j}+\bar Y)^2,\qquad \text{SS}_E = \sum_{i,j,k}(Y_{ijk}-\bar Y_{ij})^2,

with degrees of freedom a-1, b-1, (a-1)(b-1) and ab(n-1). Each F is its mean square over \text{MS}_E. The interaction term is the part of the cell means that is not additive in row and column effects. In a 2 × 2 it is proportional to the squared difference in differences, \text{SS}_{AB} = \tfrac{n}{4}\big[(\bar Y_{22}-\bar Y_{21})-(\bar Y_{12}-\bar Y_{11})\big]^2. In the example, \tfrac{30}{4}(2.600)^2 \approx 50.7, matching the table.

Simple effects of A at level j of B use only that column’s cells but keep the full model’s error: F = \text{SS}_{A\text{ at }B_j}/\text{MS}_E on (a-1,\ ab(n-1)) df.

ANCOVA by adjusted sums of squares. Let \text{SS}_T(Y), \text{SS}_W(Y) be total and within-group sums of squares of the outcome, \text{SS}_T(X), \text{SS}_W(X) those of the covariate, and \text{SC}_T, \text{SC}_W the corresponding sums of cross-products. Removing the part explained by X:

\text{SS}^{\text{adj}}_T = \text{SS}_T(Y)-\frac{\text{SC}_T^2}{\text{SS}_T(X)},\qquad \text{SS}^{\text{adj}}_W = \text{SS}_W(Y)-\frac{\text{SC}_W^2}{\text{SS}_W(X)},\qquad \text{SS}^{\text{adj}}_B = \text{SS}^{\text{adj}}_T-\text{SS}^{\text{adj}}_W .

Then F = \text{SS}^{\text{adj}}_B/(k-1) \,\big/\, \text{SS}^{\text{adj}}_W/(N-k-1): one error df is spent on the slope. In the example (Martin and Bridgmon 2012): 493.94 - 1081^2/9145.45 = 366.16; 447.20 - 974^2/8900.98 = 340.62; so \text{SS}^{\text{adj}}_B = 25.54 and F = 25.54 / (340.62/111) \approx 8.3. The pooled within-group slope is b_W = \text{SC}_W/\text{SS}_W(X) = 974/8900.98 \approx 0.109, which gives the adjusted means \bar Y_g - b_W(\bar X_g-\bar X).

As a regression. ANCOVA is OLS of Y on group dummies and X: Y_i = \beta_0 + \tau D_i + \gamma X_i + u_i. Here \hat\gamma = b_W and \hat\tau is the adjusted mean difference. The parallel-slopes test adds D_i X_i and tests its coefficient. By Frisch–Waugh–Lovell, \hat\tau is the group difference after partialling X out of both Y and D, which is why a chance group imbalance in X moves it.

Effect sizes. \eta^2_p = \text{SS}_{\text{eff}}/(\text{SS}_{\text{eff}}+\text{SS}_E) and \omega^2 = \big(\text{SS}_{\text{eff}} - df_{\text{eff}}\,\text{MS}_E\big) / \big(\text{SS}_T + \text{MS}_E\big). For the factorial condition effect: (61.633 - 2.155)/(370.8 + 2.155) \approx .159 by that total-SS form. The book instead puts \text{SS}_{\text{eff}}+\text{SS}_E+\text{MS}_E in the denominator (a partial form), giving .190. (Martin and Bridgmon 2012)

::: {.lead-kernel}
One-way ANOVA asks whether *one* factor moves the outcome. Real designs ask more. **Factorial ANOVA**
crosses two factors and asks whether their effects simply add up or whether one factor's effect
*depends on* the other, which is an **interaction**. **ANCOVA** adds a measured covariate and compares
groups *as if they had started equal on it*. Both are the ANOVA you already know, rebuilt as a
regression with a product term or a control variable.
:::

## Why it matters

A great deal of applied work in psychology, education, health and evaluation is reported in the
language of ANOVA: "a 2 × 2 between-subjects design", "a significant condition × status interaction",
"adjusted means controlling for age". If you learned regression first, these read like a foreign
dialect. They aren't. A factorial ANOVA is a regression with dummy variables and their product, and an
ANCOVA is a regression with a group dummy and a continuous control. [@martin_bridgmon] Knowing both
dialects lets you read either literature. It also shows you the questions each design is really asking:
*does the treatment work for everyone, or only for some?* and *how much of this group difference is
just a head start?*

The sources treat these as the natural next step after [comparing groups](../02-inference/comparing-groups.qmd).
Martin & Bridgmon give each design a full chapter, with a complete worked study, assumption checks, hand
calculations and a written-up result. [@martin_bridgmon] This entry follows their two studies closely,
both simulated on a real contingency-management trial for substance use.

## Two factors at once: main effects and interactions

A **factorial design** crosses every level of one factor with every level of another. With two
treatments and two patient types you get four **cells**, and one experiment answers three questions:
[@martin_bridgmon]

- **Main effect of A.** Averaging over B, does A change the outcome?
- **Main effect of B.** Averaging over A, does B change the outcome?
- **The A × B interaction.** Is A's effect *the same* at every level of B, or does it depend on B?

The interaction is the prize. On a **profile plot**, which shows cell means with one line per level of
B, *no interaction* means parallel lines: A shifts everyone by the same amount. An interaction means the
lines are not parallel. They might fan out (A helps one group more) or even cross (A helps one group and
does nothing, or harm, to another). [@martin_bridgmon] The number of hypotheses grows quickly. A
2 × 3 × 2 design has 12 cells and seven null hypotheses: three main effects, three two-way interactions
and one three-way. [@martin_bridgmon]

### Worked example: who does contingency management help?

Martin & Bridgmon's factorial study follows 120 cocaine users in outpatient treatment, 30 per cell.
[@martin_bridgmon]

- **Factor A, condition** (randomised): standard care (SC) vs standard care plus **contingency
  management** (SC+CM), which pays monetary rewards for drug-free samples and for completing goal
  activities.
- **Factor B, treatment history** (observed, not assigned): 0–1 prior treatment attempts vs 2 or more.
- **Outcome:** retention, meaning the number of weeks out of 12 the participant stayed in treatment.

The cell means (weeks retained):

|  | 0–1 prior attempts | ≥ 2 prior attempts | Row mean |
|---|---|---|---|
| **SC** | 5.567 | 4.800 | 5.183 |
| **SC + CM** | 5.700 | 7.533 | 6.617 |
| **Column mean** | 5.633 | 6.167 | 5.900 |

And the two-way ANOVA (α = .01): [@martin_bridgmon]

| Source | SS | df | F | p | partial η² |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Condition | 61.633 | 1 | 28.605 | < .001 | .198 |
| History | 8.533 | 1 | 3.961 | .049 | .033 |
| Condition × History | 50.700 | 1 | 23.531 | < .001 | .169 |
| Error | 249.933 | 116 | | | |

Read naively, the table says "CM works, history barely matters". The row means agree: 6.617 weeks with CM
against 5.183 without. But look at the cells. For people with 0–1 prior attempts, CM moves retention from
5.567 to 5.700, which is nothing. For people with two or more prior attempts it moves retention from
4.800 to **7.533**. The lines on the profile plot **cross**. The "main effect" of CM is an average of a
large effect and a zero effect.

### Simple effects: unpacking the interaction

When the interaction is significant, the next step is **simple effects**: the effect of one factor at
*each single level* of the other, tested against the full model's error term. [@martin_bridgmon]

| Simple effect | F(1, 116) | p |
|---|---|---|
| CM vs SC, among 0–1 prior attempts | 0.12 | .726 |
| CM vs SC, among ≥ 2 prior attempts | 52.01 | < .001 |
| History, within SC | 4.09 | .045 |
| History, within SC+CM | 23.40 | < .001 |

The conclusion changes shape. Contingency management helps the people with a history of failed treatment,
and has no detectable effect on the others. [@martin_bridgmon] That is a finding about *for whom* the
treatment works, which a one-way comparison of CM against SC would have hidden. Note also that the history
factor was observed, not randomised. The interaction is a well-measured **moderation**, but it is not proof
that prior treatment *causes* people to respond to CM. Anything that travels with treatment history
(severity, age, motivation) could be doing the work. (This is the same caution the
[heterogeneous-effects entry](../05-causal-inference/heterogeneous-effects-in-experiments.qmd) applies
to subgroup findings in experiments.)

### It's a regression with a product term

Code condition as a 0/1 dummy $\text{CM}$ and history as a 0/1 dummy $\text{H}$ (1 = two or more prior
attempts). Then the four cell means are reproduced exactly by

$$
\text{weeks} = \beta_0 + \beta_1\,\text{CM} + \beta_2\,\text{H} + \beta_3\,(\text{CM}\times\text{H}) + u,
$$

which is the [interaction model](functional-form.qmd) from the functional-form entry. Solving from the
table (our arithmetic on the book's cell means): $\beta_0 = 5.567$ (SC, 0–1 attempts), $\beta_1 = 0.133$
(CM's effect among 0–1), $\beta_2 = -0.767$ (history's effect under SC), and

$$
\beta_3 = (7.533 - 4.800) - (5.700 - 5.567) = 2.733 - 0.133 = 2.600 .
$$

The interaction coefficient is a **difference in differences**: CM's effect among the repeat-treatment
group minus CM's effect among the others. It is the same logic as
[difference-in-differences](../05-causal-inference/difference-in-differences.qmd), applied to two factors
instead of group and time. With equal cell sizes, the ANOVA's interaction F test and the t-test on
$\beta_3$ are the same test ($F = t^2$).

## ANCOVA: comparing groups as if they started equal

A **covariate** is a measured variable that predicts the outcome but is not the factor you are studying.
Martin & Bridgmon describe it as an extraneous variable you choose to measure and account for, rather
than leave in the error term. [@martin_bridgmon] **Analysis of covariance** combines ANOVA with
regression. It removes the part of the outcome the covariate predicts, and then compares the groups.
It does two jobs: [@martin_bridgmon]

1. **Precision.** Variation the covariate explains no longer counts as noise, so the error term shrinks
   and the test gets sharper.
2. **Mean adjustment.** Each group's mean is moved to what it would be if every group had the *same*
   covariate value, the grand mean. These are the **adjusted means**.

The adjustment is simple. Fit one common within-group slope $b_W$ of outcome on covariate, then slide
each group's mean along it to the grand mean of the covariate:

$$
\bar Y_{g}^{\text{adj}} = \bar Y_g - b_W\,(\bar X_g - \bar X).
$$

A group that happened to have a higher covariate value (and so, via the slope, a higher outcome) gets
marked down; a group that started lower gets marked up. [@martin_bridgmon]

### Worked example: abstinence, adjusted for age

Martin & Bridgmon's ANCOVA study randomises 114 treatment-seeking substance users to standard care or
standard care plus contingency management (57 each). [@martin_bridgmon] The outcome is the **longest
duration of verified abstinence** (weeks, 0–12). The covariate is **age**, on the reasoning that older
participants bring more prior treatment experience and coping skills.

|  | Mean age | Raw mean abstinence | Adjusted mean (age = 33.29) |
|---|---|---|---|
| SC | 31.82 | 4.79 | **4.950** |
| SC + CM | 34.75 | 6.07 | **5.910** |

The pooled within-group slope is about 0.11 weeks of abstinence per year of age. The CM group was, by
chance, about three years older, so part of its raw 1.28-week advantage is what age alone would predict.
After adjustment the gap is **0.96 weeks** (99% CI 0.09 to 1.83), and the ANCOVA gives
**F(1, 111) = 8.332, p = .005**, partial η² = .07. Age itself is a strong predictor
(F = 34.72, partial η² = .24). [@martin_bridgmon]

The book also works the whole calculation by hand. It adjusts the total and within-group sums of squares
by removing the part each shares with age, and takes the adjusted between-groups SS as the difference:
25.54 on 1 df against an adjusted error of 340.62 on 111 df, giving F ≈ 8.3. [@martin_bridgmon] The
appendix below sets this out.

### The lesson hiding in the numbers: ANCOVA is not a power button

A common belief is that adding a covariate always makes your effect "more significant". This example says
otherwise. From the book's own printed sums of squares (SS between 46.74, SS within 447.20), a plain
one-way ANOVA on abstinence would give $F(1,112) \approx 46.74 / (447.20/112) \approx 11.7$ (our
arithmetic). The ANCOVA F is *smaller*, at 8.33.

Both of ANCOVA's jobs happened, and they pulled in opposite directions:

- **Precision went up.** The error mean square fell from about 3.99 to 3.07. Age soaked up roughly a
  quarter of the within-group variation (the within-group $r^2$ is .238).
- **The effect went down.** Randomisation happened to put older, and so longer-abstaining, participants
  in the CM arm. The adjustment took that head start back.

This is ANCOVA working correctly. In a randomised trial, the adjusted estimate corrects a chance imbalance
and is more precise. It is not a reliably larger number, and choosing covariates *because* they make the
result bigger is a [researcher degree of freedom](../06-field-realities/questionable-research-practices.qmd).
It is also the precision role that the [experiments entry](../05-causal-inference/randomized-experiments.qmd)
means by "regression adjustment": covariates in a randomised trial are there to sharpen the estimate, not
to rescue it.

### The homogeneity-of-slopes assumption

ANCOVA assumes the covariate–outcome slope is the **same in every group**: the lines are parallel, so the
adjusted difference is the same whatever covariate value you evaluate it at. [@martin_bridgmon] You test
this by adding the group × covariate interaction; a significant interaction means the slopes differ.

In the book's example that test gives **F(1, 110) = 5.377, p = .022**. [@martin_bridgmon] The authors
proceed because they set α = .01. At the conventional .05, the assumption would have failed. If the
slopes truly differ, "the" adjusted effect no longer exists: the treatment effect depends on age, which is
an interaction question, and belongs in the factorial framework above, not a single adjusted mean.

## Effect sizes for these designs

Both designs are reported with **partial η²**, the share of variance an effect explains once the other
effects are set aside: $\text{SS}_{\text{effect}} / (\text{SS}_{\text{effect}} + \text{SS}_{\text{error}})$.
Martin & Bridgmon also report **ω²**, which corrects for the fact that sample η² overstates the population
effect. For the factorial study, ω² is .190 for condition, .024 for history and .160 for the interaction,
each a little below its partial η². [@martin_bridgmon] The book plans its sample sizes around these
quantities too. For the ANCOVA study, an expected partial η² of .11 at α = .01 and power .80 called for
98 participants. [@martin_bridgmon] See [effect sizes](../02-inference/effect-sizes.qmd) and
[statistical power](../02-inference/statistical-power.qmd) for the general machinery.

## Common pitfalls

::: {.callout-warning title="Where this goes wrong"}

- **Reporting a main effect when the interaction crosses.** "CM increased retention" is true on average
  and false for half the sample. When the interaction is significant, lead with the simple effects.
  [@martin_bridgmon] It is the ANOVA form of the [functional-form](functional-form.qmd) warning about
  reading a main effect alone.

- **Treating an observed factor as if it were randomised.** Treatment history was measured, not assigned,
  so the design is only partly experimental. Its main effect and the interaction describe *who responds*,
  not what history *causes*.

- **Misreading the covariate's F test.** The covariate row tests whether the covariate predicts the
  outcome *within* groups. It does not test whether the groups differ on the covariate. (Martin &
  Bridgmon's own write-up slips here, reading a significant age row as evidence the groups differed in
  age. [@martin_bridgmon])

- **Letting α decide the parallel-slopes assumption.** A slopes test at p = .022 passes at α = .01 and
  fails at .05. If the slopes plausibly differ, report the interaction, not one adjusted effect.

- **Adjusting for something the treatment can change.** Covariates should be measured *before* treatment
  or be fixed traits like age. Adjusting for a post-treatment variable removes part of the effect you are
  trying to measure, the [bad-control](../05-causal-inference/directed-acyclic-graphs.qmd) problem.

- **Expecting ANCOVA to boost significance, or shopping for covariates that do.** Adjustment can shrink an
  effect that a chance imbalance had inflated, as it did here. Pre-specify covariates.

- **Leaning on "observed power".** The book reports post-hoc observed power (.605 for the ANCOVA
  treatment effect). It is a transformation of the p-value and adds nothing
  ([why](../02-inference/statistical-power.qmd)). [@martin_bridgmon]

- **Forgetting multiplicity in follow-ups.** Four simple-effects tests after an omnibus F are four more
  chances for a false positive ([multiple testing](../02-inference/multiple-testing.qmd)).

:::

## Appendix · the mathematics {.unnumbered}

::: {.math-appendix}

**Two-way ANOVA decomposition (balanced, $n$ per cell).** With $a$ levels of A, $b$ levels of B, cell
means $\bar Y_{ij}$, marginal means $\bar Y_{i\cdot}$, $\bar Y_{\cdot j}$ and grand mean $\bar Y$:

$$
\text{SS}_A = nb\sum_i(\bar Y_{i\cdot}-\bar Y)^2,\qquad
\text{SS}_B = na\sum_j(\bar Y_{\cdot j}-\bar Y)^2,
$$
$$
\text{SS}_{AB} = n\sum_{i,j}(\bar Y_{ij}-\bar Y_{i\cdot}-\bar Y_{\cdot j}+\bar Y)^2,\qquad
\text{SS}_E = \sum_{i,j,k}(Y_{ijk}-\bar Y_{ij})^2,
$$

with degrees of freedom $a-1$, $b-1$, $(a-1)(b-1)$ and $ab(n-1)$. Each $F$ is its mean square over
$\text{MS}_E$. The interaction term is the part of the cell means that is *not* additive in row and column
effects. In a 2 × 2 it is proportional to the squared difference in differences,
$\text{SS}_{AB} = \tfrac{n}{4}\big[(\bar Y_{22}-\bar Y_{21})-(\bar Y_{12}-\bar Y_{11})\big]^2$. In the
example, $\tfrac{30}{4}(2.600)^2 \approx 50.7$, matching the table.

**Simple effects** of A at level $j$ of B use only that column's cells but keep the full model's error:
$F = \text{SS}_{A\text{ at }B_j}/\text{MS}_E$ on $(a-1,\ ab(n-1))$ df.

**ANCOVA by adjusted sums of squares.** Let $\text{SS}_T(Y)$, $\text{SS}_W(Y)$ be total and within-group
sums of squares of the outcome, $\text{SS}_T(X)$, $\text{SS}_W(X)$ those of the covariate, and $\text{SC}_T$,
$\text{SC}_W$ the corresponding sums of cross-products. Removing the part explained by $X$:

$$
\text{SS}^{\text{adj}}_T = \text{SS}_T(Y)-\frac{\text{SC}_T^2}{\text{SS}_T(X)},\qquad
\text{SS}^{\text{adj}}_W = \text{SS}_W(Y)-\frac{\text{SC}_W^2}{\text{SS}_W(X)},\qquad
\text{SS}^{\text{adj}}_B = \text{SS}^{\text{adj}}_T-\text{SS}^{\text{adj}}_W .
$$

Then $F = \text{SS}^{\text{adj}}_B/(k-1) \,\big/\, \text{SS}^{\text{adj}}_W/(N-k-1)$: one error df is spent on
the slope. In the example [@martin_bridgmon]: $493.94 - 1081^2/9145.45 = 366.16$;
$447.20 - 974^2/8900.98 = 340.62$; so $\text{SS}^{\text{adj}}_B = 25.54$ and
$F = 25.54 / (340.62/111) \approx 8.3$. The pooled within-group slope is
$b_W = \text{SC}_W/\text{SS}_W(X) = 974/8900.98 \approx 0.109$, which gives the adjusted means
$\bar Y_g - b_W(\bar X_g-\bar X)$.

**As a regression.** ANCOVA is OLS of $Y$ on group dummies and $X$:
$Y_i = \beta_0 + \tau D_i + \gamma X_i + u_i$. Here $\hat\gamma = b_W$ and $\hat\tau$ is the adjusted mean
difference. The parallel-slopes test adds $D_i X_i$ and tests its coefficient. By
[Frisch–Waugh–Lovell](multiple-regression.qmd), $\hat\tau$ is the group difference after partialling $X$
out of both $Y$ and $D$, which is why a chance group imbalance in $X$ moves it.

**Effect sizes.** $\eta^2_p = \text{SS}_{\text{eff}}/(\text{SS}_{\text{eff}}+\text{SS}_E)$ and
$\omega^2 = \big(\text{SS}_{\text{eff}} - df_{\text{eff}}\,\text{MS}_E\big) / \big(\text{SS}_T + \text{MS}_E\big)$.
For the factorial condition effect: $(61.633 - 2.155)/(370.8 + 2.155) \approx .159$ by that total-SS form.
The book instead puts $\text{SS}_{\text{eff}}+\text{SS}_E+\text{MS}_E$ in the denominator (a partial form), giving .190.
[@martin_bridgmon]

:::

- **Choosing it:** [choosing a statistical test](../02-inference/choosing-a-statistical-test.qmd) places
  these designs on the map. Two or more categorical factors call for a factorial ANOVA; a group comparison
  with a continuous pre-treatment covariate calls for ANCOVA.
