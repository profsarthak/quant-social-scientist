Censoring, Truncation & Sample Selection: Tobit and Heckman

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source

When the outcome piles up at zero, is cut off at a threshold, or is only seen for people who chose to show it — and why OLS on what you can see quietly bends the line.

OLS assumes you see a random slice of the population. Three common situations break that promise in different ways. The outcome can pile up at a corner (zero hours worked). It can be censored at a threshold (wealth reported only as “over $500,000”). Or some units can be missing altogether, for reasons tied to the outcome itself (wages seen only for people who work). Each needs its own fix: Tobit, censored or truncated regression, and Heckman’s selection correction. All three rest on one idea: a hidden term, the inverse Mills ratio, has been omitted from the regression.

Why it matters

Social-science outcomes are rarely clean continuous variables observed for everyone. Hours worked, charitable giving and spending on alcohol are zero for a large share of people and spread out above that. Survey incomes are top-coded. Durations until re-arrest are cut off when the follow-up period ends. And the most famous case in labour economics: we observe a wage offer only for people who actually take a job. (Wooldridge 2019)

In every one of these cases, running OLS on the data you have gives estimates that are biased and inconsistent, usually shrunk toward zero. The standard error gives no hint of it. (Wooldridge 2019) The fixes are standard maximum-likelihood tools. The harder skill is diagnosis. You need to tell a behavioural corner from a data-collection cut-off, and selection on x (harmless) from selection on y (fatal).

Three different problems that look alike

Wooldridge stresses that these are easy to confuse, and that confusing them leads to the wrong model. (Wooldridge 2019)

Situation What you see Why it happens Tool
Corner solution y = 0 for many units, continuous above Behaviour: many people choose zero Tobit
Censoring x for everyone; y only up to a threshold, beyond it just “at least c” Data collection: top-coding, follow-up ends Censored regression
Truncation Nothing at all (neither y nor x) for units beyond a threshold in y Sampling design: the survey only targets part of the population Truncated regression
Incidental truncation x for everyone; y only when some other choice happens Self-selection: wage seen only if the person works Heckman (Heckit)

The first distinction is the one people most often get wrong. In a Tobit application to married women’s labour supply, “there is no problem with observing hours worked”. Many women simply choose zero hours, and the pile-up at zero is a real feature of the outcome. (Wooldridge 2019) In a censored-regression application the pile-up is an artefact. The true value exists but was not recorded. The two call for different models, and their coefficients mean different things.

When is selection harmless?

Start with the reassuring case, because it is more common than people fear. Suppose the sample is chosen on the explanatory variables. For example, a savings survey covers only adults over 35. OLS is then still unbiased and consistent for the population model. The regression function E(\text{saving}\mid\text{income},\text{age},\text{size}) is the same in any subpopulation defined by those regressors, so you simply have fewer observations. (Wooldridge 2019) Wooldridge calls this exogenous sample selection. Selection that is purely random, or that depends on factors independent of the error, is also harmless. (Wooldridge 2019) The same logic applies to stratified samples. A military pay survey that oversamples women is fine for estimating the gender gap, because the stratification is on a regressor. One that oversamples low earners is not, because the stratification is on the outcome. (Wooldridge 2019)

The dangerous case is selection on the outcome, or endogenous sample selection. Keep only people with wealth below $250,000 and you get biased, inconsistent estimates of the wealth equation. If the sample is based on whether y is above or below a threshold, “bias always occurs in OLS”. (Wooldridge 2019) The rule compresses to one line. Selection is harmless if it is independent of the error u given x, and harmful if it is correlated with u. Selecting on y is the most direct way to correlate with u, because y contains u.

Truncation and censoring flatten the line

Here is why selecting on y biases OLS toward zero. Suppose income rises with education, and the survey only records people earning under $50,000. The high-education, high-income points are precisely the ones cut off, so the top of the cloud is sliced off at the high-education end. The surviving points trace a flatter line than the true one. Hausman and Wise (1977) made this point about a negative-income-tax sample truncated by income. (Wooldridge 2019) Censoring at the same threshold (top-coding) keeps those observations but pushes them down onto the cap. That also flattens the naive line, just less brutally.

Show the figure code 
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from math import erf, sqrt

rng = np.random.default_rng(17)
n = 400
educ = rng.uniform(8, 20, n)
income = -40 + 5.0 * educ + rng.normal(0, 14, n)    # population line: slope 5
cap = 50.0

Phi = np.vectorize(lambda z: 0.5 * (1 + erf(z / sqrt(2))))

def ols(x, y):
    X = np.column_stack([np.ones_like(x), x])
    return np.linalg.lstsq(X, y, rcond=None)[0]

def censored_loglik(p, x, w, c):
    """Censored-normal (right-censored at c) log-likelihood; p = (b0, b1, log sigma)."""
    b0, b1, ls = p
    s = np.exp(ls)
    mu = b0 + b1 * x
    cens = w >= c
    z = (w[~cens] - mu[~cens]) / s
    ll_obs = -0.5 * np.log(2 * np.pi) - ls - 0.5 * z**2          # density part
    ll_cen = np.log(np.clip(1 - Phi((c - mu[cens]) / s), 1e-300, None))  # P(y* >= c)
    return ll_obs.sum() + ll_cen.sum()

def newton(f, p, steps=60, h=1e-4):
    """Newton ascent with finite-difference gradient/Hessian and step halving."""
    p = np.asarray(p, float)
    k = len(p)
    E = np.eye(k) * h
    for _ in range(steps):
        g = np.array([(f(p + E[i]) - f(p - E[i])) / (2 * h) for i in range(k)])
        H = np.array([[(f(p + E[i] + E[j]) - f(p + E[i] - E[j])
                        - f(p - E[i] + E[j]) + f(p - E[i] - E[j])) / (4 * h * h)
                       for j in range(k)] for i in range(k)])
        step = np.linalg.solve(H, g)
        t = 1.0
        while f(p - t * step) < f(p) and t > 1e-4:
            t /= 2
        p = p - t * step
        if np.abs(t * step).max() < 1e-8:
            break
    return p

keep = income < cap
w = np.minimum(income, cap)                                   # top-coded outcome
b_trunc = ols(educ[keep], income[keep])
b_naive = ols(educ, w)
p_mle = newton(lambda p: censored_loglik(p, educ, w, cap), [*b_naive, np.log(14)])

xs = np.linspace(8, 20, 50)
fig, (axT, axC) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(9.4, 4.0), sharey=True)

axT.scatter(educ[keep], income[keep], s=9, color="#b7b6a8", label="sampled")
axT.scatter(educ[~keep], income[~keep], s=9, facecolors="none", edgecolors="#b7b6a8",
            linewidths=0.7, label="never sampled")
axT.plot(xs, -40 + 5 * xs, color="#161613", ls="--", lw=1.6, label="true line (slope 5.0)")
axT.plot(xs, b_trunc[0] + b_trunc[1] * xs, color="#e8531d", lw=2.2,
         label=f"OLS, truncated sample (slope {b_trunc[1]:.1f})")
axT.set_title("Truncation: the top is never seen", fontsize=10)

axC.scatter(educ, w, s=9, color="#b7b6a8", label="recorded (cap = 50)")
axC.plot(xs, -40 + 5 * xs, color="#161613", ls="--", lw=1.6, label="true line (slope 5.0)")
axC.plot(xs, b_naive[0] + b_naive[1] * xs, color="#e8531d", lw=2.2,
         label=f"naive OLS (slope {b_naive[1]:.1f})")
axC.plot(xs, p_mle[0] + p_mle[1] * xs, color="#5e5e54", lw=2.0,
         label=f"censored MLE (slope {p_mle[1]:.1f})")
axC.set_title("Censoring: the top is flattened onto the cap", fontsize=10)

for ax in (axT, axC):
    ax.axhline(cap, color="#8a8a7e", lw=0.9, ls=":")
    ax.set_xlabel("education (years)")
    ax.legend(fontsize=7.2, frameon=False, loc="upper left")
axT.set_ylabel("income ($000s)")
fig.suptitle("Selecting on the outcome bends the regression line toward zero", fontsize=11.5)
fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()

The fix in both cases is to tell the likelihood what happened. Censored regression keeps every unit. It uses the ordinary normal density for uncensored values and, for censored ones, the probability of lying beyond the threshold. Its coefficients are read “just as in a linear regression model under random sampling”. (Wooldridge 2019) Truncated regression renormalises each unit’s density by the probability of having been sampled at all. It recovers consistent estimates, but it can use less information than censored regression, because it has none on the units it never saw. (Wooldridge 2019)

Worked example: time to re-arrest. Wooldridge follows 1,445 inmates released from North Carolina prisons. For 893 of them (≈62%) the follow-up ended before any re-arrest, so their durations are right-censored. (Wooldridge 2019) A censored normal regression for \log(\text{duration}) finds the following:

  • Each additional prior conviction cuts the expected time to re-arrest by almost 14%.
  • A history of alcohol or drug abuse substantially shortens it.
  • The in-prison work programme shows no detectable effect: about 6.3% shorter, with a small t statistic.

(Wooldridge 2019) Then the warning. Run OLS on everyone and treat censored durations as if they were real, and every coefficient is shrunk toward zero. The priors coefficient falls from −.137 to −.059, and alcohol from −.635 to −.262. The signs survive but the magnitudes are “greatly diminished”. (Wooldridge 2019) You could instead drop the censored inmates and fit a truncated regression on the remaining 552, but you would never do that. Knowing that 893 men lasted at least 70–81 months is real information. Censored regression uses it and truncation throws it away. (Wooldridge 2019) (Duration data with censoring is also the home ground of survival analysis, which models the hazard rather than the mean.)

Tobit: when zero is a choice

For a corner-solution outcome the model is a latent index, y^* = x\beta + u with u \sim \mathcal N(0,\sigma^2), and we observe y = \max(0, y^*). (Wooldridge 2019) The mathematics resembles censored regression. The interpretation does not, because y^* usually has no economic meaning. Nobody works “−300 hours”. The quantities you care about are E(y\mid x) and E(y\mid y>0, x), and both are nonlinear in x. (Wooldridge 2019)

Two consequences follow.

  • OLS on the positive observations alone is inconsistent. Among people with y>0, E(y\mid y>0,x) = x\beta + \sigma\lambda(x\beta/\sigma). Here \lambda(\cdot) is the inverse Mills ratio, a ratio of the normal density to the normal CDF. Dropping it means “the inverse Mills ratio is an omitted variable”, and it is correlated with x. (Wooldridge 2019)
  • Tobit coefficients are not marginal effects. The effect of x_j on E(y\mid x) is \beta_j\,\Phi(x\beta/\sigma): the coefficient shrunk by the probability of being above the corner. \sigma is therefore not an “ancillary” nuisance. It sets the magnitudes of every partial effect. (Wooldridge 2019)

Worked example: married women’s hours. In Wooldridge’s MROZ data, 428 of 753 married women worked and 325 worked zero hours. Hours among workers range from 12 to 4,950. (Wooldridge 2019) Tobit and OLS agree on every sign, and it is tempting to compare magnitudes directly. The Tobit educ coefficient is 80.65 against OLS’s 28.76. But that comparison “is not very informative”. (Wooldridge 2019) Rescale Tobit by the average adjustment factor (\approx .589) and a year of education adds about 47.5 hours, still well above OLS’s 28.8. At the average woman (factor \approx .645) it adds about 52 hours. (Wooldridge 2019) The comparison that means something is between partial effects, not raw coefficients.

Heckman: when the outcome is seen only for those who opted in

The subtlest case is incidental truncation. Whether y is observed depends on another outcome. The classic example is the wage offer. Every working-age person faces one, but we only see it for people who work. If the unobservables that make someone take a job (motivation, say) also raise their wage, then workers are not a random draw of wage offers. OLS on workers is then biased for the population wage equation. (Wooldridge 2019) The same problem arises from attrition in panels, where who drops out “may be related to the effectiveness of a program”. (Wooldridge 2019)

Heckman’s two-step correction (the Heckit) models the selection explicitly:

  1. Selection equation. Using all units, fit a probit for whether y is observed (does she work?) on a set of variables z. Compute each unit’s inverse Mills ratio \hat\lambda_i.
  2. Outcome equation. On the selected units only, regress y on x and \hat\lambda_i. The \beta’s are now consistent. The t statistic on \hat\lambda is a simple test for selection bias. (Wooldridge 2019)

The machinery is easy. The hard part is an exclusion restriction, and it is worth dwelling on, because it plays the same role as an instrument. You need at least one variable in z that affects selection but not y. Without one the method still runs, but \hat\lambda is nearly a linear function of x. That produces severe multicollinearity, and “it is extremely difficult, if not impossible, to distinguish sample selection from a misspecified functional form”. (Wooldridge 2019) Heckit without an exclusion restriction relies entirely on the normality assumption.

Worked example: an honest null. For the same 753 married women, Wooldridge models participation with other income, age, and the numbers of young and older children. These are assumed to affect the decision to work but not the wage offer. (Wooldridge 2019) The result is instructive because nothing happens. The coefficient on \hat\lambda has a t statistic of just .239, and the return to education is .108 by OLS versus .109 by Heckit. (Wooldridge 2019) Selection correction is a check that can come back clean. If the test finds no evidence of selection, “there is no reason to continue”. (Wooldridge 2019)

Common pitfalls

WarningWhere this goes wrong

  • Using Tobit for data censoring, or censored regression for a corner solution. Zero hours is behaviour. A top-coded $500,000 is a recording rule. The models look alike but their coefficients answer different questions. Tobit’s are latent-index parameters that need rescaling, while censored regression’s are ordinary regression slopes. (Wooldridge 2019)

  • Reading Tobit coefficients as marginal effects. The effect on E(y\mid x) is \beta_j\Phi(x\beta/\sigma), which is always smaller than \beta_j. Report average partial effects, and do not call \sigma “ancillary”. (Wooldridge 2019)

  • Dropping the zeros, or the censored cases, and running OLS. That selects on y, which guarantees inconsistency. In the recidivism data it shrank every coefficient toward zero. (Wooldridge 2019)

  • Panicking about every non-random sample. Selection on the regressors (an age band, an oversampled demographic group) leaves OLS consistent. Only selection correlated with the error needs a correction. (Wooldridge 2019)

  • Running Heckit with no exclusion restriction. Identification then comes only from the normal functional form. Standard errors balloon, and “selection” becomes indistinguishable from misspecification. Defend a variable that shifts selection but not the outcome, as you would an instrument. (Wooldridge 2019)

  • Trusting the two-step standard errors as printed. The second-step OLS standard errors ignore that \hat\lambda was estimated. Use corrected standard errors (or full MLE) whenever \hat\lambda matters. (Wooldridge 2019)

  • Forgetting that all of these models lean on normality and homoskedasticity. Unlike OLS on a random sample, censored, truncated and Tobit MLEs are inconsistent if the latent error is heteroskedastic or non-normal. Censoring is costly for that reason. (Wooldridge 2019)

Appendix · the mathematics

The inverse Mills ratio. For z\sim\mathcal N(0,1) and any constant c, E(z\mid z>c) = \phi(c)/[1-\Phi(c)]. Writing \lambda(c) = \phi(c)/\Phi(c) and using symmetry, E(z\mid z>-c) = \lambda(c). This is the positive “extra” mean you pick up by looking only above a threshold.

Tobit expectations. With y^* = x\beta+u, u\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2), y=\max(0,y^*):

E(y\mid y>0,x) = x\beta + \sigma\,\lambda(x\beta/\sigma),\qquad E(y\mid x) = \Phi(x\beta/\sigma)\,x\beta + \sigma\,\phi(x\beta/\sigma),

and, remarkably, \partial E(y\mid x)/\partial x_j = \beta_j\,\Phi(x\beta/\sigma). The Tobit log-likelihood for unit i is

\ell_i = \mathbf 1[y_i=0]\log\!\big[1-\Phi(x_i\beta/\sigma)\big] + \mathbf 1[y_i>0]\log\!\Big[\tfrac1\sigma\,\phi\!\big((y_i-x_i\beta)/\sigma\big)\Big].

Censored and truncated likelihoods. Right-censoring at c_i (observe w_i=\min(y_i,c_i)) gives density \tfrac1\sigma\phi((w-x_i\beta)/\sigma) for w<c_i and probability 1-\Phi((c_i-x_i\beta)/\sigma) at w=c_i. This is exactly what the figure maximises. Truncation from above instead renormalises: g(y\mid x_i,c_i) = f(y\mid x_i\beta,\sigma^2)/F(c_i\mid x_i\beta,\sigma^2) for y\le c_i.

Why selection biases OLS. Write the observed-sample regression as s_iy_i = s_ix_i\beta + s_iu_i, where s_i=1 if observed. OLS is consistent if E(s\,x_j u)=0. When s depends only on x (or on noise independent of u), this holds. When s = \mathbf 1[y\le c], s is a function of u itself and it fails.

Heckman’s correction. Outcome y=x\beta+u, selection s=\mathbf 1[z\gamma+v>0] with v\sim\mathcal N(0,1), (u,v) jointly normal with E(u\mid v)=\rho v, and x\subset z. Then

E(y\mid z, s=1) = x\beta + \rho\,\lambda(z\gamma).

Estimate \gamma by probit on everyone, form \hat\lambda_i=\lambda(z_i\hat\gamma), and regress y on (x,\hat\lambda) in the selected sample. H_0:\rho=0 (no selection bias) is the usual t test on \hat\lambda. Without an element of z excluded from x, \hat\lambda is close to linear in x and identification hangs on the normal functional form.

::: {.lead-kernel}
OLS assumes you see a random slice of the population. Three common situations break that promise
in different ways. The outcome can **pile up at a corner** (zero hours worked). It can be **censored**
at a threshold (wealth reported only as "over $500,000"). Or some units can be **missing
altogether**, for reasons tied to the outcome itself (wages seen only for people who work). Each
needs its own fix: **Tobit**, **censored or truncated regression**, and **Heckman's selection
correction**. All three rest on one idea: a hidden term, the *inverse Mills ratio*, has been omitted
from the regression.
:::

## Why it matters

Social-science outcomes are rarely clean continuous variables observed for everyone. Hours worked,
charitable giving and spending on alcohol are **zero for a large share of people** and spread out
above that. Survey incomes are **top-coded**. Durations until re-arrest are **cut off** when the
follow-up period ends. And the most famous case in labour economics: we observe a **wage offer only
for people who actually take a job**. [@wooldridge_intro]

In every one of these cases, running OLS on the data you have gives estimates that are biased and
inconsistent, usually **shrunk toward zero**. The standard error gives no hint of it. [@wooldridge_intro]
The fixes are standard [maximum-likelihood](../02-inference/maximum-likelihood.qmd) tools. The harder
skill is **diagnosis**. You need to tell a *behavioural* corner from a *data-collection* cut-off, and
selection on $x$ (harmless) from selection on $y$ (fatal).

## Three different problems that look alike

Wooldridge stresses that these are easy to confuse, and that confusing them leads to the wrong
model. [@wooldridge_intro]

| Situation | What you see | Why it happens | Tool |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Corner solution** | $y = 0$ for many units, continuous above | *Behaviour*: many people choose zero | **Tobit** |
| **Censoring** | $x$ for everyone; $y$ only up to a threshold, beyond it just "at least $c$" | *Data collection*: top-coding, follow-up ends | **Censored regression** |
| **Truncation** | Nothing at all (neither $y$ nor $x$) for units beyond a threshold in $y$ | *Sampling design*: the survey only targets part of the population | **Truncated regression** |
| **Incidental truncation** | $x$ for everyone; $y$ only when some *other* choice happens | *Self-selection*: wage seen only if the person works | **Heckman (Heckit)** |

The first distinction is the one people most often get wrong. In a Tobit application to married
women's labour supply, "there is no problem with observing hours worked". Many women simply choose
zero hours, and the pile-up at zero is a real feature of the outcome. [@wooldridge_intro] In a
censored-regression application the pile-up is an **artefact**. The true value exists but was not
recorded. The two call for different models, and their coefficients mean different things.

## When is selection harmless?

Start with the reassuring case, because it is more common than people fear. Suppose the sample is
chosen **on the explanatory variables**. For example, a savings survey covers only adults over 35.
OLS is then still unbiased and consistent for the population model. The regression function
$E(\text{saving}\mid\text{income},\text{age},\text{size})$ is the same in any subpopulation defined
by those regressors, so you simply have fewer observations. [@wooldridge_intro] Wooldridge calls this
**exogenous sample selection**. Selection that is purely random, or that depends on factors
independent of the error, is also harmless. [@wooldridge_intro] The same logic applies to stratified
samples. A military pay survey that *oversamples women* is fine for estimating the gender gap,
because the stratification is on a regressor. One that *oversamples low earners* is not, because the
stratification is on the outcome. [@wooldridge_intro]

The dangerous case is **selection on the outcome**, or **endogenous sample selection**. Keep only
people with wealth below $250,000 and you get biased, inconsistent estimates of the wealth equation.
If the sample is based on whether $y$ is above or below a threshold, "bias always occurs in OLS".
[@wooldridge_intro] The rule compresses to one line. **Selection is harmless if it is independent of
the error $u$ given $x$, and harmful if it is correlated with $u$.** Selecting on $y$ is the most
direct way to correlate with $u$, because $y$ contains $u$.

## Truncation and censoring flatten the line

Here is why selecting on $y$ biases OLS toward zero. Suppose income rises with education, and the
survey only records people earning under $50,000. The high-education, high-income points are
precisely the ones cut off, so the top of the cloud is sliced off at the high-education end. The
surviving points trace a **flatter line** than the true one. Hausman and Wise (1977) made this point
about a negative-income-tax sample truncated by income. [@wooldridge_intro] **Censoring** at the same
threshold (top-coding) keeps those observations but pushes them *down onto the cap*. That also
flattens the naive line, just less brutally.

```{python}
#| label: fig-censor-truncate
#| fig-cap: "Simulated income on education with true slope 5 (dashed ink line). Left, truncation: units earning above the $50k cap are never sampled (hollow points). OLS on the surviving points (orange) is visibly flatter. Right, censoring (top-coding): units above the cap are recorded at the cap. Naive OLS on the recorded values (orange) is again flattened, but censored-normal maximum likelihood (grey), which uses only the knowledge that those values are 'at least $50k', recovers the true slope. Everything is estimated by hand with numpy."
#| code-fold: true
#| code-summary: "Show the figure code"

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from math import erf, sqrt

rng = np.random.default_rng(17)
n = 400
educ = rng.uniform(8, 20, n)
income = -40 + 5.0 * educ + rng.normal(0, 14, n)    # population line: slope 5
cap = 50.0

Phi = np.vectorize(lambda z: 0.5 * (1 + erf(z / sqrt(2))))

def ols(x, y):
    X = np.column_stack([np.ones_like(x), x])
    return np.linalg.lstsq(X, y, rcond=None)[0]

def censored_loglik(p, x, w, c):
    """Censored-normal (right-censored at c) log-likelihood; p = (b0, b1, log sigma)."""
    b0, b1, ls = p
    s = np.exp(ls)
    mu = b0 + b1 * x
    cens = w >= c
    z = (w[~cens] - mu[~cens]) / s
    ll_obs = -0.5 * np.log(2 * np.pi) - ls - 0.5 * z**2          # density part
    ll_cen = np.log(np.clip(1 - Phi((c - mu[cens]) / s), 1e-300, None))  # P(y* >= c)
    return ll_obs.sum() + ll_cen.sum()

def newton(f, p, steps=60, h=1e-4):
    """Newton ascent with finite-difference gradient/Hessian and step halving."""
    p = np.asarray(p, float)
    k = len(p)
    E = np.eye(k) * h
    for _ in range(steps):
        g = np.array([(f(p + E[i]) - f(p - E[i])) / (2 * h) for i in range(k)])
        H = np.array([[(f(p + E[i] + E[j]) - f(p + E[i] - E[j])
                        - f(p - E[i] + E[j]) + f(p - E[i] - E[j])) / (4 * h * h)
                       for j in range(k)] for i in range(k)])
        step = np.linalg.solve(H, g)
        t = 1.0
        while f(p - t * step) < f(p) and t > 1e-4:
            t /= 2
        p = p - t * step
        if np.abs(t * step).max() < 1e-8:
            break
    return p

keep = income < cap
w = np.minimum(income, cap)                                   # top-coded outcome
b_trunc = ols(educ[keep], income[keep])
b_naive = ols(educ, w)
p_mle = newton(lambda p: censored_loglik(p, educ, w, cap), [*b_naive, np.log(14)])

xs = np.linspace(8, 20, 50)
fig, (axT, axC) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(9.4, 4.0), sharey=True)

axT.scatter(educ[keep], income[keep], s=9, color="#b7b6a8", label="sampled")
axT.scatter(educ[~keep], income[~keep], s=9, facecolors="none", edgecolors="#b7b6a8",
            linewidths=0.7, label="never sampled")
axT.plot(xs, -40 + 5 * xs, color="#161613", ls="--", lw=1.6, label="true line (slope 5.0)")
axT.plot(xs, b_trunc[0] + b_trunc[1] * xs, color="#e8531d", lw=2.2,
         label=f"OLS, truncated sample (slope {b_trunc[1]:.1f})")
axT.set_title("Truncation: the top is never seen", fontsize=10)

axC.scatter(educ, w, s=9, color="#b7b6a8", label="recorded (cap = 50)")
axC.plot(xs, -40 + 5 * xs, color="#161613", ls="--", lw=1.6, label="true line (slope 5.0)")
axC.plot(xs, b_naive[0] + b_naive[1] * xs, color="#e8531d", lw=2.2,
         label=f"naive OLS (slope {b_naive[1]:.1f})")
axC.plot(xs, p_mle[0] + p_mle[1] * xs, color="#5e5e54", lw=2.0,
         label=f"censored MLE (slope {p_mle[1]:.1f})")
axC.set_title("Censoring: the top is flattened onto the cap", fontsize=10)

for ax in (axT, axC):
    ax.axhline(cap, color="#8a8a7e", lw=0.9, ls=":")
    ax.set_xlabel("education (years)")
    ax.legend(fontsize=7.2, frameon=False, loc="upper left")
axT.set_ylabel("income ($000s)")
fig.suptitle("Selecting on the outcome bends the regression line toward zero", fontsize=11.5)
fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

The fix in both cases is to **tell the likelihood what happened**. **Censored regression** keeps
every unit. It uses the ordinary normal density for uncensored values and, for censored ones, the
*probability* of lying beyond the threshold. Its coefficients are read "just as in a linear
regression model under random sampling". [@wooldridge_intro] **Truncated regression** renormalises
each unit's density by the probability of having been sampled at all. It recovers consistent
estimates, but it can use less information than censored regression, because it has none on the
units it never saw. [@wooldridge_intro]

**Worked example: time to re-arrest.** Wooldridge follows 1,445 inmates released from North
Carolina prisons. For **893 of them (≈62%)** the follow-up ended before any re-arrest, so their
durations are right-censored. [@wooldridge_intro] A censored normal regression for $\log(\text{duration})$
finds the following:

- Each additional **prior conviction cuts the expected time to re-arrest by almost 14%**.
- A history of alcohol or drug abuse substantially shortens it.
- The in-prison **work programme shows no detectable effect**: about 6.3% *shorter*, with a small t
  statistic.

[@wooldridge_intro] Then the warning. Run OLS on everyone and treat censored durations as if they
were real, and every coefficient is **shrunk toward zero**. The *priors* coefficient falls from
−.137 to −.059, and *alcohol* from −.635 to −.262. The signs survive but the magnitudes are
"greatly diminished". [@wooldridge_intro] You could instead drop the censored inmates and fit a
truncated regression on the remaining 552, but you would never do that. Knowing that 893 men lasted
*at least* 70–81 months is real information. Censored regression uses it and truncation throws it
away. [@wooldridge_intro] (Duration data with censoring is also the home ground of
[survival analysis](survival-analysis.qmd), which models the hazard rather than the mean.)

## Tobit: when zero is a choice

For a **corner-solution** outcome the model is a latent index,
$y^* = x\beta + u$ with $u \sim \mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)$, and we observe $y = \max(0, y^*)$.
[@wooldridge_intro] The mathematics resembles censored regression. The *interpretation* does not,
because $y^*$ usually has no economic meaning. Nobody works "−300 hours". The quantities you care
about are $E(y\mid x)$ and $E(y\mid y>0, x)$, and both are **nonlinear** in $x$. [@wooldridge_intro]

Two consequences follow.

- **OLS on the positive observations alone is inconsistent.** Among people with $y>0$,
  $E(y\mid y>0,x) = x\beta + \sigma\lambda(x\beta/\sigma)$. Here $\lambda(\cdot)$ is the **inverse
  Mills ratio**, a ratio of the normal density to the normal CDF. Dropping it means "the inverse Mills
  ratio is an omitted variable", and it is correlated with $x$. [@wooldridge_intro]
- **Tobit coefficients are not marginal effects.** The effect of $x_j$ on $E(y\mid x)$ is
  $\beta_j\,\Phi(x\beta/\sigma)$: the coefficient shrunk by the probability of being above the
  corner. $\sigma$ is therefore *not* an "ancillary" nuisance. It sets the magnitudes of every
  partial effect. [@wooldridge_intro]

**Worked example: married women's hours.** In Wooldridge's MROZ data, 428 of 753 married women
worked and **325 worked zero hours**. Hours among workers range from 12 to 4,950. [@wooldridge_intro]
Tobit and OLS agree on every sign, and it is tempting to compare magnitudes directly. The Tobit
*educ* coefficient is 80.65 against OLS's 28.76. But that comparison "is not very informative".
[@wooldridge_intro] Rescale Tobit by the average adjustment factor ($\approx .589$) and a year of
education adds about **47.5 hours**, still well above OLS's 28.8. At the average woman (factor
$\approx .645$) it adds about 52 hours. [@wooldridge_intro] The comparison that means something is
between *partial effects*, not raw coefficients.

## Heckman: when the outcome is seen only for those who opted in

The subtlest case is **incidental truncation**. Whether $y$ is observed depends on *another* outcome.
The classic example is the **wage offer**. Every working-age person faces one, but we only see it
for people who work. If the unobservables that make someone take a job (motivation, say) also raise
their wage, then workers are not a random draw of wage offers. OLS on workers is then biased for the
population wage equation. [@wooldridge_intro] The same problem arises from **attrition in panels**,
where who drops out "may be related to the effectiveness of a program". [@wooldridge_intro]

Heckman's two-step correction (the **Heckit**) models the selection explicitly:

1. **Selection equation.** Using *all* units, fit a [probit](limited-dependent-variables.qmd) for
   whether $y$ is observed (does she work?) on a set of variables $z$. Compute each unit's
   **inverse Mills ratio** $\hat\lambda_i$.
2. **Outcome equation.** On the *selected* units only, regress $y$ on $x$ **and** $\hat\lambda_i$.
   The $\beta$'s are now consistent. The t statistic on $\hat\lambda$ is a simple **test for
   selection bias**. [@wooldridge_intro]

The machinery is easy. The hard part is an **exclusion restriction**, and it is worth dwelling on,
because it plays the same role as an [instrument](../05-causal-inference/instrumental-variables.qmd).
You need at least one variable in $z$ that **affects selection but not $y$**. Without one the
method still runs, but $\hat\lambda$ is nearly a linear function of $x$. That produces severe
multicollinearity, and "it is extremely difficult, if not impossible, to distinguish sample
selection from a misspecified functional form". [@wooldridge_intro] Heckit without an exclusion
restriction relies entirely on the normality assumption.

**Worked example: an honest null.** For the same 753 married women, Wooldridge models participation
with *other income, age, and the numbers of young and older children*. These are **assumed** to
affect the decision to work but not the wage offer. [@wooldridge_intro] The result is instructive
because nothing happens. The coefficient on $\hat\lambda$ has a t statistic of just **.239**, and the
return to education is **.108 by OLS versus .109 by Heckit**. [@wooldridge_intro] Selection
correction is a *check* that can come back clean. If the test finds no evidence of selection,
"there is no reason to continue". [@wooldridge_intro]

## Common pitfalls

::: {.callout-warning title="Where this goes wrong"}

- **Using Tobit for data censoring, or censored regression for a corner solution.** Zero hours is
  *behaviour*. A top-coded $500,000 is a *recording rule*. The models look alike but their
  coefficients answer different questions. Tobit's are latent-index parameters that need rescaling,
  while censored regression's are ordinary regression slopes. [@wooldridge_intro]

- **Reading Tobit coefficients as marginal effects.** The effect on $E(y\mid x)$ is
  $\beta_j\Phi(x\beta/\sigma)$, which is always smaller than $\beta_j$. Report average partial
  effects, and do not call $\sigma$ "ancillary". [@wooldridge_intro]

- **Dropping the zeros, or the censored cases, and running OLS.** That selects on $y$, which
  guarantees inconsistency. In the recidivism data it shrank every coefficient toward zero.
  [@wooldridge_intro]

- **Panicking about every non-random sample.** Selection on the regressors (an age band, an
  oversampled demographic group) leaves OLS consistent. Only selection correlated with the error
  needs a correction. [@wooldridge_intro]

- **Running Heckit with no exclusion restriction.** Identification then comes only from the normal
  functional form. Standard errors balloon, and "selection" becomes indistinguishable from
  misspecification. Defend a variable that shifts selection but not the outcome, as you would an
  instrument. [@wooldridge_intro]

- **Trusting the two-step standard errors as printed.** The second-step OLS standard errors ignore
  that $\hat\lambda$ was estimated. Use corrected standard errors (or full MLE) whenever $\hat\lambda$
  matters. [@wooldridge_intro]

- **Forgetting that all of these models lean on normality and homoskedasticity.** Unlike OLS on a
  random sample, censored, truncated and Tobit MLEs are *inconsistent* if the latent error is
  heteroskedastic or non-normal. Censoring is costly for that reason. [@wooldridge_intro]

:::

## Appendix · the mathematics {.unnumbered}

::: {.math-appendix}

**The inverse Mills ratio.** For $z\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$ and any constant $c$,
$E(z\mid z>c) = \phi(c)/[1-\Phi(c)]$. Writing $\lambda(c) = \phi(c)/\Phi(c)$ and using symmetry,
$E(z\mid z>-c) = \lambda(c)$. This is the positive "extra" mean you pick up by looking only above a
threshold.

**Tobit expectations.** With $y^* = x\beta+u$, $u\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)$, $y=\max(0,y^*)$:

$$
E(y\mid y>0,x) = x\beta + \sigma\,\lambda(x\beta/\sigma),\qquad
E(y\mid x) = \Phi(x\beta/\sigma)\,x\beta + \sigma\,\phi(x\beta/\sigma),
$$

and, remarkably, $\partial E(y\mid x)/\partial x_j = \beta_j\,\Phi(x\beta/\sigma)$. The Tobit
log-likelihood for unit $i$ is

$$
\ell_i = \mathbf 1[y_i=0]\log\!\big[1-\Phi(x_i\beta/\sigma)\big]
+ \mathbf 1[y_i>0]\log\!\Big[\tfrac1\sigma\,\phi\!\big((y_i-x_i\beta)/\sigma\big)\Big].
$$

**Censored and truncated likelihoods.** Right-censoring at $c_i$ (observe $w_i=\min(y_i,c_i)$) gives
density $\tfrac1\sigma\phi((w-x_i\beta)/\sigma)$ for $w<c_i$ and probability
$1-\Phi((c_i-x_i\beta)/\sigma)$ at $w=c_i$. This is exactly what the figure maximises. Truncation from
above instead renormalises: $g(y\mid x_i,c_i) = f(y\mid x_i\beta,\sigma^2)/F(c_i\mid x_i\beta,\sigma^2)$
for $y\le c_i$.

**Why selection biases OLS.** Write the observed-sample regression as $s_iy_i = s_ix_i\beta + s_iu_i$,
where $s_i=1$ if observed. OLS is consistent if $E(s\,x_j u)=0$. When $s$ depends only on $x$ (or on
noise independent of $u$), this holds. When $s = \mathbf 1[y\le c]$, $s$ is a function of $u$ itself
and it fails.

**Heckman's correction.** Outcome $y=x\beta+u$, selection $s=\mathbf 1[z\gamma+v>0]$ with
$v\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$, $(u,v)$ jointly normal with $E(u\mid v)=\rho v$, and $x\subset z$. Then

$$
E(y\mid z, s=1) = x\beta + \rho\,\lambda(z\gamma).
$$

Estimate $\gamma$ by probit on everyone, form $\hat\lambda_i=\lambda(z_i\hat\gamma)$, and regress $y$ on
$(x,\hat\lambda)$ in the selected sample. $H_0:\rho=0$ (no selection bias) is the usual t test on
$\hat\lambda$. Without an element of $z$ excluded from $x$, $\hat\lambda$ is close to linear in $x$ and
identification hangs on the normal functional form.

:::
