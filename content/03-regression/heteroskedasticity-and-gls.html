Heteroskedasticity, Robust Errors & GLS

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source

When the error variance isn’t constant, OLS still hits the right answer on average — but its standard errors lie, and every t-test built on them is wrong. Two cures: be agnostic (robust SEs) or model it (GLS).

The Gauss–Markov ideal assumes constant error variance. Real data rarely oblige — spending varies more among the rich, test scores more in big schools. Under this heteroskedasticity, OLS coefficients stay unbiased, but the usual standard errors are biased, so your t- and F-tests are invalid. The fixes split into two philosophies: robust standard errors that work without knowing the variance pattern, and (feasible) GLS that models it to recover efficiency.

Why it matters

Heteroskedasticity is the rule, not the exception, in cross-sectional social data — and its danger is subtle: nothing looks wrong (the point estimates are fine), but the inference silently misleads, and the problem does not vanish as the sample grows (Wooldridge 2019). Knowing how to detect it and which correction to use is basic hygiene for any regression you’ll defend.

What it is, and what it costs

Heteroskedasticity means the error variance, conditional on the regressors, is not constant: \operatorname{Var}(u\mid X)=\sigma^2(X) depends on X (Wooldridge 2019). The consequences are specific (Wooldridge 2019):

  • Still unbiased and consistent. These properties need only \mathbb{E}[u\mid X]=0, not constant variance — so OLS gets the right answer on average (Wooldridge 2019).
  • No longer BLUE / efficient. OLS loses its minimum-variance crown among linear unbiased estimators (Wooldridge 2019).
  • Invalid inference. The usual SE formulas are biased, so t, F, and confidence intervals are wrong — and stay wrong even in large samples (Wooldridge 2019).

Robust standard errors: the agnostic fix

The everyday response is heteroskedasticity-robust (White / Huber–Eicker) standard errors (Wooldridge 2019). They adjust the SEs to be valid in large samples regardless of the form of the heteroskedasticity — without your having to model \operatorname{Var}(u\mid X) at all (Wooldridge 2019). This is why “robust SEs” is the modern default. One caveat from Wooldridge: in small samples robust SEs can be more misleading than conventional ones, because they carry higher sampling variability themselves (Wooldridge 2019).

Robust standard errors are biased too: HC0 to HC3 and the max rule

Angrist and Pischke explain where Wooldridge’s caveat comes from, and what to do about it (Angrist and Pischke 2009). Their starting point: both the conventional and the robust variance estimators are biased in finite samples. The conventional one has an easy fix that every package already applies. The robust one does not, and when heteroskedasticity is mild, it can be more biased than the conventional one, “perhaps a lot more”.

Why residuals understate errors. Robust SEs plug each squared residual \hat e_i^2 in for the unknown error variance of observation i. But fitted residuals are smaller, on average, than the true errors, because the regression line has been pulled toward each data point. How hard it is pulled depends on the point’s leverage h_{ii}, which measures how much observation i influences its own fitted value. An observation with an unusual x, far from the mean, has high leverage. Leverages lie between 0 and 1 and add up to the number of coefficients k. Under constant variance, E[\hat e_i^2]=\sigma^2(1-h_{ii}) (Angrist and Pischke 2009):

  • For the conventional estimator, these shortfalls are averaged over all observations, and the average works out to exactly (N-k)/N. Dividing by N-k instead of N removes the bias.
  • For the robust estimator, each residual is weighted by its own x, and the high-leverage points are exactly the ones whose residuals are shrunk most. With a single regressor and constant variance, the robust estimator’s bias is worse than the conventional one’s whenever leverage varies across observations at all (Angrist and Pischke 2009). Unless the heteroskedasticity is strong, robust SEs are biased downward (a result Angrist and Pischke credit to Chesher and Jewitt).

The four HC versions. MacKinnon and White’s family, as Angrist and Pischke present it, differs only in how each squared residual is scaled up before going into the sandwich (Angrist and Pischke 2009):

Version Uses, for observation i Idea
HC0 (White) \hat e_i^2 The original; consistent, but biased in small samples
HC1 \frac{N}{N-k}\,\hat e_i^2 The conventional degrees-of-freedom fix, applied to everyone
HC2 \hat e_i^2/(1-h_{ii}) Undo each residual’s own leverage shrinkage; unbiased if errors are actually homoskedastic
HC3 \hat e_i^2/(1-h_{ii})^2 Approximates the jackknife (refit leaving out one point at a time); larger again

In the applications Angrist and Pischke have seen, the standard errors grow down the list, though that is not a theorem.

A test case: a two-group comparison. Regress y on a single treatment dummy. The slope is the difference in group means, and the HC versions differ only in how they handle the two groups’ variances. HC0 divides each group’s sum of squares by N_j, HC2 by N_j-1 (the usual unbiased variance), HC3 by more still, while the conventional estimator pools the groups. In a balanced design (half treated) the conventional estimator equals HC1 and all five barely differ. The trouble is in unbalanced designs, where the small group’s observations have high leverage (Angrist and Pischke 2009). Angrist and Pischke simulate the worst case: N=30, only 3 treated (h_{ii}=1/3 for them, 1/27 for the controls), treated errors with SD 1, controls with SD 0.5 (lots of heteroskedasticity), 0.85 (a little) or 1 (none), 25,000 replications. The results (Angrist and Pischke 2009):

  • Lots of heteroskedasticity. The true SD of the estimate is 0.586. Conventional SEs average 0.331, about half of that. HC0 to HC2 are better but still too small. Only HC3 (0.636) is not. Every test still rejects a true null far more often than 5%: about 26–28% of the time with conventional SEs and 12–13% even with HC3 (the range covers comparing t with the t or the normal distribution).
  • The SEs are themselves noisy. Robust SEs vary much more from sample to sample than conventional ones, and the bias corrections make it worse: HC3’s SD is about 50% above HC0’s. A robust SE can be too small just by chance.
  • Little heteroskedasticity. Conventional SEs are now only about 15% too low, and they reject less often (8–10%) than any robust version (10–22%). HC2 and HC3 are larger on average than conventional SEs, but are too small “by accident” often enough to raise the rejection rate.
  • No heteroskedasticity. Conventional SEs are right (5–6%). HC0 rejects about 20% of the time.

Even HC2, which is unbiased for the variance, gives standard errors that are too small on average (0.52 vs 0.59 in the first case), because the square root of a noisy, unbiased variance estimate is biased downward (Angrist and Pischke 2009).

The rule of thumb. Angrist and Pischke treat a robust SE that comes out below the conventional one as a red flag, probably bias or bad luck. Their recommendation is to report the larger of the conventional and a robust standard error. Taking the maximum cuts off the too-small robust values, which reduces both the bias and the variability (Angrist and Pischke 2009). With a little heteroskedasticity, using the t-distribution, max(HC2, conventional) rejects 5.7% of the time and max(HC3, conventional) 4.5%. With a lot, the rule is still much better than the robust SEs alone. The cost shows up when there is no heteroskedasticity, where the conventional SE was already best and the maximum makes intervals a little too wide. Angrist and Pischke accept that cost on purpose: underestimating precision only sends you looking for more data, but overestimating it can lead you to a wrong substantive conclusion (Angrist and Pischke 2009).

They also point out when this matters outside textbook examples. In Angrist and Lang’s study of busing, 3,000 students sat in 56 schools, and the regressor of interest varied only by school, so the effective sample was close to 56. There, HC1 standard errors came out below the conventional ones (Angrist and Pischke 2009). Whenever the regressor varies only at the level of a group, the number of groups, not the number of people, is the real sample size. This is the bridge to clustered standard errors.

TipWhat about the bootstrap?

Angrist and Pischke discuss bootstrapping as an alternative. The wild bootstrap keeps each observation’s x and flips the sign of its residual at random, so each observation keeps its own residual variance (Angrist and Pischke 2009). Bootstrapping the t-statistic can in principle give better tests in small samples. Their practical view is that applied researchers want a standard error to print under each coefficient, so they would rather use analytic corrections like HC2 and HC3, together with the max rule (Angrist and Pischke 2009). See the bootstrap for the resampling idea itself.

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
beta1_true = 1.0
n, S = 60, 3000

def fit(x, y):
    X = np.column_stack([np.ones(len(x)), x])
    XtX_inv = np.linalg.inv(X.T @ X)
    b = XtX_inv @ X.T @ y
    e = y - X @ b
    # classical SE
    s2 = (e @ e) / (len(x) - 2)
    se_cl = np.sqrt((s2 * XtX_inv)[1, 1])
    # White (HC0) robust SE: sandwich
    meat = X.T @ (e[:, None]**2 * X)
    Vrob = XtX_inv @ meat @ XtX_inv
    se_rob = np.sqrt(Vrob[1, 1])
    return b[1], se_cl, se_rob

cover_cl = cover_rob = 0
for _ in range(S):
    x = rng.uniform(0, 5, n)
    y = 1.0 + beta1_true * x + (0.2 + 1.2 * np.abs(x - 2.5)) * rng.normal(size=n)   # heteroskedastic
    b1, se_cl, se_rob = fit(x, y)
    cover_cl += abs(b1 - beta1_true) <= 1.96 * se_cl
    cover_rob += abs(b1 - beta1_true) <= 1.96 * se_rob
cover_cl, cover_rob = 100*cover_cl/S, 100*cover_rob/S

fig = plt.figure(figsize=(9.6, 8.2))
gs = fig.add_gridspec(2, 2, width_ratios=[1.3, 1], height_ratios=[1, 1.05])

Formal tests

Two standard tests of the null H_0: homoskedasticity (Wooldridge 2019):

  • Breusch–Pagan. Regress the squared OLS residuals on the regressors: \hat u^2 = \delta_0 + \delta_1 x_1 + \cdots + \delta_k x_k + v. The null is \delta_1=\cdots=\delta_k=0 (variance unrelated to X); a significant F/LM rejects it (Wooldridge 2019).
  • White test. More general — regress \hat u^2 on the regressors, their squares, and all cross-products. A parsimonious version uses just the fitted values and their square, \hat u^2 = \delta_0 + \delta_1\hat y + \delta_2\hat y^2 + \text{error} (Wooldridge 2019).

WLS and feasible GLS: model the variance

When you can model the variance, generalized least squares (GLS) restores efficiency (Wooldridge 2019):

  • Weighted least squares (WLS). If \operatorname{Var}(u_i\mid x_i)=\sigma^2 h_i, divide every variable (including the intercept) by \sqrt{h_i} (Wooldridge 2019). This down-weights high-variance observations, making the transformed errors homoskedastic and the estimator BLUE (Wooldridge 2019).
  • Feasible GLS (FGLS). When h_i is unknown, estimate it — typically model the variance as an exponential (to stay positive): regress \log(\hat u^2) on the regressors, exponentiate the fitted values to get \hat h_i = \exp(\hat g_i), and use those as WLS weights (Wooldridge 2019).
TipThe efficiency–robustness trade-off

FGLS is asymptotically more efficient than OLS if the variance model is correct. But if the variance model is wrong, WLS can be less efficient than OLS — so OLS with robust SEs is the “safer,” more interpretable default (Wooldridge 2019). Reach for FGLS when you genuinely know the variance form (or efficiency is critical); otherwise, robust SEs.

The time-series cousin: serial correlation

In time series, errors are often serially correlated — today’s error related to yesterday’s (Wooldridge 2019). The parallels to heteroskedasticity are close: with strictly exogenous regressors OLS stays unbiased/consistent but inefficient, and the usual SEs and tests are invalid (Wooldridge 2019). Detection and cures (Wooldridge 2019; Ashley 2012):

  • Durbin–Watson — the classic AR(1) test; ≈ 2 means no correlation, near 0 means strong positive. Often inconclusive, and invalid with a lagged dependent variable (Wooldridge 2019).
  • Breusch–Godfrey (LM) — more general: regress OLS residuals on the regressors and lagged residuals \hat u_{t-1},\dots,\hat u_{t-q} (Wooldridge 2019).
  • Newey–West (HAC) SEs — robust to both heteroskedasticity and arbitrary serial correlation in large samples (Wooldridge 2019).
  • Cochrane–Orcutt / Prais–Winsten — FGLS via a quasi-differencing transform that removes AR(1), given an estimate of \rho (Wooldridge 2019).
  • Dynamic respecification. Ashley’s emphasis: serial correlation is often a symptom of missing dynamics — adding lagged terms can “cure” it by specifying the model properly (Ashley 2012).

Common pitfalls

WarningWhere this goes wrong
  • Trusting default OLS SEs on cross-sectional data. Assume heteroskedasticity; use robust SEs by default. (Wooldridge 2019)
  • Using robust SEs in a tiny sample. They have high variability themselves and can mislead at small n. (Wooldridge 2019)
  • Reading “robust” as “correct”. HC0 and HC1 are biased downward in small or unbalanced samples, and every HC version over-rejects when a few observations carry high leverage. If a robust SE comes out smaller than the conventional one, treat it as a red flag and report the larger of the two. (Angrist and Pischke 2009)
  • Counting people when the regressor varies by group. If treatment varies only across 56 schools, the effective sample is close to 56, not 3,000; small-sample SE problems apply even in “big” data. (Angrist and Pischke 2009)
  • Applying WLS with a wrong variance model. You can end up less efficient than plain OLS — and biased if the weights are endogenous. (Wooldridge 2019)
  • Treating serial correlation as just a SE problem. It often signals a misspecified dynamic model; fix the specification, don’t only patch the SEs. (Ashley 2012)
  • Reading Durbin–Watson with a lagged dependent variable. It’s invalid there — use Breusch–Godfrey. (Wooldridge 2019)

Appendix · the mathematics

The sandwich (White) estimator. OLS gives \hat\beta=(X'X)^{-1}X'y with \operatorname{Var}(\hat\beta\mid X)=(X'X)^{-1}\big(X'\,\Omega\,X\big)(X'X)^{-1}, where \Omega=\operatorname{diag}(\sigma_i^2). Under homoskedasticity \Omega=\sigma^2 I and this collapses to \sigma^2(X'X)^{-1}. White’s insight: you don’t need each \sigma_i^2 — replace the unknown “meat” X'\Omega X=\sum_i \sigma_i^2 x_i x_i' with \sum_i \hat u_i^2\, x_i x_i' (HC0), which is consistent for it. That is exactly the meat = X.T @ (e**2 * X) line in the code; the bread is (X'X)^{-1} on either side. (Where (X'X)^{-1} and the sandwich come from, built up from a three-observation example: OLS in matrix form.)

Why residuals are too small: leverage (Angrist and Pischke 2009). Write \hat{\mathbf e}=M\mathbf e with M=I-H and H=X(X'X)^{-1}X'. With fixed regressors and \operatorname{Var}(e_i)=\psi_i,

E[\hat e_i^2]=\psi_i-2\psi_ih_{ii}+\sum_j h_{ij}^2\psi_j ,\qquad h_{ii}=x_i'(X'X)^{-1}x_i .

Under homoskedasticity (\psi_i=\sigma^2) and since \sum_jh_{ij}^2=h_{ii} (H is idempotent), this is \sigma^2(1-h_{ii}). Because \operatorname{tr}H=k, the average is \sigma^2(N-k)/N: hence the N-k divisor. In a bivariate regression with demeaned \tilde x, h_{ii}=\tilde x_i^2/\sum\tilde x_j^2 and

E[\hat V_{\text{conv}}]=\frac{\sigma^2}{Ns_x^2}\Big(1-\frac1N\Big),\qquad E[\hat V_{\text{HC0}}]=\frac{\sigma^2}{Ns_x^2}\Big(1-\sum_i h_{ii}^2\Big),

and \sum h_{ii}^2>1/N by Jensen’s inequality unless every h_{ii}=1/N. So under constant variance the robust estimator is the more biased one.

The two-group case. With a dummy regressor, S_j^2=\sum_{d_i=j}(y_i-\bar y_j)^2, h_{ii}=1/N_j and r=N_1/N:

\hat V_{\text{conv}}=\frac{1}{Nr(1-r)}\cdot\frac{S_0^2+S_1^2}{N-2},\quad \text{HC0}=\frac{S_0^2}{N_0^2}+\frac{S_1^2}{N_1^2},\quad \text{HC2}=\frac{S_0^2}{N_0(N_0-1)}+\frac{S_1^2}{N_1(N_1-1)},\quad \text{HC3}=\frac{S_0^2}{(N_0-1)^2}+\frac{S_1^2}{(N_1-1)^2},

with HC1 =\frac{N}{N-2}HC0. HC2 is exactly unbiased here, with or without heteroskedasticity; HC3 is therefore too large. In the figure’s design (N_0=27, N_1=3, equal variances) the true variance is \sigma^2(1/27+1/3)\approx0.370\sigma^2, while E[\text{HC0}]=\sigma^2(26/27^2+2/9)\approx0.258\sigma^2: on average HC0 captures only about 70% of the variance (our arithmetic).

Why WLS is BLUE. Dividing through by \sqrt{h_i} gives errors u_i/\sqrt{h_i} with variance \sigma^2 h_i/h_i=\sigma^2 — constant. Gauss–Markov now applies to the transformed model, so WLS is BLUE. Equivalently, GLS is \hat\beta_{\text{GLS}}=(X'\Omega^{-1}X)^{-1}X'\Omega^{-1}y, the variance-weighted normal equations.

Newey–West. Generalises the sandwich meat to include lagged cross-products \sum_{\ell} w_\ell \sum_t \hat u_t \hat u_{t-\ell}(x_t x_{t-\ell}' + x_{t-\ell}x_t') with Bartlett weights w_\ell tapering to zero — making the SE robust to serial correlation up to the chosen lag.

::: {.lead-kernel}
The [Gauss–Markov](gauss-markov-assumptions.qmd) ideal assumes **constant error variance**. Real data rarely
oblige — spending varies more among the rich, test scores more in big schools. Under this
**heteroskedasticity**, OLS coefficients stay **unbiased**, but the usual **standard errors are biased**, so
your $t$- and $F$-tests are invalid. The fixes split into two philosophies: **robust standard errors** that
work *without* knowing the variance pattern, and **(feasible) GLS** that *models* it to recover efficiency.
:::

## Why it matters

Heteroskedasticity is the rule, not the exception, in cross-sectional social data — and its danger is
**subtle**: nothing looks wrong (the point estimates are fine), but the inference silently misleads, and
**the problem does not vanish as the sample grows** [@wooldridge_intro]. Knowing how to detect it and which
correction to use is basic hygiene for any regression you'll defend.

## What it is, and what it costs

**Heteroskedasticity** means the error variance, conditional on the regressors, is **not constant**:
$\operatorname{Var}(u\mid X)=\sigma^2(X)$ depends on $X$ [@wooldridge_intro]. The consequences are specific
[@wooldridge_intro]:

- **Still unbiased and consistent.** These properties need only $\mathbb{E}[u\mid X]=0$, not constant
  variance — so OLS gets the right answer on average [@wooldridge_intro].
- **No longer BLUE / efficient.** OLS loses its minimum-variance crown among linear unbiased estimators
  [@wooldridge_intro].
- **Invalid inference.** The usual SE formulas are **biased**, so $t$, $F$, and confidence intervals are
  **wrong — and stay wrong even in large samples** [@wooldridge_intro].

## Robust standard errors: the agnostic fix

The everyday response is **heteroskedasticity-robust** (**White / Huber–Eicker**) standard errors
[@wooldridge_intro]. They adjust the SEs to be **valid in large samples regardless of the form** of the
heteroskedasticity — **without** your having to model $\operatorname{Var}(u\mid X)$ at all
[@wooldridge_intro]. This is why "robust SEs" is the modern default. One caveat from Wooldridge: in **small
samples** robust SEs can be **more misleading than conventional ones**, because they carry **higher sampling
variability** themselves [@wooldridge_intro].

## Robust standard errors are biased too: HC0 to HC3 and the max rule {#sec-hc-bias}

Angrist and Pischke explain where Wooldridge's caveat comes from, and what to do about it
[@angrist_mostly_harmless]. Their starting point: **both** the conventional and the robust variance
estimators are biased in finite samples. The conventional one has an easy fix that every package already
applies. The robust one does not, and when heteroskedasticity is mild, it can be **more** biased than the
conventional one, "perhaps a lot more".

**Why residuals understate errors.** Robust SEs plug each squared residual $\hat e_i^2$ in for the unknown
error variance of observation $i$. But fitted residuals are smaller, on average, than the true errors,
because the regression line has been pulled toward each data point. How hard it is pulled depends on the
point's **leverage** $h_{ii}$, which measures how much observation $i$ influences its own fitted value. An
observation with an unusual $x$, far from the mean, has high leverage. Leverages lie between 0 and 1 and add
up to the number of coefficients $k$. Under constant variance, $E[\hat e_i^2]=\sigma^2(1-h_{ii})$
[@angrist_mostly_harmless]:

- For the **conventional** estimator, these shortfalls are averaged over all observations, and the average
  works out to exactly $(N-k)/N$. Dividing by $N-k$ instead of $N$ removes the bias.
- For the **robust** estimator, each residual is weighted by its own $x$, and the high-leverage points are
  exactly the ones whose residuals are shrunk most. With a single regressor and constant variance, the
  robust estimator's bias is worse than the conventional one's whenever leverage varies across
  observations at all [@angrist_mostly_harmless]. Unless the heteroskedasticity is strong, robust SEs are
  biased **downward** (a result Angrist and Pischke credit to Chesher and Jewitt).

**The four HC versions.** MacKinnon and White's family, as Angrist and Pischke present it, differs only in
how each squared residual is scaled up before going into the sandwich [@angrist_mostly_harmless]:

| Version | Uses, for observation $i$ | Idea |
|---|---|---|
| **HC0** (White) | $\hat e_i^2$ | The original; consistent, but biased in small samples |
| **HC1** | $\frac{N}{N-k}\,\hat e_i^2$ | The conventional degrees-of-freedom fix, applied to everyone |
| **HC2** | $\hat e_i^2/(1-h_{ii})$ | Undo each residual's own leverage shrinkage; unbiased if errors are actually homoskedastic |
| **HC3** | $\hat e_i^2/(1-h_{ii})^2$ | Approximates the jackknife (refit leaving out one point at a time); larger again |

In the applications Angrist and Pischke have seen, the standard errors grow down the list, though that
is not a theorem.

**A test case: a two-group comparison.** Regress $y$ on a single treatment dummy. The slope is the
difference in group means, and the HC versions differ only in how they handle the two groups' variances.
HC0 divides each group's sum of squares by $N_j$, HC2 by $N_j-1$ (the usual unbiased variance), HC3 by more
still, while the conventional estimator **pools** the groups. In a **balanced** design (half treated) the
conventional estimator equals HC1 and all five barely differ. The trouble is in **unbalanced** designs,
where the small group's observations have high leverage [@angrist_mostly_harmless]. Angrist and Pischke
simulate the worst case: $N=30$, only 3 treated ($h_{ii}=1/3$ for them, $1/27$ for the controls), treated
errors with SD 1, controls with SD 0.5 (lots of heteroskedasticity), 0.85 (a little) or 1 (none), 25,000
replications. The results [@angrist_mostly_harmless]:

- **Lots of heteroskedasticity.** The true SD of the estimate is 0.586. Conventional SEs average 0.331, about
  half of that. HC0 to HC2 are better but still too small. Only HC3 (0.636) is not. Every test still rejects
  a true null far more often than 5%: about 26–28% of the time with conventional SEs and 12–13% even with
  HC3 (the range covers comparing $t$ with the $t$ or the normal distribution).
- **The SEs are themselves noisy.** Robust SEs vary much more from sample to sample than conventional ones,
  and the bias corrections make it worse: HC3's SD is about 50% above HC0's. A robust SE can be too small
  just by chance.
- **Little heteroskedasticity.** Conventional SEs are now only about 15% too low, and they reject *less*
  often (8–10%) than any robust version (10–22%). HC2 and HC3 are larger on average than conventional SEs,
  but are too small "by accident" often enough to raise the rejection rate.
- **No heteroskedasticity.** Conventional SEs are right (5–6%). HC0 rejects about 20% of the time.

Even HC2, which is unbiased for the *variance*, gives standard errors that are too small on average (0.52 vs
0.59 in the first case), because the square root of a noisy, unbiased variance estimate is biased downward
[@angrist_mostly_harmless].

**The rule of thumb.** Angrist and Pischke treat a robust SE that comes out *below* the conventional one as
a red flag, probably bias or bad luck. Their recommendation is to report **the larger of the conventional and
a robust standard error**. Taking the maximum cuts off the too-small robust values, which reduces both the
bias and the variability [@angrist_mostly_harmless]. With a little heteroskedasticity, using the
$t$-distribution, max(HC2, conventional) rejects 5.7% of the time and max(HC3, conventional) 4.5%. With a lot, the rule is still much
better than the robust SEs alone. The cost shows up when there is no heteroskedasticity, where the
conventional SE was already best and the maximum makes intervals a little too wide. Angrist and Pischke
accept that cost on purpose: underestimating precision only sends you looking for more data, but
overestimating it can lead you to a wrong substantive conclusion [@angrist_mostly_harmless].

They also point out when this matters outside textbook examples. In Angrist and Lang's study of busing,
3,000 students sat in 56 schools, and the regressor of interest varied only by school, so the effective
sample was close to 56. There, HC1 standard errors came out *below* the conventional ones
[@angrist_mostly_harmless]. Whenever the regressor varies only at the level of a group, the number of groups,
not the number of people, is the real sample size. This is the bridge to
[clustered standard errors](../05-causal-inference/difference-in-differences.qmd).

::: {.callout-tip title="What about the bootstrap?"}
Angrist and Pischke discuss bootstrapping as an alternative. The **wild bootstrap** keeps each observation's
$x$ and flips the sign of its residual at random, so each observation keeps its own residual variance
[@angrist_mostly_harmless]. Bootstrapping the $t$-statistic can in principle give better tests in small
samples. Their practical view is that applied researchers want a standard error to print under each
coefficient, so they would rather use analytic corrections like HC2 and HC3, together with the max rule
[@angrist_mostly_harmless]. See [the bootstrap](../02-inference/the-bootstrap.qmd) for the resampling idea
itself.
:::

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

n, S = 60, 3000

def fit(x, y):
    X = np.column_stack([np.ones(len(x)), x])
    XtX_inv = np.linalg.inv(X.T @ X)
    b = XtX_inv @ X.T @ y
    e = y - X @ b
    # classical SE
    s2 = (e @ e) / (len(x) - 2)
    se_cl = np.sqrt((s2 * XtX_inv)[1, 1])
    # White (HC0) robust SE: sandwich
    meat = X.T @ (e[:, None]**2 * X)
    Vrob = XtX_inv @ meat @ XtX_inv
    se_rob = np.sqrt(Vrob[1, 1])
    return b[1], se_cl, se_rob

## Formal tests

Two standard tests of the null $H_0$: homoskedasticity [@wooldridge_intro]:

- **Breusch–Pagan.** Regress the **squared OLS residuals** on the regressors:
  $\hat u^2 = \delta_0 + \delta_1 x_1 + \cdots + \delta_k x_k + v$. The null is
  $\delta_1=\cdots=\delta_k=0$ (variance unrelated to $X$); a significant $F$/LM rejects it
  [@wooldridge_intro].
- **White test.** More general — regress $\hat u^2$ on the regressors, **their squares, and all
  cross-products**. A parsimonious version uses just the fitted values and their square,
  $\hat u^2 = \delta_0 + \delta_1\hat y + \delta_2\hat y^2 + \text{error}$ [@wooldridge_intro].

## WLS and feasible GLS: model the variance

When you can *model* the variance, **generalized least squares (GLS)** restores efficiency
[@wooldridge_intro]:

- **Weighted least squares (WLS).** If $\operatorname{Var}(u_i\mid x_i)=\sigma^2 h_i$, **divide every variable
  (including the intercept) by $\sqrt{h_i}$** [@wooldridge_intro]. This **down-weights high-variance
  observations**, making the transformed errors homoskedastic and the estimator **BLUE** [@wooldridge_intro].
- **Feasible GLS (FGLS).** When $h_i$ is unknown, **estimate** it — typically model the variance as an
  exponential (to stay positive): regress $\log(\hat u^2)$ on the regressors, exponentiate the fitted values
  to get $\hat h_i = \exp(\hat g_i)$, and use those as WLS weights [@wooldridge_intro].

::: {.callout-tip title="The efficiency–robustness trade-off"}
FGLS is **asymptotically more efficient than OLS *if* the variance model is correct**. But if the variance
model is **wrong**, WLS can be **less** efficient than OLS — so **OLS with robust SEs is the "safer,"
more interpretable default** [@wooldridge_intro]. Reach for FGLS when you genuinely know the variance form
(or efficiency is critical); otherwise, robust SEs.
:::

## The time-series cousin: serial correlation

In time series, errors are often **serially correlated** — today's error related to yesterday's
[@wooldridge_intro]. The parallels to heteroskedasticity are close: with strictly exogenous regressors OLS
stays **unbiased/consistent but inefficient**, and the **usual SEs and tests are invalid**
[@wooldridge_intro]. Detection and cures [@wooldridge_intro; @ashley_econometrics]:

- **Durbin–Watson** — the classic AR(1) test; ≈ 2 means no correlation, near 0 means strong positive. Often
  inconclusive, and invalid with a lagged dependent variable [@wooldridge_intro].
- **Breusch–Godfrey (LM)** — more general: regress OLS residuals on the regressors **and lagged residuals**
  $\hat u_{t-1},\dots,\hat u_{t-q}$ [@wooldridge_intro].
- **Newey–West (HAC)** SEs — robust to **both** heteroskedasticity and **arbitrary serial correlation** in
  large samples [@wooldridge_intro].
- **Cochrane–Orcutt / Prais–Winsten** — FGLS via a **quasi-differencing** transform that removes AR(1),
  given an estimate of $\rho$ [@wooldridge_intro].
- **Dynamic respecification.** Ashley's emphasis: serial correlation is often a **symptom of missing
  dynamics** — adding lagged terms can **"cure"** it by specifying the model properly
  [@ashley_econometrics].

## Common pitfalls

::: {.callout-warning title="Where this goes wrong"}
- **Trusting default OLS SEs on cross-sectional data.** Assume heteroskedasticity; use robust SEs by default.
  [@wooldridge_intro]
- **Using robust SEs in a tiny sample.** They have high variability themselves and can mislead at small $n$.
  [@wooldridge_intro]
- **Reading "robust" as "correct".** HC0 and HC1 are biased downward in small or unbalanced samples, and
  every HC version over-rejects when a few observations carry high leverage. If a robust SE comes out
  *smaller* than the conventional one, treat it as a red flag and report the larger of the two.
  [@angrist_mostly_harmless]
- **Counting people when the regressor varies by group.** If treatment varies only across 56 schools, the
  effective sample is close to 56, not 3,000; small-sample SE problems apply even in "big" data.
  [@angrist_mostly_harmless]
- **Applying WLS with a wrong variance model.** You can end up *less* efficient than plain OLS — and biased
  if the weights are endogenous. [@wooldridge_intro]
- **Treating serial correlation as just a SE problem.** It often signals a misspecified dynamic model; fix
  the specification, don't only patch the SEs. [@ashley_econometrics]
- **Reading Durbin–Watson with a lagged dependent variable.** It's invalid there — use Breusch–Godfrey.
  [@wooldridge_intro]
:::

## Appendix · the mathematics {.unnumbered}

::: {.math-appendix}

**The sandwich (White) estimator.** OLS gives $\hat\beta=(X'X)^{-1}X'y$ with
$\operatorname{Var}(\hat\beta\mid X)=(X'X)^{-1}\big(X'\,\Omega\,X\big)(X'X)^{-1}$, where
$\Omega=\operatorname{diag}(\sigma_i^2)$. Under homoskedasticity $\Omega=\sigma^2 I$ and this collapses to
$\sigma^2(X'X)^{-1}$. White's insight: you don't need each $\sigma_i^2$ — replace the unknown "meat"
$X'\Omega X=\sum_i \sigma_i^2 x_i x_i'$ with $\sum_i \hat u_i^2\, x_i x_i'$ (HC0), which is consistent for it.
That is exactly the `meat = X.T @ (e**2 * X)` line in the code; the **bread** is $(X'X)^{-1}$ on either side.
(Where $(X'X)^{-1}$ and the sandwich come from, built up from a three-observation example:
[OLS in matrix form](../../appendices/math/ols-in-matrix-form.qmd#sec-three-moves).)

**Why residuals are too small: leverage** [@angrist_mostly_harmless]. Write $\hat{\mathbf e}=M\mathbf e$
with $M=I-H$ and $H=X(X'X)^{-1}X'$. With fixed regressors and $\operatorname{Var}(e_i)=\psi_i$,

$$
E[\hat e_i^2]=\psi_i-2\psi_ih_{ii}+\sum_j h_{ij}^2\psi_j ,\qquad h_{ii}=x_i'(X'X)^{-1}x_i .
$$

Under homoskedasticity ($\psi_i=\sigma^2$) and since $\sum_jh_{ij}^2=h_{ii}$ ($H$ is idempotent), this is
$\sigma^2(1-h_{ii})$. Because $\operatorname{tr}H=k$, the average is $\sigma^2(N-k)/N$: hence the $N-k$
divisor. In a bivariate regression with demeaned $\tilde x$, $h_{ii}=\tilde x_i^2/\sum\tilde x_j^2$ and

$$
E[\hat V_{\text{conv}}]=\frac{\sigma^2}{Ns_x^2}\Big(1-\frac1N\Big),\qquad
E[\hat V_{\text{HC0}}]=\frac{\sigma^2}{Ns_x^2}\Big(1-\sum_i h_{ii}^2\Big),
$$

and $\sum h_{ii}^2>1/N$ by Jensen's inequality unless every $h_{ii}=1/N$. So under constant variance the
robust estimator is the more biased one.

**The two-group case.** With a dummy regressor, $S_j^2=\sum_{d_i=j}(y_i-\bar y_j)^2$, $h_{ii}=1/N_j$ and
$r=N_1/N$:

$$
\hat V_{\text{conv}}=\frac{1}{Nr(1-r)}\cdot\frac{S_0^2+S_1^2}{N-2},\quad
\text{HC0}=\frac{S_0^2}{N_0^2}+\frac{S_1^2}{N_1^2},\quad
\text{HC2}=\frac{S_0^2}{N_0(N_0-1)}+\frac{S_1^2}{N_1(N_1-1)},\quad
\text{HC3}=\frac{S_0^2}{(N_0-1)^2}+\frac{S_1^2}{(N_1-1)^2},
$$

with HC1 $=\frac{N}{N-2}$HC0. HC2 is exactly unbiased here, with or without heteroskedasticity; HC3 is
therefore too large. In the figure's design ($N_0=27$, $N_1=3$, equal variances) the true variance is
$\sigma^2(1/27+1/3)\approx0.370\sigma^2$, while $E[\text{HC0}]=\sigma^2(26/27^2+2/9)\approx0.258\sigma^2$: on
average HC0 captures only about 70% of the variance (our arithmetic).

**Why WLS is BLUE.** Dividing through by $\sqrt{h_i}$ gives errors $u_i/\sqrt{h_i}$ with variance
$\sigma^2 h_i/h_i=\sigma^2$ — constant. Gauss–Markov now applies to the transformed model, so WLS is BLUE.
Equivalently, GLS is $\hat\beta_{\text{GLS}}=(X'\Omega^{-1}X)^{-1}X'\Omega^{-1}y$, the variance-weighted
normal equations.

**Newey–West.** Generalises the sandwich meat to include lagged cross-products
$\sum_{\ell} w_\ell \sum_t \hat u_t \hat u_{t-\ell}(x_t x_{t-\ell}' + x_{t-\ell}x_t')$ with Bartlett weights
$w_\ell$ tapering to zero — making the SE robust to serial correlation up to the chosen lag.

:::
