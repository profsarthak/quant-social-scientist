Hurdle Models: When Zero Has Two Meanings

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source

A pile of zeros can mean “never would” or “not this time”. Tobit treats them as one; a hurdle model lets each have its own explanation, and lets one variable push the two in opposite directions.

When most people give nothing, the zeros come from two places: people who would never give, and people who might give but didn’t this time. A hurdle model gives each source its own equation. One equation decides whether someone is a potential giver, the other decides how much they give. That lets a variable make giving less likely and also make gifts larger, a pattern a single-equation model averages away.

Why it matters

In a dictator game, one player splits a sum of money with a stranger who has no say. Most dictators give nothing, and in a public goods game many people contribute nothing to the common pot. In both games zero is the selfish, Nash-equilibrium prediction, so the data pile up there. (Moffatt 2016) The same pile appears in household spending on cigarettes or meat, in loan defaults, and in hours worked. (Moffatt 2016; Wooldridge 2019)

The standard tool for a pile at zero is Tobit. Tobit says every zero is a corner solution: the person “wanted” to give a negative amount and the floor at zero stopped them. Moffatt notes that many experimental economists are uneasy with this. A selfish dictator does not want to give -\$3. They are a different kind of person, a zero type, and they would give nothing whatever the treatment. (Moffatt 2016) If that is how the data arise, then asking “what raises giving?” is really two questions. What makes someone a potential giver at all? And what makes a potential giver give more? Those questions can have different answers, so they need two equations.

One process or two?

Tobit’s hidden assumption is that the process that makes a contribution positive is the same process that sets its size. If a treatment strongly raises the amount people give, Tobit must also predict that it makes people more likely to give anything. Nothing guarantees this in advance. (Moffatt 2016)

Moffatt gives an example. Let the outcome be a person’s income from crime. Most values are zero, we hope. White-collar workers are probably less likely than blue-collar workers to commit crime at all. But white-collar criminals probably earn more from it when they do. One variable then pushes the two parts of the outcome in opposite directions. A model with one index cannot represent that. (Moffatt 2016)

Wooldridge gives a quick way to check for the problem in ordinary data. Fit a probit for “is y positive?” and compare its coefficients with the Tobit coefficients divided by \hat\sigma. If Tobit is right, the two should be roughly the same. In his married women’s hours data they are close but not identical. For small children the probit coefficient is .868 and the rescaled Tobit coefficient is .797. That suggests young children matter a little more for the decision to work at all than for how many hours a working mother chooses, and Tobit averages the two effects together. A coefficient that is significant with opposite signs in the two models would be a clear warning. (Wooldridge 2019) When Tobit fails this check, Wooldridge points to models in which P(y=0\mid x) and E(y\mid y>0,x) have separate parameters. (Wooldridge 2019) Hurdle models are the best-known family of these.

Two hurdles

The double hurdle model is due to Cragg (1971). To give a positive amount, a subject must clear two hurdles. (Moffatt 2016)

  1. First hurdle: are you a potential contributor? People who fall here are the zero types, the “selfish” dictators or the “free riders”. They give zero, and nothing in the experiment changes that.
  2. Second hurdle: do you contribute this time, and how much? Potential contributors then behave like a Tobit. Their circumstances or treatment can still leave them at a corner (a censored zero) or carry them to a positive amount.

Each hurdle has its own regression. A variable can appear in one, the other, or both, and it can have opposite signs in the two. (Moffatt 2016) An observed zero now has two possible explanations: a zero type, or a potential contributor at the corner. The model keeps track of the probability of each.

The family has some simpler relatives, and the names are confusing, so it helps to list them. (Moffatt 2016)

Model Where zeros come from What it adds to Tobit
Tobit only corner solutions —
p-Tobit (Deaton & Irish, 1984) corners, plus a fixed share 1-p of “abstainers” one number: the share of never-contributors
Double hurdle (Cragg, 1971) corners, plus zero types whose share depends on covariates a whole first-hurdle equation
Single hurdle only zero types: anyone who clears hurdle 1 gives a positive amount “first-hurdle dominance” (Jones, 1989); second hurdle is a truncated regression

The p-Tobit is a double hurdle whose first equation has only an intercept. Moffatt estimates it that way, then converts the intercept \hat\alpha_0 into the share \hat p = \Phi(\hat\alpha_0), using the delta method for its standard error. (Moffatt 2016)

Tip“Two-part model” is not the same thing

Some studies fit a probit for zero vs positive and a separate truncated regression for the positive amounts, and report the pair as “double hurdle” estimates. Moffatt calls that a two-part model. He keeps the hurdle label for a model that estimates both equations jointly, by full-information maximum likelihood. (Moffatt 2016) Wooldridge’s textbook uses “hurdle or two-part models” for the whole family, so check which one an author means. (Wooldridge 2019) The two-part fit is still useful: it is where you get starting values for the full model. (Moffatt 2016)

Panel data: a type is for the whole experiment

Experiments usually record the same subject many times: several rounds, or several decisions. That makes the hurdle model sharper, because a type cannot change between rounds. The first hurdle is decided once per subject, and if a subject falls there, every observation on them is zero. The second hurdle runs round by round, with a subject-level random effect u_i for people who are generally generous or generally stingy. (Moffatt 2016)

Panel data also rules out the single hurdle. Under first-hurdle dominance, a potential contributor would have to give a positive amount in every round, and a zero type gives zero in every round. No subject could mix zeros and positive amounts, yet most real panels are full of such subjects. The panel double hurdle allows mixing, because a potential contributor can still hit the corner in some rounds. (Moffatt 2016)

Moffatt adds three refinements, each answering a practical worry. (Moffatt 2016)

  • Upper censoring. In public goods games many subjects give their whole endowment. The second hurdle can be censored at the top as well as at zero.
  • A tremble. As stated, the model is too strict. A subject who gives zero in 19 rounds out of 20 can never be classed as a free rider. A tremble parameter \omega allows a lapse of concentration on any single task. When it happens, the choice is random (uniform between zero and the maximum). The near-always-zero subject can then be a free rider who slipped once.
  • Dependence. The subject’s unobserved tendency to clear the first hurdle can be correlated (by \rho) with their unobserved generosity in the second. In a single cross-section \rho is hard to identify (Smith, 2003). Moffatt says panel data let it be estimated “quite precisely”, though in his own simulation (below) its interval is still very wide.

Who is a zero type? Ask Bayes

The model never tells you which subjects are zero types. It gives a posterior probability of being one, using Bayes’ rule after estimation. The prior is the first-hurdle probability. The evidence is the subject’s whole record of choices. (Moffatt 2016) This is the same reasoning about types as in structural estimation, with only two types.

The tremble changes the answer a lot. Without a tremble, one positive contribution proves a subject is not a zero type: their posterior drops to exactly zero. With a tremble, an occasional positive amount is weak evidence, and a subject who nearly always gives zero can keep a high posterior. In Moffatt’s simulation (n = 100 subjects, T = 20 tasks, true tremble 0.04), every subject with fewer than five positive contributions came out as very likely a zero type. (Moffatt 2016) The figure re-runs that simulation and asks what happens if the tremble is left out.

Show the figure code (simulation + Bayes by quadrature, numpy only) 
import math
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(971156)

# Moffatt's simulation design (Experimetrics §11.5.9)
n, T = 100, 20
a0, a1 = -1.5, 2.0            # first hurdle:  d* = a0 + a1 z + e1
b0, b1 = -5.0, 10.0           # second hurdle: y** = b0 + b1 x + su*u + se*e2
su, se, rho, w, ymax = 2.5, 5.0, 0.4, 0.04, 10.0

z = rng.uniform(0, 1, n)                       # subject characteristic
u = rng.normal(size=n)                         # subject random effect (standardised)
e1 = rho * u + np.sqrt(1 - rho**2) * rng.normal(size=n)
d = (a0 + a1 * z + e1 > 0).astype(int)         # 1 = potential contributor
x = rng.uniform(0, 1, (n, T))                  # task characteristic
y_latent = b0 + b1 * x + su * u[:, None] + se * rng.normal(size=(n, T))
y = np.minimum(np.maximum(y_latent, 0) * d[:, None], ymax)
tremble = rng.uniform(size=(n, T)) < w
y[tremble] = ymax * rng.uniform(size=tremble.sum())

Phi = np.vectorize(lambda v: 0.5 * (1 + math.erf(v / math.sqrt(2))))
phi = lambda v: np.exp(-v * v / 2) / math.sqrt(2 * math.pi)
nodes, wts = np.polynomial.hermite_e.hermegauss(40)   # integrate over u ~ N(0,1)
wts = wts / wts.sum()

n_pos = (y > 0).sum(axis=1)

def posterior_zero_type(om):
    """P(zero type | all T choices), Bayes' rule at the true parameters."""
    L0 = (om / ymax) ** n_pos * (1 - om) ** (T - n_pos)    # record if a zero type
    num, den = np.zeros(n), np.zeros(n)
    for v, wt in zip(nodes, wts):
        m = b0 + b1 * x + su * v
        p = np.where(y == 0, (1 - om) * (1 - Phi(m / se)),
            np.where(y >= ymax, (1 - om) * (1 - Phi((ymax - m) / se)),
                     (1 - om) * phi((y - m) / se) / se + om / ymax))
        L1 = p.prod(axis=1)                                 # record if a contributor
        p1 = Phi((a0 + a1 * z + rho * v) / np.sqrt(1 - rho**2))
        num += wt * (1 - p1) * L0
        den += wt * (p1 * L1 + (1 - p1) * L0)
    return num / den

post_tremble = posterior_zero_type(w)
post_strict = posterior_zero_type(0.0)

fig, (axL, axR) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(10, 4.3),
                               gridspec_kw={"width_ratios": [1, 1.25]})

# Left: where the zeros come from

censored_zeros = ((y == 0) & (d[:, None] == 1)).sum()

        label=f"zero type ({zero_type_zeros})")
axL.bar(-0.75, censored_zeros, width=0.9, bottom=zero_type_zeros, color="#b7b6a8",
        edgecolor="#f4f3ec", label=f"censored zero, potential contributor ({censored_zeros})")
axL.bar(edges[:-1] + 0.5, counts, width=0.95, color="#5e5e54", edgecolor="#f4f3ec",
        label=f"positive amount ({pos.size})")
axL.set_xticks([-0.75, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10])
axL.set_xticklabels(["0", "2", "4", "6", "8", "10"])
axL.set_xlabel("amount contributed in a task")

axR.scatter(n_pos + jit, post_tremble, s=26, facecolors="none", edgecolors="#e8531d",

The figure uses the true parameters, so it shows the classification logic and not estimation error. Moffatt estimates the same model by maximum simulated likelihood and recovers every true value inside its 95% interval. The intervals differ a lot in width. The tremble is pinned down tightly (0.035, interval 0.026–0.045). The dependence parameter \rho is not: true value 0.4, estimate 0.14, interval −0.85 to 1.14. (Moffatt 2016) That interval covers almost every possible correlation, and its upper end goes past 1, which a correlation cannot reach. It is a symmetric Wald interval on a bounded parameter. So “estimated quite precisely” is not supported by this simulation.

Worked example: earned income and giving

Erkal et al. (2011) asked whether people with more income give more. Subjects in groups of four did a real-effort task and were paid by rank: $60, $45, $30 or $15. Each then chose a transfer to each of the other three, knowing everyone’s earnings and knowing that only one transfer would be carried out. (Moffatt 2016) That gives 108 dictators making 3 decisions each, a natural panel. Of the 324 decisions, 264 (81%) were zero. (Moffatt 2016)

Two simple tables already show the pattern. (Moffatt 2016)

Rank (earnings) % of dictators who gave at least once Mean received by a receiver of this rank
1 ($60) 26% $0.16
2 ($45) 67% $0.20
3 ($30) 37% $0.70
4 ($15) 22% $1.60
All 38% $0.67

The top earners were less likely to give than the second-ranked, about as unlikely as the poorest. Gifts went steeply toward the poor: the lowest earners received ten times as much as the highest. The model’s design follows from what each variable can be. The dictator’s own rank is a fixed feature of the subject, so it can enter the first hurdle. The receiver’s rank changes from one decision to the next, so it can only enter the second. (Moffatt 2016)

The panel hurdle estimates (with dependence) confirm the main finding. Being ranked second rather than first raises the chance of being a giving type (coefficient 0.99, s.e. 0.41). Among giving types, gifts rise steadily as the receiver’s rank falls (0.61, 3.97, 6.94 relative to the top earner). (Moffatt 2016) A tremble added nothing here and was dropped, and \rho was not significant (−0.30, s.e. 0.40). Moffatt’s practical advice is to take starting values from the two-part model. Even then the full model may not converge in one go, so maximise in stages: fix some parameters first, then free them one at a time. (Moffatt 2016)

The gender result is what a hurdle model is for. Men were less likely to be giving types (−0.58 in the first hurdle). Men who did give gave more (+1.89 in the second). Andreoni & Vesterlund (2001) had already found this pattern in dictator giving. (Moffatt 2016) A Mann–Whitney test on the amounts finds no gender difference at all (z = 0.495, p = 0.62). The two effects cancel in the raw data and only separate when the two decisions are modelled apart. (Moffatt 2016) (The two gender coefficients are about 1.6 standard errors from zero. Moffatt’s stars, labelled p<0.05, are best read as one-sided tests.)

Is it income, or who earns it? Rank in this experiment was earned. Perhaps high income makes people stingy, or perhaps self-interested people work harder and end up ranked first. Erkal et al. ran a third treatment in which earnings were assigned by luck (56 dictators). There, none of the own-earnings dummies in the first hurdle was significant. When income is random, its level does not change the propensity to give. The original pattern came from self-selection of self-interested people into the top rank. (Moffatt 2016) The model separates the two decisions, but it took a randomised treatment to explain the result.

Worked example: house money and free riders

Clark (2002) tested the house money effect: do people behave differently with money the experimenter gave them than with their own? In his public goods game 150 subjects played ten rounds in groups of five, with groups reshuffled each round and an 80-token endowment. Half used house money and half their own. (Moffatt 2016) Clark’s own tests used session averages, which left only ten observations, and found nothing. Harrison (2007) re-analysed the data at the individual level and found that house money changed the share of free riders. (Moffatt 2016) That is a first-hurdle question.

A simple count points the same way. Twenty subjects never contributed: 13 of the 75 own-money subjects (17.3%) and 7 of the 75 house-money subjects (9.3%). The difference is not significant (χ² p = 0.150, Fisher’s exact p = 0.229), and both tests assume subjects in a session act independently. (Moffatt 2016) In the panel hurdle model, the second hurdle behaves as expected. Desired contributions fall by about 2.5–3 tokens a round. They rise with what others gave last round, but less and less as that amount grows, which is evidence of reciprocity. (For round 1, where there is no “last round”, Moffatt assumes subjects expect 66 tokens. He chose the value by grid search and says the house-money result does not depend on it.) (Moffatt 2016)

The house-money coefficient in the first hurdle is about 0.34–0.39 in every model without a tremble, and none of these is significant. Only the model with a tremble gives a significant effect (0.50, s.e. 0.25). That model also fits best by AIC (9,379 against about 9,430), and it estimates a large tremble (\hat\omega = 0.09). (Moffatt 2016) The reason is the posterior. Adding the tremble lets a few subjects who contributed only occasionally count partly as free riders, and those subjects are mostly in the own-money group. Those few subjects decide whether the study finds a house money effect. (Moffatt 2016) That is worth knowing whichever way you read it. The best-fitting model finds the effect. But the finding depends on one modelling choice, and it comes from a handful of subjects whose type the data cannot settle.

Common pitfalls

WarningWhere this goes wrong

  • Assuming every zero is a corner solution. Tobit has one route to zero. If some people would never participate, Tobit mixes “whether” and “how much” into one coefficient. Compare probit coefficients with Tobit’s \beta/\sigma as a first check. (Wooldridge 2019; Moffatt 2016)

  • Calling a two-part model a double hurdle. A probit plus a separate truncated regression is a two-part model. A hurdle model estimates both equations jointly. Say which one you fitted. (Moffatt 2016)

  • Putting task variables in the first hurdle. In a panel, type is fixed for the whole experiment, so the first hurdle can only contain subject characteristics, such as gender or earned rank. Features of the task, such as the receiver’s rank or the round number, belong in the second. (Moffatt 2016)

  • Concluding “no effect” from a test on raw amounts. The Mann–Whitney test found no gender gap (p = 0.62) because two real, opposite effects cancelled. Opposite signs across the two hurdles are the most interesting results these models produce. (Moffatt 2016)

  • Reading the posterior as a verdict. A subject’s zero-type probability depends on the model. Leave out the tremble and one stray contribution “proves” someone is not a free rider; include it and the same person may be classed as one. In the house-money data that single choice decides whether the effect is significant. Report results with and without it. (Moffatt 2016)

  • Reading first-hurdle effects causally when the variable was not randomised. Earned rank predicted giving type, but the luck treatment showed that the cause was self-selection, not income. (Moffatt 2016)

Appendix · the mathematics

Tobit, for reference. With y_i^* = x_i'\beta + \epsilon_i, \epsilon_i\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2) and y_i=\max(y_i^*,0), \ln L = \sum_i \mathbf 1[y_i=0]\ln\Phi\!\Big(\!-\tfrac{x_i'\beta}{\sigma}\Big) + \mathbf 1[y_i>0]\ln\Big[\tfrac1\sigma\phi\Big(\tfrac{y_i-x_i'\beta}{\sigma}\Big)\Big].

p-Tobit. A share 1-p never contributes: \ln L = \sum_i \mathbf 1[y_i=0]\ln\Big[1-p\,\Phi\Big(\tfrac{x_i'\beta}{\sigma}\Big)\Big] + \mathbf 1[y_i>0]\ln\Big[p\,\tfrac1\sigma\phi\Big(\tfrac{y_i-x_i'\beta}{\sigma}\Big)\Big].

Double hurdle (Cragg). First hurdle d_i^* = z_i'\alpha + \epsilon_i, d_i=\mathbf 1[d_i^*>0]; second hurdle y_i^{**} = x_i'\beta + u_i, y_i^*=\max(y_i^{**},0); observed y_i=d_i y_i^*, with (\epsilon_i,u_i) independent normals of variances 1 and \sigma^2. Then \ln L = \sum_i \mathbf 1[y_i=0]\ln\Big[1-\Phi(z_i'\alpha)\,\Phi\Big(\tfrac{x_i'\beta}{\sigma}\Big)\Big] + \mathbf 1[y_i>0]\ln\Big[\Phi(z_i'\alpha)\,\tfrac1\sigma\phi\Big(\tfrac{y_i-x_i'\beta}{\sigma}\Big)\Big]. A zero has probability 1-P(\text{pass 1})P(\text{pass 2}): the L-shaped region of the joint density of (d^*,y^{**}). With z_i'\alpha=\alpha_0 only, this is the p-Tobit with p=\Phi(\alpha_0), and the delta method gives \operatorname{se}(\hat p)\approx\phi(\hat\alpha_0)\operatorname{se}(\hat\alpha_0). The single hurdle replaces censoring in the second hurdle with truncation: positive outcomes have density \Phi(z'\alpha)\,\sigma^{-1}\phi(\cdot)/\Phi(x'\beta/\sigma), and every zero comes from hurdle 1.

Panel hurdle. Subject i, tasks t=1,\dots,T: d_i=\mathbf 1[z_i'\alpha+\epsilon_{1i}>0] once; y_{it}^{**}=x_{it}'\beta+u_i+\epsilon_{2it}, u_i\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma_u^2), \epsilon_{2it}\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2); y_{it}=d_i\max(y_{it}^{**},0). Conditional on u_i, L_i\mid d_i{=}1,u_i = \prod_{t}\Big[1-\Phi\Big(\tfrac{x_{it}'\beta+u_i}{\sigma}\Big)\Big]^{\mathbf 1[y_{it}=0]} \Big[\tfrac1\sigma\phi\Big(\tfrac{y_{it}-x_{it}'\beta-u_i}{\sigma}\Big)\Big]^{\mathbf 1[y_{it}>0]}, \qquad L_i\mid d_i{=}0 = \mathbf 1\Big[\textstyle\sum_t y_{it}=0\Big], and L_i = \int\Big\{\Phi(z_i'\alpha)\,(L_i\mid d_i{=}1,u) + [1-\Phi(z_i'\alpha)]\,(L_i\mid d_i{=}0)\Big\}f(u)\,du, \qquad \ln L=\sum_i\ln L_i . Upper censoring at y_{\max} multiplies in [1-\Phi((y_{\max}-x_{it}'\beta-u_i)/\sigma)]^{\mathbf 1[y_{it}=y_{\max}]}. Tremble \omega: each zero term picks up a factor (1-\omega), each positive term becomes (1-\omega)\sigma^{-1}\phi(\cdot)+\omega/y_{\max}, and L_i\mid d_i{=}0=(1-\omega)^{N_i(0)}(\omega/y_{\max})^{N_i(+)}, with N_i(\cdot) the subject’s count of zero and positive choices. Dependence: with \operatorname{corr}(\epsilon_1,u)=\rho, write \epsilon_1=\rho u/\sigma_u+\sqrt{1-\rho^2}\,\xi, so P(d_i=1\mid u)=\Phi\big((z_i'\alpha+\rho u/\sigma_u)/\sqrt{1-\rho^2}\big) goes inside the integral. The integral has no closed form. Moffatt approximates it by averaging over Halton draws of u (maximum simulated likelihood); the figure uses Gauss–Hermite quadrature instead.

Posterior type probability. By Bayes’ rule, P(d_i=0\mid y_{i1},\dots,y_{iT}) = \frac{\int [1-P(d_i{=}1\mid u)]\,(L_i\mid d_i{=}0)\,f(u)\,du}{L_i}. With \omega=0, (L_i\mid d_i{=}0)=0 as soon as one y_{it}>0, so the posterior is exactly zero. With \omega>0 it is positive for every subject. Each positive choice in place of a zero multiplies (L_i\mid d_i{=}0) by \omega/[(1-\omega)\,y_{\max}] (about 0.004 in the figure’s design), so the zero-type account fades quickly but never reaches zero. This is the contrast in the figure.

::: {.lead-kernel}
When most people give nothing, the zeros come from two places: people who would **never** give, and people
who might give but **didn't this time**. A hurdle model gives each source its own equation. One equation
decides *whether* someone is a potential giver, the other decides *how much* they give. That lets a variable
make giving less likely and also make gifts larger, a pattern a single-equation model averages away.

## Why it matters

In a dictator game, one player splits a sum of money with a stranger who has no say. Most dictators give
nothing, and in a public goods game many people contribute nothing to the common pot. In both games zero
is the selfish, Nash-equilibrium prediction, so the data pile up there. [@moffatt_experimetrics] The same
pile appears in household spending on cigarettes or meat, in loan defaults, and in hours worked.
[@moffatt_experimetrics; @wooldridge_intro]

The standard tool for a pile at zero is [Tobit](censoring-truncation-and-selection.qmd). Tobit says every
zero is a **corner solution**: the person "wanted" to give a negative amount and the floor at zero stopped
them. Moffatt notes that many experimental economists are uneasy with this. A selfish dictator does not
want to give $-\$3$. They are a different *kind* of person, a **zero type**, and they would give nothing
whatever the treatment. [@moffatt_experimetrics] If that is how the data arise, then asking "what raises
giving?" is really two questions. What makes someone a potential giver at all? And what makes a potential
giver give more? Those questions can have different answers, so they need two equations.

## One process or two?

Tobit's hidden assumption is that **the process that makes a contribution positive is the same process that
sets its size**. If a treatment strongly raises the amount people give, Tobit must also predict that it
makes people more likely to give anything. Nothing guarantees this in advance. [@moffatt_experimetrics]

Moffatt gives an example. Let the outcome be a person's income from crime. Most values are zero, we hope.
White-collar workers are probably *less likely* than blue-collar workers to commit crime at all. But
white-collar criminals probably *earn more* from it when they do. One variable then pushes the two parts
of the outcome in opposite directions. A model with one index cannot represent that. [@moffatt_experimetrics]

Wooldridge gives a quick way to check for the problem in ordinary data. Fit a probit for "is $y$ positive?"
and compare its coefficients with the Tobit coefficients divided by $\hat\sigma$. If Tobit is right, the two
should be roughly the same. In his married women's hours data they are close but not identical. For small
children the probit coefficient is .868 and the rescaled Tobit coefficient is .797. That suggests young
children matter a little more for the decision to work at all than for how many hours a working mother
chooses, and **Tobit averages the two effects together**. A coefficient that is significant with opposite
signs in the two models would be a clear warning. [@wooldridge_intro] When Tobit fails this check,
Wooldridge points to models in which $P(y=0\mid x)$ and $E(y\mid y>0,x)$ have **separate parameters**.
[@wooldridge_intro] Hurdle models are the best-known family of these.

## Two hurdles

The **double hurdle** model is due to Cragg (1971). To give a positive amount, a subject must clear two
hurdles. [@moffatt_experimetrics]

1. **First hurdle: are you a potential contributor?** People who fall here are the zero types, the
   "selfish" dictators or the "free riders". They give zero, and nothing in the experiment changes that.
2. **Second hurdle: do you contribute this time, and how much?** Potential contributors then behave like
   a Tobit. Their circumstances or treatment can still leave them at a corner (a *censored* zero) or carry
   them to a positive amount.

Each hurdle has its own regression. A variable can appear in one, the other, or both, and **it can have
opposite signs in the two**. [@moffatt_experimetrics] An observed zero now has two possible explanations:
a zero type, or a potential contributor at the corner. The model keeps track of the probability of each.

The family has some simpler relatives, and the names are confusing, so it helps to list them.
[@moffatt_experimetrics]

| Model | Where zeros come from | What it adds to Tobit |
|---|---|---|
| **Tobit** | only corner solutions | — |
| **p-Tobit** (Deaton & Irish, 1984) | corners, plus a fixed share $1-p$ of "abstainers" | one number: the share of never-contributors |
| **Double hurdle** (Cragg, 1971) | corners, plus zero types whose share depends on covariates | a whole first-hurdle equation |
| **Single hurdle** | only zero types: anyone who clears hurdle 1 gives a positive amount | "first-hurdle dominance" (Jones, 1989); second hurdle is a truncated regression |

The p-Tobit is a double hurdle whose first equation has only an intercept. Moffatt estimates it that way,
then converts the intercept $\hat\alpha_0$ into the share $\hat p = \Phi(\hat\alpha_0)$, using the delta
method for its standard error. [@moffatt_experimetrics]

::: {.callout-tip title="\"Two-part model\" is not the same thing"}
Some studies fit a probit for zero vs positive and a separate truncated regression for the positive amounts,
and report the pair as "double hurdle" estimates. Moffatt calls that a **two-part model**. He keeps the
**hurdle** label for a model that estimates both equations *jointly*, by full-information maximum
likelihood. [@moffatt_experimetrics] Wooldridge's textbook uses "hurdle or two-part models" for the whole
family, so check which one an author means. [@wooldridge_intro] The two-part fit is still useful: it is
where you get starting values for the full model. [@moffatt_experimetrics]
:::

## Panel data: a type is for the whole experiment

Experiments usually record the same subject many times: several rounds, or several decisions. That makes
the hurdle model sharper, because a **type cannot change between rounds**. The first hurdle is decided once
per subject, and if a subject falls there, *every* observation on them is zero. The second hurdle runs
round by round, with a subject-level [random effect](mixed-effects-models.qmd) $u_i$ for people who are
generally generous or generally stingy. [@moffatt_experimetrics]

Panel data also rules out the single hurdle. Under first-hurdle dominance, a potential contributor would
have to give a positive amount *in every round*, and a zero type gives zero in every round. No subject
could mix zeros and positive amounts, yet most real panels are full of such subjects. The panel *double*
hurdle allows mixing, because a potential contributor can still hit the corner in some rounds.
[@moffatt_experimetrics]

Moffatt adds three refinements, each answering a practical worry. [@moffatt_experimetrics]

- **Upper censoring.** In public goods games many subjects give their *whole* endowment. The second hurdle
  can be censored at the top as well as at zero.
- **A tremble.** As stated, the model is too strict. A subject who gives zero in 19 rounds out of 20 can
  never be classed as a free rider. A **tremble parameter** $\omega$ allows a lapse of concentration on any
  single task. When it happens, the choice is random (uniform between zero and the maximum). The
  near-always-zero subject can then be a free rider who slipped once.
- **Dependence.** The subject's unobserved tendency to clear the first hurdle can be correlated (by $\rho$)
  with their unobserved generosity in the second. In a single cross-section $\rho$ is hard to identify
  (Smith, 2003). Moffatt says panel data let it be estimated "quite precisely", though in his own
  simulation (below) its interval is still very wide.

## Who is a zero type? Ask Bayes

The model never tells you which subjects *are* zero types. It gives a **posterior probability** of being one,
using [Bayes' rule](../01-foundations/bayes-theorem.qmd) after estimation. The prior is the first-hurdle
probability. The evidence is the subject's whole record of choices. [@moffatt_experimetrics] This is the same
reasoning about types as in [structural estimation](../05-causal-inference/structural-estimation.qmd), with
only two types.

The tremble changes the answer a lot. Without a tremble, **one positive contribution proves a subject is not
a zero type**: their posterior drops to exactly zero. With a tremble, an occasional positive amount is weak
evidence, and a subject who nearly always gives zero can keep a high posterior. In Moffatt's simulation
($n = 100$ subjects, $T = 20$ tasks, true tremble 0.04), every subject with fewer than five positive
contributions came out as very likely a zero type. [@moffatt_experimetrics] The figure re-runs that
simulation and asks what happens if the tremble is left out.

```{python}
#| label: fig-hurdle-posterior
#| fig-cap: "Two kinds of zero, and why the tremble matters. A re-simulation of Moffatt's panel hurdle design (100 subjects × 20 tasks, his true parameter values, including a 4% tremble and upper censoring at 10). Left: 80% of all choices are zero. Most come from zero types (ink); about one in seven is a censored zero from a potential contributor (grey). A single Tobit treats every zero alike. Right: each subject's posterior probability of being a zero type, computed with Bayes' rule at the true parameters, against how many times they gave a positive amount. With the tremble in the model (orange), all 71 true zero types are classified correctly, even those who gave once or twice by mistake. Without it (ink crosses), a single positive choice sends the posterior to zero, and 42 of the 71 zero types are wrongly read as certain contributors. Points are jittered horizontally."
#| code-fold: true
#| code-summary: "Show the figure code (simulation + Bayes by quadrature, numpy only)"

import math
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(971156)

# Moffatt's simulation design (Experimetrics §11.5.9)
n, T = 100, 20
a0, a1 = -1.5, 2.0            # first hurdle:  d* = a0 + a1 z + e1
b0, b1 = -5.0, 10.0           # second hurdle: y** = b0 + b1 x + su*u + se*e2
su, se, rho, w, ymax = 2.5, 5.0, 0.4, 0.04, 10.0

z = rng.uniform(0, 1, n)                       # subject characteristic
u = rng.normal(size=n)                         # subject random effect (standardised)
e1 = rho * u + np.sqrt(1 - rho**2) * rng.normal(size=n)
d = (a0 + a1 * z + e1 > 0).astype(int)         # 1 = potential contributor
x = rng.uniform(0, 1, (n, T))                  # task characteristic
y_latent = b0 + b1 * x + su * u[:, None] + se * rng.normal(size=(n, T))
y = np.minimum(np.maximum(y_latent, 0) * d[:, None], ymax)
tremble = rng.uniform(size=(n, T)) < w
y[tremble] = ymax * rng.uniform(size=tremble.sum())

Phi = np.vectorize(lambda v: 0.5 * (1 + math.erf(v / math.sqrt(2))))
phi = lambda v: np.exp(-v * v / 2) / math.sqrt(2 * math.pi)
nodes, wts = np.polynomial.hermite_e.hermegauss(40)   # integrate over u ~ N(0,1)
wts = wts / wts.sum()

n_pos = (y > 0).sum(axis=1)

def posterior_zero_type(om):
    """P(zero type | all T choices), Bayes' rule at the true parameters."""
    L0 = (om / ymax) ** n_pos * (1 - om) ** (T - n_pos)    # record if a zero type
    num, den = np.zeros(n), np.zeros(n)
    for v, wt in zip(nodes, wts):
        m = b0 + b1 * x + su * v
        p = np.where(y == 0, (1 - om) * (1 - Phi(m / se)),
            np.where(y >= ymax, (1 - om) * (1 - Phi((ymax - m) / se)),
                     (1 - om) * phi((y - m) / se) / se + om / ymax))
        L1 = p.prod(axis=1)                                 # record if a contributor
        p1 = Phi((a0 + a1 * z + rho * v) / np.sqrt(1 - rho**2))
        num += wt * (1 - p1) * L0
        den += wt * (p1 * L1 + (1 - p1) * L0)
    return num / den

post_tremble = posterior_zero_type(w)
post_strict = posterior_zero_type(0.0)

fig, (axL, axR) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(10, 4.3),
                               gridspec_kw={"width_ratios": [1, 1.25]})

# Left: where the zeros come from
zero_type_zeros = ((y == 0) & (d[:, None] == 0)).sum()
censored_zeros = ((y == 0) & (d[:, None] == 1)).sum()
pos = y[y > 0]
edges = np.arange(0, 10.5, 1.0)
counts, _ = np.histogram(pos, bins=edges)
axL.bar(-0.75, zero_type_zeros, width=0.9, color="#161613", edgecolor="#f4f3ec",
        label=f"zero type ({zero_type_zeros})")
axL.bar(-0.75, censored_zeros, width=0.9, bottom=zero_type_zeros, color="#b7b6a8",
        edgecolor="#f4f3ec", label=f"censored zero, potential contributor ({censored_zeros})")
axL.bar(edges[:-1] + 0.5, counts, width=0.95, color="#5e5e54", edgecolor="#f4f3ec",
        label=f"positive amount ({pos.size})")
axL.set_xticks([-0.75, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10])
axL.set_xticklabels(["0", "2", "4", "6", "8", "10"])
axL.set_xlabel("amount contributed in a task")
axL.set_ylabel("number of choices (of 2,000)")
axL.set_title("One spike at zero, two sources", fontsize=10)
axL.legend(fontsize=7.5, frameon=False, loc="upper right")

# Right: posterior zero-type probability vs positive contributions
jit = rng.uniform(-0.18, 0.18, n)
axR.scatter(n_pos + jit, post_strict, marker="x", s=26, color="#161613", lw=1.0,
            label="model without tremble")
axR.scatter(n_pos + jit, post_tremble, s=26, facecolors="none", edgecolors="#e8531d",
            lw=1.2, label="model with tremble (ω = 0.04)")
miss = ((d == 0) & (post_strict < 0.5)).sum()
axR.annotate(f"{miss} true zero types\nread as certain contributors",
             xy=(1.6, 0.02), xytext=(4.5, 0.42), fontsize=8, color="#161613",
             arrowprops=dict(arrowstyle="->", color="#161613", lw=0.9))
axR.set_xlabel("number of positive contributions (of 20)")
axR.set_ylabel("posterior P(zero type)")
axR.set_ylim(-0.05, 1.08)
axR.set_xticks(range(0, 21, 2))
axR.set_title("Bayes' rule, with and without a tremble", fontsize=10)
axR.legend(fontsize=7.5, frameon=False, loc="center right")
fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

The figure uses the true parameters, so it shows the classification logic and not estimation error.
Moffatt estimates the same model by maximum simulated likelihood and recovers every true value inside its
95% interval. The intervals differ a lot in width. The tremble is pinned down tightly (0.035, interval
0.026–0.045). The dependence parameter $\rho$ is not: true value 0.4, estimate 0.14, interval −0.85 to 1.14.
[@moffatt_experimetrics] That interval covers almost every possible correlation, and its upper end goes
past 1, which a correlation cannot reach. It is a symmetric Wald interval on a bounded parameter. So
"estimated quite precisely" is not supported by this simulation.

## Worked example: earned income and giving

Erkal et al. (2011) asked whether people with more income give more. Subjects in groups of four did a
real-effort task and were paid by rank: \$60, \$45, \$30 or \$15. Each then chose a transfer to each of the
other three, knowing everyone's earnings and knowing that only one transfer would be carried out.
[@moffatt_experimetrics] That gives 108 dictators making 3 decisions each, a natural panel. Of the 324
decisions, **264 (81%) were zero**. [@moffatt_experimetrics]

Two simple tables already show the pattern. [@moffatt_experimetrics]

| Rank (earnings) | % of dictators who gave at least once | Mean received by a receiver of this rank |
|---|---|---|
| 1 (\$60) | 26% | \$0.16 |
| 2 (\$45) | 67% | \$0.20 |
| 3 (\$30) | 37% | \$0.70 |
| 4 (\$15) | 22% | \$1.60 |
| All | 38% | \$0.67 |

The top earners were *less* likely to give than the second-ranked, about as unlikely as the poorest. Gifts
went steeply toward the poor: the lowest earners received ten times as much as the highest. The model's
design follows from what each variable can be. The dictator's own rank is a fixed feature of the subject,
so it can enter the first hurdle. The receiver's rank changes from one decision to the next, so it can only
enter the second. [@moffatt_experimetrics]

The panel hurdle estimates (with dependence) confirm the main finding. Being ranked second rather than first
raises the chance of being a giving type (coefficient 0.99, s.e. 0.41). Among giving types, gifts rise
steadily as the receiver's rank falls (0.61, 3.97, 6.94 relative to the top earner). [@moffatt_experimetrics]
A tremble added nothing here and was dropped, and $\rho$ was not significant (−0.30, s.e. 0.40). Moffatt's
practical advice is to take starting values from the two-part model. Even then the full model may not
converge in one go, so maximise in stages: fix some parameters first, then free them one at a time.
[@moffatt_experimetrics]

**The gender result is what a hurdle model is for.** Men were less likely to be giving types (−0.58 in the
first hurdle). Men who did give gave *more* (+1.89 in the second). Andreoni & Vesterlund (2001) had already
found this pattern in dictator giving. [@moffatt_experimetrics] A Mann–Whitney test on the amounts finds no
gender difference at all ($z = 0.495$, $p = 0.62$). The two effects cancel in the raw data and only
separate when the two decisions are modelled apart. [@moffatt_experimetrics] (The two gender coefficients
are about 1.6 standard errors from zero. Moffatt's stars, labelled $p<0.05$, are best read as one-sided
tests.)

**Is it income, or who earns it?** Rank in this experiment was *earned*. Perhaps high income makes people
stingy, or perhaps self-interested people work harder and end up ranked first. Erkal et al. ran a third
treatment in which earnings were assigned **by luck** (56 dictators). There, none of the own-earnings
dummies in the first hurdle was significant. When income is random, its level does not change the
propensity to give. The original pattern came from **self-selection** of self-interested people into the
top rank. [@moffatt_experimetrics] The model separates the two decisions, but it took a
[randomised](../05-causal-inference/randomized-experiments.qmd) treatment to explain the result.

## Worked example: house money and free riders

Clark (2002) tested the **house money effect**: do people behave differently with money the experimenter
gave them than with their own? In his public goods game 150 subjects played ten rounds in groups of five,
with groups reshuffled each round and an 80-token endowment. Half used house money and half their own.
[@moffatt_experimetrics] Clark's own tests used session averages, which left only ten observations, and
found nothing. Harrison (2007) re-analysed the data at the individual level and found that house money
changed the *share of free riders*. [@moffatt_experimetrics] That is a first-hurdle question.

A simple count points the same way. Twenty subjects never contributed: 13 of the 75 own-money subjects
(17.3%) and 7 of the 75 house-money subjects (9.3%). The difference is not significant (χ² $p = 0.150$,
Fisher's exact $p = 0.229$), and both tests assume subjects in a session act independently.
[@moffatt_experimetrics] In the panel hurdle model, the second hurdle behaves as expected. Desired
contributions fall by about 2.5–3 tokens a round. They rise with what others gave last round, but less and less as that
amount grows, which is evidence of reciprocity. (For round 1, where there is no "last round", Moffatt
assumes subjects expect 66 tokens. He chose the value by grid search and says the house-money result does
not depend on it.) [@moffatt_experimetrics]

The house-money coefficient in the first hurdle is about 0.34–0.39 in every model **without** a tremble,
and none of these is significant. Only the model **with** a tremble gives a significant effect (0.50, s.e.
0.25). That model also fits best by AIC (9,379 against about 9,430), and it estimates a large tremble
($\hat\omega = 0.09$). [@moffatt_experimetrics] The reason is the posterior. Adding the tremble lets a few
subjects who contributed only occasionally count partly as free riders, and those subjects are mostly in
the own-money group. Those few subjects decide whether the study finds a house money effect.
[@moffatt_experimetrics] That is worth knowing whichever way you read it. The best-fitting model finds the
effect. But the finding depends on one modelling choice, and it comes from a handful of subjects whose type
the data cannot settle.

## Common pitfalls

::: {.callout-warning title="Where this goes wrong"}

- **Assuming every zero is a corner solution.** Tobit has one route to zero. If some people would never
  participate, Tobit mixes "whether" and "how much" into one coefficient. Compare probit coefficients with
  Tobit's $\beta/\sigma$ as a first check. [@wooldridge_intro; @moffatt_experimetrics]

- **Calling a two-part model a double hurdle.** A probit plus a separate truncated regression is a
  two-part model. A hurdle model estimates both equations jointly. Say which one you fitted.
  [@moffatt_experimetrics]

- **Putting task variables in the first hurdle.** In a panel, type is fixed for the whole experiment, so
  the first hurdle can only contain subject characteristics, such as gender or earned rank. Features of the
  task, such as the receiver's rank or the round number, belong in the second. [@moffatt_experimetrics]

- **Concluding "no effect" from a test on raw amounts.** The Mann–Whitney test found no gender gap
  ($p = 0.62$) because two real, opposite effects cancelled. Opposite signs across the two hurdles are the
  most interesting results these models produce. [@moffatt_experimetrics]

- **Reading the posterior as a verdict.** A subject's zero-type probability depends on the model. Leave
  out the tremble and one stray contribution "proves" someone is not a free rider; include it and the same
  person may be classed as one. In the house-money data that single choice decides whether the effect is
  significant. Report results with and without it. [@moffatt_experimetrics]

- **Reading first-hurdle effects causally when the variable was not randomised.** Earned rank predicted
  giving type, but the luck treatment showed that the cause was self-selection, not income.
  [@moffatt_experimetrics]

:::

## Appendix · the mathematics {.unnumbered}

::: {.math-appendix}

**Tobit, for reference.** With $y_i^* = x_i'\beta + \epsilon_i$, $\epsilon_i\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)$ and
$y_i=\max(y_i^*,0)$,
$$
\ln L = \sum_i \mathbf 1[y_i=0]\ln\Phi\!\Big(\!-\tfrac{x_i'\beta}{\sigma}\Big)
+ \mathbf 1[y_i>0]\ln\Big[\tfrac1\sigma\phi\Big(\tfrac{y_i-x_i'\beta}{\sigma}\Big)\Big].
$$

**p-Tobit.** A share $1-p$ never contributes:
$$
\ln L = \sum_i \mathbf 1[y_i=0]\ln\Big[1-p\,\Phi\Big(\tfrac{x_i'\beta}{\sigma}\Big)\Big]
+ \mathbf 1[y_i>0]\ln\Big[p\,\tfrac1\sigma\phi\Big(\tfrac{y_i-x_i'\beta}{\sigma}\Big)\Big].
$$

**Double hurdle (Cragg).** First hurdle $d_i^* = z_i'\alpha + \epsilon_i$, $d_i=\mathbf 1[d_i^*>0]$;
second hurdle $y_i^{**} = x_i'\beta + u_i$, $y_i^*=\max(y_i^{**},0)$; observed $y_i=d_i y_i^*$, with
$(\epsilon_i,u_i)$ independent normals of variances $1$ and $\sigma^2$. Then
$$
\ln L = \sum_i \mathbf 1[y_i=0]\ln\Big[1-\Phi(z_i'\alpha)\,\Phi\Big(\tfrac{x_i'\beta}{\sigma}\Big)\Big]
+ \mathbf 1[y_i>0]\ln\Big[\Phi(z_i'\alpha)\,\tfrac1\sigma\phi\Big(\tfrac{y_i-x_i'\beta}{\sigma}\Big)\Big].
$$
A zero has probability $1-P(\text{pass 1})P(\text{pass 2})$: the L-shaped region of the joint density of
$(d^*,y^{**})$. With $z_i'\alpha=\alpha_0$ only, this is the p-Tobit with $p=\Phi(\alpha_0)$, and the
delta method gives $\operatorname{se}(\hat p)\approx\phi(\hat\alpha_0)\operatorname{se}(\hat\alpha_0)$.
The **single hurdle** replaces censoring in the second hurdle with truncation: positive outcomes have
density $\Phi(z'\alpha)\,\sigma^{-1}\phi(\cdot)/\Phi(x'\beta/\sigma)$, and every zero comes from hurdle 1.

**Panel hurdle.** Subject $i$, tasks $t=1,\dots,T$: $d_i=\mathbf 1[z_i'\alpha+\epsilon_{1i}>0]$ once;
$y_{it}^{**}=x_{it}'\beta+u_i+\epsilon_{2it}$, $u_i\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma_u^2)$,
$\epsilon_{2it}\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)$; $y_{it}=d_i\max(y_{it}^{**},0)$. Conditional on $u_i$,
$$
L_i\mid d_i{=}1,u_i = \prod_{t}\Big[1-\Phi\Big(\tfrac{x_{it}'\beta+u_i}{\sigma}\Big)\Big]^{\mathbf 1[y_{it}=0]}
\Big[\tfrac1\sigma\phi\Big(\tfrac{y_{it}-x_{it}'\beta-u_i}{\sigma}\Big)\Big]^{\mathbf 1[y_{it}>0]},
\qquad
L_i\mid d_i{=}0 = \mathbf 1\Big[\textstyle\sum_t y_{it}=0\Big],
$$
and
$$
L_i = \int\Big\{\Phi(z_i'\alpha)\,(L_i\mid d_i{=}1,u) + [1-\Phi(z_i'\alpha)]\,(L_i\mid d_i{=}0)\Big\}f(u)\,du,
\qquad \ln L=\sum_i\ln L_i .
$$
**Upper censoring** at $y_{\max}$ multiplies in $[1-\Phi((y_{\max}-x_{it}'\beta-u_i)/\sigma)]^{\mathbf 1[y_{it}=y_{\max}]}$.
**Tremble** $\omega$: each zero term picks up a factor $(1-\omega)$, each positive term becomes
$(1-\omega)\sigma^{-1}\phi(\cdot)+\omega/y_{\max}$, and $L_i\mid d_i{=}0=(1-\omega)^{N_i(0)}(\omega/y_{\max})^{N_i(+)}$,
with $N_i(\cdot)$ the subject's count of zero and positive choices.
**Dependence**: with $\operatorname{corr}(\epsilon_1,u)=\rho$, write
$\epsilon_1=\rho u/\sigma_u+\sqrt{1-\rho^2}\,\xi$, so
$P(d_i=1\mid u)=\Phi\big((z_i'\alpha+\rho u/\sigma_u)/\sqrt{1-\rho^2}\big)$ goes inside the integral.
The integral has no closed form. Moffatt approximates it by averaging over Halton draws of $u$
([maximum simulated likelihood](../01-foundations/simulation-and-monte-carlo.qmd#sec-msl)); the figure uses
Gauss–Hermite quadrature instead.

**Posterior type probability.** By Bayes' rule,
$$
P(d_i=0\mid y_{i1},\dots,y_{iT}) =
\frac{\int [1-P(d_i{=}1\mid u)]\,(L_i\mid d_i{=}0)\,f(u)\,du}{L_i}.
$$
With $\omega=0$, $(L_i\mid d_i{=}0)=0$ as soon as one $y_{it}>0$, so the posterior is exactly zero. With
$\omega>0$ it is positive for every subject. Each positive choice in place of a zero multiplies
$(L_i\mid d_i{=}0)$ by $\omega/[(1-\omega)\,y_{\max}]$ (about 0.004 in the figure's design), so the zero-type
account fades quickly but never reaches zero. This is the contrast in the figure.
