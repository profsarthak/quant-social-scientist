The Craft of Model Building

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source

Regression is a loop, not a button: diagnostic checking, honest respecification, and the four ‘big mistakes’ that wreck applied work.

The software will estimate any model you hand it and print a tidy table of stars either way — it cannot tell you whether the assumptions behind those stars are remotely true. That job is yours, and it is done by diagnostic checking: fit, interrogate, respecify, repeat. This entry is about the judgement that surrounds the arithmetic — and the four recurring disasters that sink applied regressions.

Why it matters

Every other entry in this chapter teaches a piece of machinery — an estimator, a test, a correction. This one is about the craft that ties them together: how a working researcher actually gets from a dataset to an estimate they’ll defend. Keith McNulty calls the goal explanatory (or inferential) modelling — “using a sample of data to draw inferences about the potential causes of an outcome of interest” — and frames the loop as a disciplined sequence: choose a model type for the question, prepare the data, fit it, read the outputs, and interpret them against the problem you started with. (McNulty 2021)

Richard Ashley puts the sharper edge on it: computer software “just evaluates formulas.” Hand it a t-statistic formula and it returns a p-value “just as nicely” whether or not the assumptions that make that p-value mean anything hold. (Ashley 2012) The number is printed with the same confident typography in both cases. Diagnostic checking is the only thing standing between you and a meaningless table — and it is exactly the part no software can do for you.

Modelling is a loop, not a button

The naïve picture of regression is a single arrow: data → estimate → report. The real picture is a cycle. You specify a model, fit it, then interrogate the fitted model — plot its residuals, test its assumptions, look for instability and influential points — and let what you find send you back to respecify. (Ashley 2012) A widening fan in the residuals says “repair the standard errors”; a curved residual plot says “you missed a nonlinearity, add a log or quadratic”; a coefficient that lurches when you drop three observations says “your relationship isn’t stable across the sample.”

Ashley’s most liberating point: a failed diagnostic check is not just destructive — it is constructive. Finding that unemployment is systematically higher in one region isn’t a dead end; it’s a prompt to model that difference, turning a violated assumption into a richer, truer specification. (Ashley 2012) The check that “fails” is the check that teaches you something.

NoteResolve your definitions before you model — Albers’s credit cards

The loop starts before the first regression, at the moment you decide what your variables even mean. Michael Albers’s example: what does the average American owe on credit cards? The answer is “$3,600. Or $7,743. Or $5,234. Or $1,098” — every figure is correct, each computed differently. (Albers 2017) Total balances or per-card? Does the Federal Reserve’s card-plus-SSN population count non-citizens? Does TransUnion drop long-unused cards from a per-card average? Cards carrying a balance, or all cards? The number your model explains is manufactured by these choices, so they must be settled and reported up front — and, Albers insists, “the study must only make claims that reflect the limitations of the calculations.” (Albers 2017) Half of researcher degrees of freedom live here, before a single coefficient is estimated.

Why the computer can’t do it for you

If diagnostic checking were mechanical, we’d automate it and go home. It isn’t, for a reason Ashley makes precise: the most important checks require you to first posit how the model might be wrong. (Ashley 2012) Is the mean different for one region? Does the effect drift over time? Does one industry behave unlike the rest? Software can run the test once you’ve named the suspicion — but naming the right suspicion takes context, economic knowledge, and creative attention that a formula-evaluator does not have.

And there is “no shelter in refusing to choose” by instructing the computer to run every possible test. (Ashley 2012) Do 100 hypothesis tests and chance alone rejects the null about five times at the 5% level — you’d drown a real signal in manufactured ones (the multiple-comparisons problem, arriving here as a diagnostic discipline, not just an inferential one). The optimal amount of checking is itself a judgement call: it scales with how hard the model is to check, how likely a check is to find a fixable defect, and how much the answer matters. (Ashley 2012) Small samples carry a hidden cost here — they may support one confidence interval yet be far too thin to check the assumptions that interval rests on. (Ashley 2012)

The four “big mistakes”

Of everything that can go wrong, Ashley argues, there are just four “big mistakes” — the ones that “make a really big difference,” that “can lead to disastrously poor results,” and that are all “reasonably avoidable” if the diagnostic checking is actually done. (Ashley 2012) They are worth memorising as a pre-flight checklist.

Big Mistake #1 — Neglecting trends or strong serial dependence. Two unrelated time-series that both trend upward will look tightly related; even the local trends of strongly autocorrelated data distort the inference. This is the engine of spurious regression, and it is easy to spot — the residual plots and auxiliary regressions of routine diagnostic checking flag it at once — and usually easy to fix, by respecifying the model with trend terms or dynamics. (Ashley 2012)

Big Mistake #2 — The tyranny of the vocal (but temporary) minority. What is worth quantifying is a stable statistical regularity — a relationship “broadly characteristic of the entire sample,” not one propped up by a couple of observations or one whose strength drifts across the data. A few unusual fluctuations in a regressor are actually a gift (they’re natural experiments that sharpen your estimates), but a coefficient that wanders across subsamples is “an unalloyed bad thing” — a symptom of misspecified functional form, an incomplete theory, or overzealous data-mining. (Ashley 2012) Checking for influential outliers and parameter instability catches it.

Big Mistake #3 — The danger of overextrapolation. A model’s prediction interval is only trustworthy inside the range of the regressors you actually observed. Push the predictor far beyond that range and you are “betting the farm” on the assumption that the relationship stays linear out there — and here diagnostic checking is structurally powerless: it can detect a failure of linearity only where you have data, and is “inherently unable” to detect one outside the observed range. (Ashley 2012) The prediction interval keeps quoting a reassuringly-narrow width even as its centre drifts onto a badly wrong value. The figure below makes this vivid.

Big Mistake #4 — The wrongly-omitted-variable trap. In a sense every deficiency is a version of this one. Ashley’s clean framing: the error term, by definition, is the sum of every explanatory variable you left out. (Ashley 2012) So any included regressor correlated with an omitted one is correlated with the error — endogenous — and its coefficient is biased and inconsistent even in large samples (worse, in fact, because large samples get more credence). His running example is the classic: regress wages on schooling with no measure of ability, and schooling “proxies” for the missing ability, biasing its coefficient upward. (Ashley 2012) The tactics range from include the variable (or drop the collinear one) through instruments — the whole apparatus of what regression can’t fix by itself.

Show the figure code 
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(11)

# True relationship: concave, saturating — nearly linear inside the sample window.
def truth(x):
    return 12.0 * (1.0 - np.exp(-0.28 * x))

# We only ever observe x in [2, 8].
x_lo, x_hi = 2.0, 8.0
n = 40
x = rng.uniform(x_lo, x_hi, n)
y = truth(x) + rng.normal(0, 0.45, n)

# Fit OLS by hand.
xbar, ybar = x.mean(), y.mean()
Sxx = np.sum((x - xbar) ** 2)
b1 = np.sum((x - xbar) * (y - ybar)) / Sxx
b0 = ybar - b1 * xbar
resid = y - (b0 + b1 * x)
s = np.sqrt(np.sum(resid ** 2) / (n - 2))       # residual standard error

# Prediction band for a NEW observation's mean (by hand): fit ± t * s * sqrt(1/n + (x0-xbar)^2/Sxx)
grid = np.linspace(0, 18, 300)
fit = b0 + b1 * grid
se_mean = s * np.sqrt(1.0 / n + (grid - xbar) ** 2 / Sxx)
band = 1.96 * se_mean

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(8.2, 4.4))

# observed-range shading (supporting grey) — where diagnostics can actually see
ax.axvspan(x_lo, x_hi, color="#b7b6a8", alpha=0.28, lw=0)

# true curve — the against-expectation result
ax.plot(grid, truth(grid), color="#8a8a7e", lw=2.2, ls="--", label="true relationship")

# fitted line: ink inside the observed range (the honest baseline)…
inside = grid <= x_hi
ax.plot(grid[inside], fit[inside], color="#161613", lw=2.0, label="fitted line (in-sample)")
ax.fill_between(grid[inside], fit[inside] - band[inside], fit[inside] + band[inside],
                color="#161613", alpha=0.12)

# …orange once we extrapolate (the thing being pointed at)
out = grid >= x_hi
ax.plot(grid[out], fit[out], color="#e8531d", lw=2.4, label="extrapolated line")
ax.fill_between(grid[out], fit[out] - band[out], fit[out] + band[out],
                color="#e8531d", alpha=0.15)

ax.scatter(x, y, s=16, color="#161613", alpha=0.6, zorder=5)
ax.text(5.0, 1.2, "observed range\n(diagnostics work here)",
        ha="center", va="bottom", fontsize=8.5, color="#5e5e54")
ax.annotate("prediction centred\non a wrong value",
            xy=(16.5, b0 + b1 * 16.5), xytext=(11.5, 4.0),
            fontsize=9, color="#e8531d",
            arrowprops=dict(arrowstyle="->", color="#e8531d", lw=1.2))

ax.set_xlabel("predictor  x")
ax.set_ylabel("outcome  y")
ax.set_title("Overextrapolation: the band stays narrow, the centre goes wrong")
ax.legend(loc="upper left", fontsize=8.5, framealpha=0.9)
fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()

Common pitfalls

WarningWhere this goes wrong

  • Trusting the table because it printed. A regression output is arithmetic on your assumptions; if the assumptions fail, the stars are decoration. The software “just evaluates formulas.” (Ashley 2012)

  • Skipping the loop. Estimate-once-and-report treats regression as a button. Fit → check → respecify → re-check is the actual method; the “failed” check is usually where the good model comes from. (Ashley 2012)

  • Automating judgement. Running every available test is not diligence — with 100 tests you manufacture ~5 false alarms at the 5% level. You must posit how the model might be wrong and test that. (Ashley 2012)

  • Extrapolating past your data. A narrow prediction interval outside the observed range of the regressors is false comfort — it quantifies sampling noise, not the risk that linearity breaks where you can’t see. (Ashley 2012)

  • Reporting a number your definitions manufactured. “Average debt” is $1,098 or $7,743 depending on choices made before modelling. Fix and disclose the definitions; claim only what the calculation supports. (Albers 2017)

  • Reflexively deleting outliers. An unusual regressor value can be a natural experiment that sharpens estimates; investigate before discarding. It’s an unstable coefficient — not a lone odd point — that signals real trouble. (Ashley 2012; McNulty 2021)

NoteWho says what
  • Ashley, Fundamentals of Applied Econometrics — the diagnostic-checking philosophy (essential; software “just evaluates formulas”; a failed check is constructive; checks require positing how the model is wrong; “no shelter” in running every test; the amount of checking is situational; small samples can’t be checked); and the four “big mistakes” — neglecting trends/serial dependence, the tyranny of the vocal-but-temporary minority, overextrapolation (“betting the farm”; the prediction band whose centre drifts), and the wrongly-omitted-variable trap (the error term is the sum of omitted variables; the schooling-proxies-for-ability example).

  • Albers, Introduction to Quantitative Data Analysis — resolve the data-analysis definitions before modelling; the credit-card-debt figures (all correct, all different) and the rule that claims must reflect the limits of how the data were calculated.

Appendix · the mathematics

Why the prediction band narrows but the centre can be wrong. For a simple regression fit on x_1,\dots,x_N, the estimated 95% interval for the mean outcome at a new point x_0 is \hat y(x_0) \;\pm\; t_{.975}\, s \sqrt{\tfrac{1}{N} + \frac{(x_0-\bar x)^2}{\sum_i (x_i-\bar x)^2}} , where s is the residual standard error. Its width grows only like |x_0-\bar x| — gently, and without bound-awareness. Crucially, this formula quantifies sampling uncertainty in the line’s height given a true constant slope \beta. It contains no term for the possibility that \beta is not constant outside the observed range. So as x_0 leaves the data, the band keeps quoting a finite, only-slowly-widening width while \hat y(x_0)=\hat\beta_0+\hat\beta_1 x_0 marches off along a straight line the world may have abandoned — the interval is narrow and mis-centred. Diagnostic checks on functional form can reject linearity only where sample points exist to reveal the curvature; beyond them the check is blind. (Ashley 2012)

Omitted variables, formally. Write the true model y = X\beta + Z\gamma + \varepsilon but fit y = X\beta + u. Then u = Z\gamma + \varepsilon, so \operatorname{Cov}(X,u)\neq 0 whenever the included X is correlated with the omitted Z, and \operatorname{plim}\hat\beta_{\text{OLS}} = \beta + (X'X)^{-1}X'Z\,\gamma \neq \beta. The bias does not vanish as N\to\infty — it is inconsistency, Ashley’s point that the trap is “worse with a large sample.” The direction is the sign of (correlation of X with Z) \times (effect \gamma of Z): schooling positively correlated with unobserved ability, ability raising wages, so the schooling coefficient is biased up. (Ashley 2012)

## The four "big mistakes"

Of everything that can go wrong, Ashley argues, there are **just four "big mistakes"** — the
ones that "make a really big difference," that "can lead to disastrously poor results," and that
are all "reasonably avoidable" *if the diagnostic checking is actually done.*
[@ashley_econometrics] They are worth memorising as a pre-flight checklist.

**Big Mistake #1 — Neglecting trends or strong serial dependence.** Two unrelated time-series
that both trend upward will look tightly related; even the *local* trends of strongly
autocorrelated data distort the inference. This is the engine of
[spurious regression](../04-predictive/unit-roots-and-stationarity.qmd), and it is *easy to
spot* — the residual plots and auxiliary regressions of routine diagnostic checking flag it at
once — and usually easy to fix, by respecifying the model with trend terms or dynamics.
[@ashley_econometrics]

**Big Mistake #2 — The tyranny of the vocal (but temporary) minority.** What is worth
quantifying is a *stable* statistical regularity — a relationship "broadly characteristic of the
entire sample," not one propped up by a couple of observations or one whose strength drifts
across the data. A few unusual *fluctuations in a regressor* are actually a gift (they're
natural experiments that sharpen your estimates), but a *coefficient* that wanders across
subsamples is "an unalloyed bad thing" — a symptom of misspecified functional form, an
incomplete theory, or overzealous data-mining. [@ashley_econometrics] Checking for influential
outliers and parameter instability catches it.

**Big Mistake #3 — The danger of overextrapolation.** A model's prediction interval is only
trustworthy *inside the range of the regressors you actually observed*. Push the predictor far
beyond that range and you are "betting the farm" on the assumption that the relationship stays
linear out there — and here diagnostic checking is **structurally powerless**: it can detect a
failure of linearity only where you have data, and is "inherently unable" to detect one outside
the observed range. [@ashley_econometrics] The prediction interval keeps quoting a
reassuringly-narrow width even as its *centre* drifts onto a badly wrong value. The figure below
makes this vivid.

**Big Mistake #4 — The wrongly-omitted-variable trap.** In a sense every deficiency is a version
of this one. Ashley's clean framing: **the error term, by definition, *is* the sum of every
explanatory variable you left out.** [@ashley_econometrics] So any included regressor correlated
with an omitted one is correlated with the error — endogenous — and its coefficient is biased
and inconsistent *even in large samples* (worse, in fact, because large samples get more
credence). His running example is the classic: regress wages on schooling with no measure of
*ability*, and schooling "proxies" for the missing ability, biasing its coefficient upward.
[@ashley_econometrics] The tactics range from *include the variable* (or drop the collinear one)
through instruments — the whole apparatus of
[what regression can't fix by itself](regression-and-causality.qmd).

```{python}
#| label: fig-overextrapolation
#| fig-cap: "Big Mistake #3, drawn. The true relationship (olive) saturates; within the observed window (x≈2–8, grey band) a straight line fits it almost perfectly, and its 95% prediction band is tight and honest. Extrapolate to the right (orange) and the fitted line keeps climbing while the truth flattens — the band stays deceptively narrow but is now centred on a badly wrong value. Diagnostic checking cannot save you here: there is no data out there to reveal the failure."
#| code-fold: true
#| code-summary: "Show the figure code"

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(11)

# True relationship: concave, saturating — nearly linear inside the sample window.
def truth(x):
    return 12.0 * (1.0 - np.exp(-0.28 * x))

# We only ever observe x in [2, 8].
x_lo, x_hi = 2.0, 8.0
n = 40
x = rng.uniform(x_lo, x_hi, n)
y = truth(x) + rng.normal(0, 0.45, n)

# Fit OLS by hand.
xbar, ybar = x.mean(), y.mean()
Sxx = np.sum((x - xbar) ** 2)
b1 = np.sum((x - xbar) * (y - ybar)) / Sxx
b0 = ybar - b1 * xbar
resid = y - (b0 + b1 * x)
s = np.sqrt(np.sum(resid ** 2) / (n - 2))       # residual standard error

# Prediction band for a NEW observation's mean (by hand): fit ± t * s * sqrt(1/n + (x0-xbar)^2/Sxx)
grid = np.linspace(0, 18, 300)
fit = b0 + b1 * grid
se_mean = s * np.sqrt(1.0 / n + (grid - xbar) ** 2 / Sxx)
band = 1.96 * se_mean

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(8.2, 4.4))

# observed-range shading (supporting grey) — where diagnostics can actually see
ax.axvspan(x_lo, x_hi, color="#b7b6a8", alpha=0.28, lw=0)

# true curve — the against-expectation result
ax.plot(grid, truth(grid), color="#8a8a7e", lw=2.2, ls="--", label="true relationship")

# fitted line: ink inside the observed range (the honest baseline)…
inside = grid <= x_hi
ax.plot(grid[inside], fit[inside], color="#161613", lw=2.0, label="fitted line (in-sample)")
ax.fill_between(grid[inside], fit[inside] - band[inside], fit[inside] + band[inside],
                color="#161613", alpha=0.12)

# …orange once we extrapolate (the thing being pointed at)
out = grid >= x_hi
ax.plot(grid[out], fit[out], color="#e8531d", lw=2.4, label="extrapolated line")
ax.fill_between(grid[out], fit[out] - band[out], fit[out] + band[out],
                color="#e8531d", alpha=0.15)

ax.scatter(x, y, s=16, color="#161613", alpha=0.6, zorder=5)
ax.text(5.0, 1.2, "observed range\n(diagnostics work here)",
        ha="center", va="bottom", fontsize=8.5, color="#5e5e54")
ax.annotate("prediction centred\non a wrong value",
            xy=(16.5, b0 + b1 * 16.5), xytext=(11.5, 4.0),
            fontsize=9, color="#e8531d",
            arrowprops=dict(arrowstyle="->", color="#e8531d", lw=1.2))

ax.set_xlabel("predictor  x")
ax.set_ylabel("outcome  y")
ax.set_title("Overextrapolation: the band stays narrow, the centre goes wrong")
ax.legend(loc="upper left", fontsize=8.5, framealpha=0.9)
fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

## Common pitfalls

::: {.callout-warning title="Where this goes wrong"}

- **Trusting the table because it printed.** A regression output is arithmetic on your
  assumptions; if the assumptions fail, the stars are decoration. The software "just evaluates
  formulas." [@ashley_econometrics]

- **Skipping the loop.** Estimate-once-and-report treats regression as a button. Fit → check →
  respecify → re-check is the actual method; the "failed" check is usually where the good model
  comes from. [@ashley_econometrics]

- **Automating judgement.** Running every available test is not diligence — with 100 tests you
  manufacture ~5 false alarms at the 5% level. You must *posit how the model might be wrong* and
  test that. [@ashley_econometrics]

- **Extrapolating past your data.** A narrow prediction interval outside the observed range of
  the regressors is false comfort — it quantifies sampling noise, not the risk that linearity
  breaks where you can't see. [@ashley_econometrics]

- **Reporting a number your definitions manufactured.** "Average debt" is \$1,098 *or* \$7,743
  depending on choices made before modelling. Fix and disclose the definitions; claim only what
  the calculation supports. [@albers_quant_data]

- **Reflexively deleting outliers.** An unusual *regressor* value can be a natural experiment
  that sharpens estimates; investigate before discarding. It's an unstable *coefficient* — not a
  lone odd point — that signals real trouble. [@ashley_econometrics; @mcnulty_rmodeling]

:::

## Appendix · the mathematics {.unnumbered}

::: {.math-appendix}

**Why the prediction band narrows but the centre can be wrong.** For a simple regression fit on
$x_1,\dots,x_N$, the estimated 95% interval for the mean outcome at a new point $x_0$ is
$$
\hat y(x_0) \;\pm\; t_{.975}\, s \sqrt{\tfrac{1}{N} + \frac{(x_0-\bar x)^2}{\sum_i (x_i-\bar x)^2}} ,
$$
where $s$ is the residual standard error. Its width grows only like $|x_0-\bar x|$ — gently, and
*without bound-awareness*. Crucially, this formula quantifies **sampling uncertainty in the
line's height given a true constant slope $\beta$.** It contains **no term for the possibility
that $\beta$ is not constant** outside the observed range. So as $x_0$ leaves the data, the
band keeps quoting a finite, only-slowly-widening width while $\hat y(x_0)=\hat\beta_0+\hat\beta_1 x_0$
marches off along a straight line the world may have abandoned — the interval is narrow *and*
mis-centred. Diagnostic checks on functional form can reject linearity only where sample points
exist to reveal the curvature; beyond them the check is blind. [@ashley_econometrics]

**Omitted variables, formally.** Write the true model $y = X\beta + Z\gamma + \varepsilon$ but fit
$y = X\beta + u$. Then $u = Z\gamma + \varepsilon$, so $\operatorname{Cov}(X,u)\neq 0$ whenever the
included $X$ is correlated with the omitted $Z$, and
$\operatorname{plim}\hat\beta_{\text{OLS}} = \beta + (X'X)^{-1}X'Z\,\gamma \neq \beta$. The bias
does **not** vanish as $N\to\infty$ — it is *inconsistency*, Ashley's point that the trap is
"worse with a large sample." The direction is the sign of (correlation of $X$ with $Z$) $\times$
(effect $\gamma$ of $Z$): schooling positively correlated with unobserved ability, ability
raising wages, so the schooling coefficient is biased **up**. [@ashley_econometrics]

:::
