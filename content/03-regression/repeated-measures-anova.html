Repeated-Measures ANOVA: The Same People, Measured Again

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source

When every participant goes through every condition, each person becomes their own control. That helps only as much as people stay consistent, and it brings an assumption of its own: sphericity.

Measure the same people under several conditions and you can subtract out who they are. The stable part of each person (heavier, lighter, keener, more tired) leaves the error term, and only each person’s wobble around their own average counts as noise. That is the whole appeal of repeated-measures ANOVA. The cost is an extra assumption, sphericity, plus the design risks that come from measuring in a fixed order over time.

Why it matters

A large share of psychology, health and education research measures people more than once: before and after a programme, at three, six, nine and twelve months, or under each of several conditions in turn. (Martin and Bridgmon 2012) Analysing those scores as if they came from different people is a mistake in both directions. It throws away the most useful feature of the design, which is that each person can be compared with themselves. And it treats correlated scores as independent, which breaks the arithmetic of the ordinary one-way ANOVA.

Repeated-measures ANOVA (RM-ANOVA) is the classical answer. It extends the paired t-test from two measurements to three or more. (Martin and Bridgmon 2012) It is also the simplest example of a pattern you will meet again in mixed-effects models and in fixed-effects panels: remove each unit’s own level, then study what changes within the unit.

Three designs, one structure

Martin & Bridgmon list three common uses of the within-subjects layout: (Martin and Bridgmon 2012)

  1. The same people measured repeatedly over time (weight at 3, 6, 9 and 12 months).
  2. The same people measured under different conditions (each participant tries every version of a task).
  3. Matched sets of people measured under different conditions (pairs or triples matched on a background variable, each member assigned to a different condition).

All three share one structure: a table with one row per person (or matched set) and one column per condition. The rows are not independent of each other’s columns. Someone who loses a lot of weight in one period tends to be someone who loses a lot in others. When a design has both a within-subjects factor and a between-subjects factor (e.g. time × treatment group), the book calls it a mixed design. (Martin and Bridgmon 2012)

The idea: take the people out of the error

In a one-way ANOVA on independent groups, the total variation splits into two parts: between conditions (the signal) and within conditions (the noise). Within-condition noise includes everything that makes one person differ from another.

With repeated measures you can split the noise again. Some of it is just stable differences between people. You can estimate each person’s average across conditions and set that variation aside, because it cannot be responsible for a difference between conditions: everyone appears in every condition. What remains is the person × condition residual, the extent to which people respond to the conditions in different ways. That residual becomes the error term: (Martin and Bridgmon 2012)

\text{SS}_{\text{total}} = \text{SS}_{\text{conditions}} + \underbrace{\text{SS}_{\text{subjects}}}_{\text{set aside}} + \text{SS}_{\text{error}} .

The F-ratio is then conditions over error, on (k-1) and (k-1)(n-1) degrees of freedom for k conditions and n people.

How much this buys depends on one quantity: how strongly a person’s scores correlate across conditions. If people are highly consistent (the heavy loser in one period is the heavy loser in every period), then most of the within-condition noise is between-person, and removing it shrinks the error dramatically. If people’s scores bounce around independently from period to period, there is little stable person-level variation to remove, and the repeated-measures analysis gains almost nothing over treating the columns as separate groups. The figure makes this concrete.

Worked example: does a weight-loss partner help?

Martin & Bridgmon’s study (simulated, after Gorin et al. 2005) follows 35 overweight adults in a behavioural weight-control treatment for a year. (Martin and Bridgmon 2012) Each participant has a support partner, a friend who is also in the programme. The design switches the partner on and off:

Period (3 months each) Condition Mean weight loss (lb) SD
1 treatment with partner 10.314 2.246
2 partner removed 4.114 1.676
3 partner added back 10.600 1.631
4 partner continued 10.257 1.821

This is a repeated-treatment design in Shadish, Cook & Campbell’s terms: introduce, remove, reintroduce. (Martin and Bridgmon 2012) If the outcome drops when the partner leaves and recovers when the partner returns, that on-off-on pattern is harder to explain by coincidence than a single before-after change.

The analysis, step by step

The book follows its six-step hypothesis-testing routine. (Martin and Bridgmon 2012)

  • Planning. α = .01, because the study replicates earlier findings. An a priori power analysis in G*Power used partial η² = .35 from a previous study, four measurements, and an assumed correlation of .55 among the repeated measures, and reported that just 6 participants would give power .80. The study has 35.
  • Screening. One participant (#24) scores 16 lb in the add-back period, z = 3.31, past the ±3.29 cut-off. Their other partner-period scores are 15 and 14, so the score is typical for that person, and it is kept. Skewness, kurtosis, Shapiro–Wilk and Q–Q plots all pass.
  • Sphericity. Mauchly’s W = .761, χ²(5) = 8.94, p = .112, so the assumption is retained (more on this below).
  • The omnibus test. F(3, 102) = 110.325, p < .001, partial η² = .764.
  • Follow-ups. Fisher’s protected LSD pairwise comparisons show that the partner-removed period differs from each of the other three (each gap is 6.1 to 6.5 lb, all 99% CIs above zero), and the three partner periods do not differ from one another.

The book also works the whole partition by hand from the printed data: (Martin and Bridgmon 2012)

Source SS df MS F
Subjects 150.73 34
Conditions 1,036.42 3 345.47 110.3
Error (subjects × conditions) 319.39 102 3.131
Total 1,506.54 139

What the subjects row tells you (our arithmetic)

Here is the instructive part. If you ignored the pairing and ran an ordinary one-way ANOVA on the four columns, the subjects SS would fall back into the error: (150.73 + 319.39)/136 = 3.457, giving F \approx 345.5/3.457 \approx 99.9. The repeated-measures F is 110.3. The design helped, but only by about 10%.

The reason is in the data. Computed from the book’s printed scores, the correlations between periods average only about .09. The largest is .42, and two are slightly negative. The power analysis had assumed .55. Under that assumption the error term would have been less than half the size of the independent-groups error. In the actual data, a person’s weight loss in one period says little about their loss in the next, so there was little stable person-level variation to take out. The design is still correct and the effect is huge, so nothing is lost here. But the a priori calculation that called for six people rested on a correlation the data did not show. For a smaller effect, that gap would have meant an underpowered study.

Seeing it

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# Martin & Bridgmon (2012), Ch 8 data: 35 participants x 4 conditions (lb lost per 3-month period)
raw = """11 6 11 9  8 5 11 8  8 4 12 10  8 3 10 12  7 4 9 11  12 2 10 8  12 6 10 9  13 7 9 7
7 1 12 14  8 2 11 13  11 4 11 12  13 4 10 11  14 5 10 10  9 4 11 9  9 6 9 8  8 3 8 8
11 2 10 9  11 4 10 7  12 5 13 12  10 4 11 12  10 3 10 11  7 5 12 9  7 6 9 10  15 7 16 14
14 2 8 10  12 1 13 10  11 4 11 12  11 4 12 11  10 3 10 10  9 3 9 9  12 5 8 12  11 5 10 11
9 6 12 10  8 7 11 12  13 2 12 9"""
y = np.array(raw.split(), dtype=float).reshape(35, 4)
n, k = y.shape

# Variance partition by hand
grand = y.mean()
ss_tot = ((y - grand) ** 2).sum()
ss_cond = n * ((y.mean(0) - grand) ** 2).sum()
ss_subj = k * ((y.mean(1) - grand) ** 2).sum()
ss_err = ss_tot - ss_cond - ss_subj
mse_rm = ss_err / ((k - 1) * (n - 1))             # 3.131
mse_ind = (ss_subj + ss_err) / (n * k - k)        # 3.457: subjects folded back into error

C = np.cov(y.T)
off = C[~np.eye(k, dtype=bool)]
rho_data = off.mean() / np.diag(C).mean()         # average between-period correlation, ~ .09

fig, (ax1, ax2) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(9.6, 4.2))

# --- Left: spaghetti plot (small jitter so tied trajectories stay visible)
rng = np.random.default_rng(8)
x = np.arange(k)
for row in y:
    ax1.plot(x, row + rng.uniform(-0.15, 0.15, k), color="#b7b6a8", lw=0.8, alpha=0.8)
ax1.plot(x, y.mean(0), "o-", color="#e8531d", lw=2.6, ms=7, label="condition mean")
ax1.set_xticks(x, ["with\npartner", "partner\nremoved", "partner\nback", "partner\ncontinued"])
ax1.set_ylabel("weight lost in the period (lb)")
ax1.set_title("Each line is one person")
ax1.legend(fontsize=8, frameon=False, loc="lower right")

# --- Right: error mean square as a function of within-person correlation
rho = np.linspace(0, 0.9, 200)
ax2.plot(rho, mse_ind * (1 - rho), color="#161613", lw=2.2, label="RM error mean square, σ²(1 − ρ)")
ax2.axhline(mse_ind, color="#5e5e54", ls="--", lw=1.3, label="independent-groups error, σ²")
ax2.plot(0.55, mse_ind * 0.45, "s", color="#8a8a7e", ms=8)
ax2.annotate("assumed in the\npower analysis, ρ = .55", (0.55, mse_ind * 0.45), xytext=(0.6, 2.35),
             fontsize=8, color="#5e5e54")
ax2.plot(rho_data, mse_rm, "o", color="#e8531d", ms=9)
ax2.annotate(f"the data: ρ ≈ {rho_data:.2f}\nMSE {mse_ind:.2f} → {mse_rm:.2f}", (rho_data, mse_rm),
             xytext=(0.02, 2.2), fontsize=8, color="#e8531d")
ax2.set_xlim(0, 0.9)

Sphericity: the assumption that comes with the gain

Independent-groups ANOVA assumes equal variances. RM-ANOVA needs a stronger condition because the columns are correlated. Martin & Bridgmon state it as the variances being constant and the covariances being constant across conditions, the pattern called compound symmetry. (Martin and Bridgmon 2012) Strictly, sphericity is a slightly weaker condition: the variance of the difference between any two conditions is the same for every pair. Compound symmetry is sufficient for sphericity; sphericity is what the F test actually needs.

The intuition: the single error term in the F-ratio pools information about every pair of conditions. That pooling is fair only if every pairwise difference is equally noisy. If some pairs of periods are tightly linked (adjacent months, say) and others are not, one pooled error term is too small for some comparisons and too large for others, and the F-test’s Type I error rate drifts above its nominal level.

Mauchly’s test checks the assumption; you want to retain its null hypothesis. (Martin and Bridgmon 2012) When it fails, the remedy is to keep the F statistic but shrink its degrees of freedom by a correction factor ε (epsilon), so that the critical value rises: (Martin and Bridgmon 2012)

Correction ε in the example Corrected df Character
none (sphericity assumed) 1 3, 102
Greenhouse–Geisser .867 2.60, 88.39 more conservative, especially in small samples
Huynh–Feldt .945 2.83, 96.36 less conservative
lower bound .333 1, 34 the most pessimistic case

Here every row gives p < .001, so the correction is moot. The example also shows how forgiving the test can be: from the book’s data, the four period variances range from 2.66 to 5.05 (nearly a factor of two), and the covariances range from −0.24 to 1.25. That is visibly not compound symmetric, yet Mauchly’s test, which has little power at n = 35, did not reject. Many analysts therefore apply the Greenhouse–Geisser or Huynh–Feldt correction routinely instead of letting Mauchly’s p-value decide.

Follow-ups: pairwise comparisons, trends, and contrasts

A significant omnibus F says only that the four means are not all equal. The book follows up three ways. (Martin and Bridgmon 2012)

Pairwise comparisons. Fisher’s protected LSD, which the book describes as a liberal procedure that finds more differences than most. (Martin and Bridgmon 2012) In the hand calculation the book uses the pooled error (\text{MS}_E = 3.131) for every pair, giving t = 14.52 for with-partner vs removed. The SPSS table instead uses a separate standard error for each pair (.458 for that comparison). Those two agree only when sphericity holds. If sphericity is doubtful, paired comparisons using each pair’s own differences are safer, because they do not borrow strength from the other pairs.

Trend analysis. When the conditions are ordered along a continuum, polynomial contrasts ask about the shape of the means: linear, quadratic, cubic. The book reports a significant quadratic trend, F(1, 34) = 114.58, partial η² = .771, reading the profile as a V. (Martin and Bridgmon 2012) But the same table shows a cubic trend with an even larger F(1, 34) = 286.68 and a linear trend with F(1, 34) = 15.68. All three are “significant” because none of them is the right description. The four means are three equal highs and one low. That is not a polynomial in time.

A planned contrast (our arithmetic). The hypothesis was always partner periods vs the no-partner period. Testing that single contrast directly (weights 1, −3, 1, 1) captures 1,034 of the 1,036 condition sums of squares, F(1, 34) ≈ 324. One theory-driven question, asked once, does all the work that the omnibus test, six pairwise tests and three trend tests did between them. This is the general lesson of multiple testing: pre-specify the comparison your hypothesis implies.

What the design cannot rule out

The repeated-treatment design is a one-group quasi-experiment, and Martin & Bridgmon say so. Without a control group or random assignment, history (outside events) and maturation (participants changing on their own) can masquerade as treatment effects. Participants who notice the on-off cycle may also react to the study itself. (Martin and Bridgmon 2012) There is a further problem specific to fixed orders. Here, partner removed is always the second three-month block. Any effect of being six months into a diet (a plateau, the summer holidays) is confounded with the absence of the partner. The introduce-remove-reintroduce sequence weakens this worry, because a plateau would not explain the recovery in period 3. It does not remove it. When conditions can be given in any order, randomising or counterbalancing the order across participants turns order into a measurable factor instead of a hidden confound. (See the research designs entry for the full list of validity threats.)

The nonparametric and modern alternatives

  • Friedman’s rank test is the nonparametric analogue of RM-ANOVA, and the Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed-ranks test is the analogue of the paired t-test. Martin & Bridgmon use Friedman’s test for a three-period on-off-on pain study whose scores fail the normality checks. (Martin and Bridgmon 2012) See resampling and nonparametric tests.
  • Mixed-effects models handle the same data with a random intercept per person. They cope with missing occasions (RM-ANOVA drops anyone with a missing score), unequal spacing, and time-varying covariates, and they let you model the covariance structure directly instead of assuming sphericity. See mixed-effects models.

Common pitfalls

WarningWhere this goes wrong

  • Analysing repeated scores as independent groups. It wastes the pairing when people are consistent, and it violates independence whatever the correlation. Use the within-subjects error term. (Martin and Bridgmon 2012)

  • Assuming the repeated-measures payoff instead of checking it. The power gain comes from the correlation between a person’s scores. A power analysis built on ρ = .55 when the data show ρ ≈ .09 overstates the design’s sensitivity by more than double. Look at the correlation matrix.

  • Letting a low-powered Mauchly test certify sphericity. A non-significant Mauchly test at modest n is weak evidence. Variances that differ by a factor of two are a reason to apply Greenhouse–Geisser or Huynh–Feldt anyway.

  • Fitting polynomial trends to conditions that are not a quantity. Trend analysis assumes equally spaced, ordered levels with a smooth relationship. An on-off-on sequence is not that, and quadratic and cubic terms can both come out “significant”. Test the contrast your hypothesis names.

  • Reading a fixed-order design as an experiment. When every participant gets the conditions in the same order, condition and time are confounded. History, maturation and reactivity remain open threats without a control group or counterbalancing. (Martin and Bridgmon 2012)

  • Leaning on “observed power”. The book reports post-hoc observed power of 1.000. It is a re-expression of the p-value and adds nothing (why). (Martin and Bridgmon 2012)

Appendix · the mathematics

The partition. For n subjects and k conditions with scores Y_{ij}, subject means \bar Y_{i\cdot}, condition means \bar Y_{\cdot j} and grand mean \bar Y:

\text{SS}_{\text{cond}} = n\sum_j(\bar Y_{\cdot j}-\bar Y)^2,\qquad \text{SS}_{\text{subj}} = k\sum_i(\bar Y_{i\cdot}-\bar Y)^2, \text{SS}_{\text{err}} = \sum_{i,j}\big(Y_{ij}-\bar Y_{i\cdot}-\bar Y_{\cdot j}+\bar Y\big)^2 = \text{SS}_{\text{tot}}-\text{SS}_{\text{cond}}-\text{SS}_{\text{subj}},

and F = \dfrac{\text{SS}_{\text{cond}}/(k-1)}{\text{SS}_{\text{err}}/\big((k-1)(n-1)\big)}. The error term is the subject × condition interaction: the part of each score explained neither by the person’s level nor by the condition’s level. In the example: 1506.54 - 1036.42 - 150.73 = 319.39 and F = 345.47/3.131 = 110.3. (Martin and Bridgmon 2012)

Why consistency shrinks the error. Suppose every condition has variance \sigma^2 and every pair of conditions has covariance \sigma^2\rho (compound symmetry). Then the expected error mean square is

\mathbb E[\text{MS}_{\text{err}}] = \sigma^2(1-\rho),

while the independent-groups error estimates \sigma^2. Equivalently, the RM error equals the average variance minus the average covariance. From the book’s data: 3.457 - 0.325 = 3.131, exactly the printed \text{MS}_E. The F-ratio is multiplied by roughly 1/(1-\rho). That is 1.10 at the observed \rho \approx .09 and 2.2 at the assumed .55.

It is a two-way model. RM-ANOVA is OLS of Y_{ij} on condition dummies and a dummy for every subject, Y_{ij} = \mu + \alpha_j + \pi_i + \varepsilon_{ij}, with no interaction. The subject dummies are exactly the fixed effects of a panel regression; treating \pi_i as random with variance \sigma^2_\pi gives the random-intercept mixed model, which implies compound symmetry with \rho = \sigma^2_\pi/(\sigma^2_\pi+\sigma^2_\varepsilon).

Sphericity and epsilon. Sphericity requires \operatorname{Var}(Y_j - Y_{j'}) to be equal for all pairs j \neq j'. With \Sigma the k\times k covariance matrix and C a (k-1)\times k matrix of orthonormal contrasts, sphericity means C\Sigma C^\top = \lambda I. The Greenhouse–Geisser estimate is

\hat\varepsilon = \frac{\big(\sum_r \lambda_r\big)^2}{(k-1)\sum_r \lambda_r^2},

where the \lambda_r are the eigenvalues of C\hat\Sigma C^\top. It equals 1 under sphericity and has a floor of 1/(k-1) (the “lower bound” row). Mauchly’s statistic is W = \det(C\hat\Sigma C^\top)\big/\big[\operatorname{tr}(C\hat\Sigma C^\top)/(k-1)\big]^{k-1}. The book’s data give \hat\varepsilon = .867 and W = .761, matching the printed table.

Contrasts. For weights c with \sum_j c_j = 0, the contrast score L_i = \sum_j c_j Y_{ij} gives \text{SS}_L = n\bar L^2/\sum_j c_j^2, tested against that contrast’s own error, F(1, n-1) = n\bar L^2/s_L^2. Orthogonal contrasts partition \text{SS}_{\text{cond}}. The linear, quadratic and cubic contrasts give 69.77 + 300.18 + 666.41 = 1036.36. The single contrast c = (1, -3, 1, 1) gives 1,034.0 on its own.

Effect size. \eta^2_p = \text{SS}_{\text{cond}}/(\text{SS}_{\text{cond}}+\text{SS}_{\text{err}}) = 1036.42/1355.81 = .764. The book’s formula labels the numerator “SS within-subjects” but uses the conditions SS, which is the correct quantity. (Martin and Bridgmon 2012)

::: {.lead-kernel}
Measure the same people under several conditions and you can subtract out **who they are**. The stable
part of each person (heavier, lighter, keener, more tired) leaves the error term, and only each person's
*wobble around their own average* counts as noise. That is the whole appeal of **repeated-measures
ANOVA**. The cost is an extra assumption, **sphericity**, plus the design risks that come from measuring
in a fixed order over time.
:::

## Why it matters

A large share of psychology, health and education research measures people more than once: before and
after a programme, at three, six, nine and twelve months, or under each of several conditions in turn.
[@martin_bridgmon] Analysing those scores as if they came from different people is a mistake in both
directions. It throws away the most useful feature of the design, which is that each person can be
compared with themselves. And it treats correlated scores as independent, which breaks the arithmetic of
the ordinary [one-way ANOVA](../02-inference/comparing-groups.qmd).

Repeated-measures ANOVA (RM-ANOVA) is the classical answer. It extends the paired *t*-test from two
measurements to three or more. [@martin_bridgmon] It is also the simplest example of a pattern you will
meet again in [mixed-effects models](mixed-effects-models.qmd) and in
[fixed-effects panels](../05-causal-inference/panel-and-fixed-effects.qmd): remove each unit's own level,
then study what changes within the unit.

## Three designs, one structure

Martin & Bridgmon list three common uses of the within-subjects layout: [@martin_bridgmon]

1. **The same people measured repeatedly over time** (weight at 3, 6, 9 and 12 months).
2. **The same people measured under different conditions** (each participant tries every version of a task).
3. **Matched sets of people measured under different conditions** (pairs or triples matched on a
   background variable, each member assigned to a different condition).

All three share one structure: a table with one row per person (or matched set) and one column per
condition. The rows are not independent of each other's columns. Someone who loses a lot of weight in
one period tends to be someone who loses a lot in others. When a design has *both* a within-subjects
factor and a between-subjects factor (e.g. time × treatment group), the book calls it a **mixed** design.
[@martin_bridgmon]

## The idea: take the people out of the error

In a one-way ANOVA on independent groups, the total variation splits into two parts: *between
conditions* (the signal) and *within conditions* (the noise). Within-condition noise includes everything
that makes one person differ from another.

With repeated measures you can split the noise again. Some of it is just **stable differences between
people**. You can estimate each person's average across conditions and set that variation aside, because
it cannot be responsible for a *difference between conditions*: everyone appears in every condition. What
remains is the **person × condition** residual, the extent to which people respond to the conditions in
different ways. That residual becomes the error term: [@martin_bridgmon]

$$
\text{SS}_{\text{total}} = \text{SS}_{\text{conditions}} + \underbrace{\text{SS}_{\text{subjects}}}_{\text{set aside}} + \text{SS}_{\text{error}} .
$$

The F-ratio is then conditions over error, on $(k-1)$ and $(k-1)(n-1)$ degrees of freedom for $k$
conditions and $n$ people.

**How much this buys depends on one quantity: how strongly a person's scores correlate across
conditions.** If people are highly consistent (the heavy loser in one period is the heavy loser in every
period), then most of the within-condition noise is between-person, and removing it shrinks the error
dramatically. If people's scores bounce around independently from period to period, there is little
stable person-level variation to remove, and the repeated-measures analysis gains almost nothing over
treating the columns as separate groups. The figure makes this concrete.

## Worked example: does a weight-loss partner help?

Martin & Bridgmon's study (simulated, after Gorin et al. 2005) follows **35 overweight adults** in a
behavioural weight-control treatment for a year. [@martin_bridgmon] Each participant has a **support
partner**, a friend who is also in the programme. The design switches the partner on and off:

| Period (3 months each) | Condition | Mean weight loss (lb) | SD |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | treatment **with** partner | 10.314 | 2.246 |
| 2 | partner **removed** | 4.114 | 1.676 |
| 3 | partner **added back** | 10.600 | 1.631 |
| 4 | partner **continued** | 10.257 | 1.821 |

This is a **repeated-treatment design** in Shadish, Cook & Campbell's terms: introduce, remove,
reintroduce. [@martin_bridgmon] If the outcome drops when the partner leaves and recovers when the
partner returns, that on-off-on pattern is harder to explain by coincidence than a single before-after
change.

### The analysis, step by step

The book follows its six-step hypothesis-testing routine. [@martin_bridgmon]

- **Planning.** α = .01, because the study replicates earlier findings. An a priori power analysis in
  G\*Power used partial η² = .35 from a previous study, four measurements, and an assumed **correlation
  of .55 among the repeated measures**, and reported that just 6 participants would give power .80. The
  study has 35.
- **Screening.** One participant (#24) scores 16 lb in the add-back period, z = 3.31, past the ±3.29
  cut-off. Their other partner-period scores are 15 and 14, so the score is typical *for that person*,
  and it is kept. Skewness, kurtosis, Shapiro–Wilk and Q–Q plots all pass.
- **Sphericity.** Mauchly's W = .761, χ²(5) = 8.94, p = .112, so the assumption is retained (more on
  this below).
- **The omnibus test.** **F(3, 102) = 110.325, p < .001, partial η² = .764.**
- **Follow-ups.** Fisher's protected LSD pairwise comparisons show that the partner-removed period
  differs from each of the other three (each gap is 6.1 to 6.5 lb, all 99% CIs above zero), and the
  three partner periods do not differ from one another.

The book also works the whole partition by hand from the printed data: [@martin_bridgmon]

| Source | SS | df | MS | F |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Subjects | 150.73 | 34 | | |
| Conditions | 1,036.42 | 3 | 345.47 | 110.3 |
| Error (subjects × conditions) | 319.39 | 102 | 3.131 | |
| Total | 1,506.54 | 139 | | |

### What the subjects row tells you (our arithmetic)

Here is the instructive part. If you ignored the pairing and ran an ordinary one-way ANOVA on the four
columns, the subjects SS would fall back into the error: $(150.73 + 319.39)/136 = 3.457$, giving
$F \approx 345.5/3.457 \approx 99.9$. The repeated-measures F is 110.3. The design helped, **but only by
about 10%.**

The reason is in the data. Computed from the book's printed scores, the correlations between periods
average only about **.09**. The largest is .42, and two are slightly negative. The power analysis had
assumed .55. Under that assumption the error term would have been less than half the size of the
independent-groups error. In the actual data, a person's weight loss in one period says little about
their loss in the next, so there was little stable person-level variation to take out. The design is
still correct and the effect is huge, so nothing is lost here. But the a priori calculation that called
for six people rested on a correlation the data did not show. For a smaller effect, that gap would have
meant an underpowered study.

## Seeing it

```{python}
#| label: fig-rm-anova
#| fig-cap: "Left: all 35 participants in Martin & Bridgmon's weight-loss study (grey lines), with the condition means (orange). Removing the support partner pulls almost everyone down, and adding the partner back restores the level. Right: why the payoff of a repeated-measures design depends on consistency. Under compound symmetry the RM error mean square is the independent-groups one multiplied by (1 − ρ), where ρ is the correlation between a person's scores in different conditions. The power analysis assumed ρ = .55 (grey). The book's own data show ρ ≈ .09 (orange), so the error fell only from 3.46 to 3.13."
#| code-fold: true
#| code-summary: "Show the figure code"

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# Martin & Bridgmon (2012), Ch 8 data: 35 participants x 4 conditions (lb lost per 3-month period)
raw = """11 6 11 9  8 5 11 8  8 4 12 10  8 3 10 12  7 4 9 11  12 2 10 8  12 6 10 9  13 7 9 7
7 1 12 14  8 2 11 13  11 4 11 12  13 4 10 11  14 5 10 10  9 4 11 9  9 6 9 8  8 3 8 8
11 2 10 9  11 4 10 7  12 5 13 12  10 4 11 12  10 3 10 11  7 5 12 9  7 6 9 10  15 7 16 14
14 2 8 10  12 1 13 10  11 4 11 12  11 4 12 11  10 3 10 10  9 3 9 9  12 5 8 12  11 5 10 11
9 6 12 10  8 7 11 12  13 2 12 9"""
y = np.array(raw.split(), dtype=float).reshape(35, 4)
n, k = y.shape

# Variance partition by hand
grand = y.mean()
ss_tot = ((y - grand) ** 2).sum()
ss_cond = n * ((y.mean(0) - grand) ** 2).sum()
ss_subj = k * ((y.mean(1) - grand) ** 2).sum()
ss_err = ss_tot - ss_cond - ss_subj
mse_rm = ss_err / ((k - 1) * (n - 1))             # 3.131
mse_ind = (ss_subj + ss_err) / (n * k - k)        # 3.457: subjects folded back into error

C = np.cov(y.T)
off = C[~np.eye(k, dtype=bool)]
rho_data = off.mean() / np.diag(C).mean()         # average between-period correlation, ~ .09

fig, (ax1, ax2) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(9.6, 4.2))

# --- Left: spaghetti plot (small jitter so tied trajectories stay visible)
rng = np.random.default_rng(8)

ax1.set_title("Each line is one person")
ax1.legend(fontsize=8, frameon=False, loc="lower right")

# --- Right: error mean square as a function of within-person correlation
rho = np.linspace(0, 0.9, 200)
ax2.plot(rho, mse_ind * (1 - rho), color="#161613", lw=2.2, label="RM error mean square, σ²(1 − ρ)")
ax2.axhline(mse_ind, color="#5e5e54", ls="--", lw=1.3, label="independent-groups error, σ²")
ax2.plot(0.55, mse_ind * 0.45, "s", color="#8a8a7e", ms=8)

ax2.plot(rho_data, mse_rm, "o", color="#e8531d", ms=9)
```

## Sphericity: the assumption that comes with the gain

Independent-groups ANOVA assumes equal variances. RM-ANOVA needs a stronger condition because the
columns are correlated. Martin & Bridgmon state it as the variances being constant *and* the covariances
being constant across conditions, the pattern called **compound symmetry**. [@martin_bridgmon] Strictly,
**sphericity** is a slightly weaker condition: the variance of the *difference* between any two
conditions is the same for every pair. Compound symmetry is sufficient for sphericity; sphericity is what
the F test actually needs.

The intuition: the single error term in the F-ratio pools information about every pair of conditions.
That pooling is fair only if every pairwise difference is equally noisy. If some pairs of periods are
tightly linked (adjacent months, say) and others are not, one pooled error term is too small for some
comparisons and too large for others, and the F-test's Type I error rate drifts above its nominal level.

**Mauchly's test** checks the assumption; you want to *retain* its null hypothesis. [@martin_bridgmon]
When it fails, the remedy is to keep the F statistic but **shrink its degrees of freedom** by a
correction factor ε (epsilon), so that the critical value rises: [@martin_bridgmon]

| Correction | ε in the example | Corrected df | Character |
|---|---|---|---|
| none (sphericity assumed) | 1 | 3, 102 | |
| **Greenhouse–Geisser** | .867 | 2.60, 88.39 | more conservative, especially in small samples |
| **Huynh–Feldt** | .945 | 2.83, 96.36 | less conservative |
| lower bound | .333 | 1, 34 | the most pessimistic case |

Here every row gives p < .001, so the correction is moot. The example also shows how forgiving the
test can be: from the book's data, the four period variances range from 2.66 to 5.05 (nearly a factor
of two), and the covariances range from −0.24 to 1.25. That is visibly *not* compound symmetric, yet
Mauchly's test, which has little power at n = 35, did not reject. Many analysts therefore apply the
Greenhouse–Geisser or Huynh–Feldt correction routinely instead of letting Mauchly's p-value decide.

## Follow-ups: pairwise comparisons, trends, and contrasts

A significant omnibus F says only that the four means are not all equal. The book follows up three
ways. [@martin_bridgmon]

**Pairwise comparisons.** Fisher's protected LSD, which the book describes as a *liberal* procedure that
finds more differences than most. [@martin_bridgmon] In the hand calculation the book uses the pooled
error ($\text{MS}_E = 3.131$) for every pair, giving $t = 14.52$ for with-partner vs removed. The SPSS
table instead uses a separate standard error for each pair (.458 for that comparison). Those two agree
only when sphericity holds. If sphericity is doubtful, paired comparisons using each pair's own
differences are safer, because they do not borrow strength from the other pairs.

**Trend analysis.** When the conditions are ordered along a continuum, polynomial contrasts ask about
the *shape* of the means: linear, quadratic, cubic. The book reports a significant quadratic trend,
F(1, 34) = 114.58, partial η² = .771, reading the profile as a V. [@martin_bridgmon] But the same table
shows a **cubic** trend with an even larger F(1, 34) = 286.68 and a linear trend with F(1, 34) = 15.68.
All three are "significant" because none of them is the right description. The four means are three
equal highs and one low. That is not a polynomial in time.

**A planned contrast (our arithmetic).** The hypothesis was always *partner periods vs the no-partner
period*. Testing that single contrast directly (weights 1, −3, 1, 1) captures **1,034 of the 1,036**
condition sums of squares, F(1, 34) ≈ 324. One theory-driven question, asked once, does all the work
that the omnibus test, six pairwise tests and three trend tests did between them. This is the
general lesson of [multiple testing](../02-inference/multiple-testing.qmd): pre-specify the comparison
your hypothesis implies.

## What the design cannot rule out

The repeated-treatment design is a one-group quasi-experiment, and Martin & Bridgmon say so. Without a
control group or random assignment, **history** (outside events) and **maturation** (participants
changing on their own) can masquerade as treatment effects. Participants who notice the on-off cycle may
also react to the study itself. [@martin_bridgmon] There is a further problem specific to fixed orders.
Here, *partner removed* is always the second three-month block. Any effect of being six months into a
diet (a plateau, the summer holidays) is confounded with the absence of the partner. The
introduce-remove-reintroduce sequence weakens this worry, because a plateau would not explain the
recovery in period 3. It does not remove it. When conditions *can* be given in any order, randomising or
**counterbalancing** the order across participants turns order into a measurable factor instead of a
hidden confound. (See the [research designs](../00-overture/research-designs.qmd) entry for the full
list of validity threats.)

## The nonparametric and modern alternatives

- **Friedman's rank test** is the nonparametric analogue of RM-ANOVA, and the **Wilcoxon matched-pairs
  signed-ranks test** is the analogue of the paired *t*-test. Martin & Bridgmon use Friedman's test for a
  three-period on-off-on pain study whose scores fail the normality checks. [@martin_bridgmon] See
  [resampling and nonparametric tests](../02-inference/resampling-and-nonparametric-tests.qmd).
- **Mixed-effects models** handle the same data with a random intercept per person. They cope with
  missing occasions (RM-ANOVA drops anyone with a missing score), unequal spacing, and time-varying
  covariates, and they let you model the covariance structure directly instead of assuming sphericity.
  See [mixed-effects models](mixed-effects-models.qmd).

## Common pitfalls

::: {.callout-warning title="Where this goes wrong"}

- **Analysing repeated scores as independent groups.** It wastes the pairing when people are consistent,
  and it violates independence whatever the correlation. Use the within-subjects error term.
  [@martin_bridgmon]

- **Assuming the repeated-measures payoff instead of checking it.** The power gain comes from the
  correlation between a person's scores. A power analysis built on ρ = .55 when the data show ρ ≈ .09
  overstates the design's sensitivity by more than double. Look at the correlation matrix.

- **Letting a low-powered Mauchly test certify sphericity.** A non-significant Mauchly test at modest n
  is weak evidence. Variances that differ by a factor of two are a reason to apply Greenhouse–Geisser or
  Huynh–Feldt anyway.

- **Fitting polynomial trends to conditions that are not a quantity.** Trend analysis assumes equally
  spaced, ordered levels with a smooth relationship. An on-off-on sequence is not that, and quadratic
  *and* cubic terms can both come out "significant". Test the contrast your hypothesis names.

- **Reading a fixed-order design as an experiment.** When every participant gets the conditions in the
  same order, condition and time are confounded. History, maturation and reactivity remain open threats
  without a control group or counterbalancing. [@martin_bridgmon]

- **Leaning on "observed power".** The book reports post-hoc observed power of 1.000. It is a
  re-expression of the p-value and adds nothing ([why](../02-inference/statistical-power.qmd)).
  [@martin_bridgmon]

:::

## Appendix · the mathematics {.unnumbered}

::: {.math-appendix}

**The partition.** For $n$ subjects and $k$ conditions with scores $Y_{ij}$, subject means
$\bar Y_{i\cdot}$, condition means $\bar Y_{\cdot j}$ and grand mean $\bar Y$:

$$
\text{SS}_{\text{cond}} = n\sum_j(\bar Y_{\cdot j}-\bar Y)^2,\qquad
\text{SS}_{\text{subj}} = k\sum_i(\bar Y_{i\cdot}-\bar Y)^2,
$$
$$
\text{SS}_{\text{err}} = \sum_{i,j}\big(Y_{ij}-\bar Y_{i\cdot}-\bar Y_{\cdot j}+\bar Y\big)^2
= \text{SS}_{\text{tot}}-\text{SS}_{\text{cond}}-\text{SS}_{\text{subj}},
$$

and $F = \dfrac{\text{SS}_{\text{cond}}/(k-1)}{\text{SS}_{\text{err}}/\big((k-1)(n-1)\big)}$. The error term is
the subject × condition interaction: the part of each score explained neither by the person's level nor
by the condition's level. In the example: $1506.54 - 1036.42 - 150.73 = 319.39$ and
$F = 345.47/3.131 = 110.3$. [@martin_bridgmon]

**Why consistency shrinks the error.** Suppose every condition has variance $\sigma^2$ and every pair of
conditions has covariance $\sigma^2\rho$ (compound symmetry). Then the expected error mean square is

$$
\mathbb E[\text{MS}_{\text{err}}] = \sigma^2(1-\rho),
$$

while the independent-groups error estimates $\sigma^2$. Equivalently, the RM error equals the **average
variance minus the average covariance**. From the book's data: $3.457 - 0.325 = 3.131$, exactly the
printed $\text{MS}_E$. The F-ratio is multiplied by roughly $1/(1-\rho)$. That is 1.10 at the observed
$\rho \approx .09$ and 2.2 at the assumed $.55$.

**It is a two-way model.** RM-ANOVA is OLS of $Y_{ij}$ on condition dummies *and* a dummy for every
subject, $Y_{ij} = \mu + \alpha_j + \pi_i + \varepsilon_{ij}$, with no interaction. The subject dummies
are exactly the fixed effects of a [panel regression](../05-causal-inference/panel-and-fixed-effects.qmd);
treating $\pi_i$ as random with variance $\sigma^2_\pi$ gives the random-intercept
[mixed model](mixed-effects-models.qmd), which implies compound symmetry with
$\rho = \sigma^2_\pi/(\sigma^2_\pi+\sigma^2_\varepsilon)$.

**Sphericity and epsilon.** Sphericity requires $\operatorname{Var}(Y_j - Y_{j'})$ to be equal for all
pairs $j \neq j'$. With $\Sigma$ the $k\times k$ covariance matrix and $C$ a $(k-1)\times k$ matrix of
orthonormal contrasts, sphericity means $C\Sigma C^\top = \lambda I$. The Greenhouse–Geisser estimate is

$$
\hat\varepsilon = \frac{\big(\sum_r \lambda_r\big)^2}{(k-1)\sum_r \lambda_r^2},
$$

where the $\lambda_r$ are the eigenvalues of $C\hat\Sigma C^\top$. It equals 1 under sphericity and has a
floor of $1/(k-1)$ (the "lower bound" row). Mauchly's statistic is
$W = \det(C\hat\Sigma C^\top)\big/\big[\operatorname{tr}(C\hat\Sigma C^\top)/(k-1)\big]^{k-1}$. The
book's data give $\hat\varepsilon = .867$ and $W = .761$, matching the printed table.

**Contrasts.** For weights $c$ with $\sum_j c_j = 0$, the contrast score $L_i = \sum_j c_j Y_{ij}$ gives
$\text{SS}_L = n\bar L^2/\sum_j c_j^2$, tested against that contrast's own error,
$F(1, n-1) = n\bar L^2/s_L^2$. Orthogonal contrasts partition $\text{SS}_{\text{cond}}$. The linear,
quadratic and cubic contrasts give $69.77 + 300.18 + 666.41 = 1036.36$. The single contrast
$c = (1, -3, 1, 1)$ gives 1,034.0 on its own.

**Effect size.** $\eta^2_p = \text{SS}_{\text{cond}}/(\text{SS}_{\text{cond}}+\text{SS}_{\text{err}})
= 1036.42/1355.81 = .764$. The book's formula labels the numerator "SS within-subjects" but uses the
conditions SS, which is the correct quantity. [@martin_bridgmon]
