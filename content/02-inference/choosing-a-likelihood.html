Choosing a Likelihood: Stories, Checklists and Overdispersed Cousins

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source

Every common distribution is a short story about how data get made. Pick the story that matches your data, and when the people or places in it differ from one another, reach for the version that allows extra spread.

A likelihood is a claim about how your data were generated, and Lambert calls choosing it “the most important decision in Bayesian inference.” The familiar distributions each come with a checklist (discrete or continuous? a fixed number of trials? independent events?). Each of the three workhorses, the binomial, the Poisson and the normal, also has an overdispersed cousin built by letting a parameter vary across groups. Heterogeneity in the population becomes extra spread in the data, and a model without it will be too sure of itself.

Why it matters

The machinery explained what a likelihood is: the data-generating model, read backwards to weigh parameter values. This entry is about which one to write down. Lambert argues that texts often assume a familiarity with distributions “appropriate for statistics fanatics”, and that this makes Bayesian work look harder than it is. (Lambert 2018) The fix is to learn each distribution as a story with a checklist and to see how they are related. The choice is not cosmetic. The likelihood defines which parameters you will estimate, and if it allows too little variation, every interval you report will be too narrow. (Lambert 2018)

The same choice sits under every maximum-likelihood fit and every generalised linear model. Only the prior is specific to Bayesian inference.

Every distribution is a story with a checklist

Lambert introduces each likelihood through a checklist and a small story. (Lambert 2018) The stories are worth keeping, because they are how you recognise the distribution in your own data.

Several of the stories are about independence, and the checklist is where you notice when it fails. The Poisson Legionella count goes wrong if some “new” outbreaks were actually spread from an existing one. The binomial flu trial goes wrong if some volunteers have asthma and are unlikely to respond to the drug at all. (Lambert 2018)

Lambert also stresses that the distributions are all related: some are special cases of others, some are limits, some are transformations. (Lambert 2018) The Bernoulli is a binomial with one trial. The exponential is a gamma for one event, and its rate is the same \lambda as the Poisson rate for that process. The normal approximates the binomial and the Poisson when counts are large (common distributions walks through these links). Knowing the family tree makes the distributions easier to remember and helps when you need to move from one to another. (Lambert 2018)

The overdispersed cousins: heterogeneity becomes spread

The binomial, Poisson and normal each carry a strong restriction. The Poisson forces the variance to equal the mean. The binomial fixes the variance once you know n and \theta. The normal has thin tails. Real social data often break these restrictions for one reason: the units are not all alike. Lambert’s three cousins are each built in the same way, by letting a parameter vary from group to group and averaging over it. (Lambert 2018)

Poisson → negative binomial. Lambert imagines flu in three villages of equal size with a common infection rate \lambda = 10, scaled by how sociable each village is: a party-going student village (\theta = 1.35), a commuter village (\theta = 1) and an academic village (\theta = 0.65, everyone at home writing papers). The average \theta is 1, so the mean is still \lambda. But the distribution of cases across villages is wider than a Poisson with the same mean. Let \theta vary across many villages according to a gamma distribution with mean 1 and you get the negative binomial, sometimes called the overdispersed Poisson. Its extra parameter \kappa is an inverse dispersion: the larger \kappa, the closer it is to Poisson. (Lambert 2018) Lambert’s uses: everything the Poisson does, plus measles cases on an island or bank failures in a financial crisis, where one event makes the next more likely. (Lambert 2018) (The frequentist version of this problem, and the false precision of Poisson standard errors on clumped counts, is in count-data models.)

Binomial → beta-binomial. “The beta-binomial is to the binomial what the negative binomial is to the Poisson.” (Lambert 2018) Lambert’s example is a depression drug tested on patients with mild and severe forms of the condition, where the drug works better for mild cases. If a trial group may come from either population, the distribution of recoveries is an average of two binomials, and it is wider than a single binomial with the same mean. Let the success probability vary continuously, drawn from a beta distribution, and you have the beta-binomial. (Lambert 2018) Uses: breast-cancer prevalence across heterogeneous patient groups, or Republican votes pooled across many states. (Lambert 2018)

Normal → Student-t. Two neighbouring schools, the fee-paying Privilege High and the free Danum Comprehensive, have the same mean arithmetic score but different spreads. Merge them and the combined distribution’s “head will rise, its shoulders will fall and its tails will fatten.” (Lambert 2018) Combine many schools whose variances follow an inverse-gamma distribution and the result is exactly a Student-t, with degrees of freedom \nu controlling how fat the tails are. As \nu grows it becomes a normal. At \nu = 1 it is the Cauchy, whose mean and variance do not exist. Lambert notes that it is nonetheless a robust choice when extreme observations occur, and that Taleb’s The Black Swan argues for this kind of distribution. (Lambert 2018)

Show the figure code 
import math
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def poisson_pmf(k, lam):
    return np.array([math.exp(-lam) * lam ** int(x) / math.factorial(int(x)) for x in k])

def binom_pmf(k, n, p):
    return np.array([math.comb(n, int(x)) * p ** int(x) * (1 - p) ** (n - int(x)) for x in k])

def normal_pdf(x, mu, sd):
    return np.exp(-0.5 * ((x - mu) / sd) ** 2) / (sd * np.sqrt(2 * np.pi))

def mean_var(support, pmf):
    m = (support * pmf).sum()
    return m, ((support - m) ** 2 * pmf).sum()

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 3, figsize=(11.2, 3.9))

# --- Poisson vs the three-village mixture
k = np.arange(0, 31)
lam, thetas = 10.0, [1.35, 1.0, 0.65]
mix = np.mean([poisson_pmf(k, lam * t) for t in thetas], axis=0)
base = poisson_pmf(k, lam)
ax = axes[0]
ax.plot(k, base, "o", color="#161613", ms=4, label=f"Poisson, var {mean_var(k, base)[1]:.1f}")
ax.plot(k, mix, "-", color="#e8531d", lw=2.2, label=f"three villages, var {mean_var(k, mix)[1]:.1f}")
ax.set_title("Poisson → negative binomial")
ax.set_xlabel("flu cases in a village")
ax.set_ylabel("probability")
ax.legend(fontsize=8, frameon=False)

# --- Binomial vs a mild/severe mixture
k = np.arange(0, 11)
mixb = 0.5 * binom_pmf(k, 10, 0.8) + 0.5 * binom_pmf(k, 10, 0.3)
pbar = 0.55
baseb = binom_pmf(k, 10, pbar)
ax = axes[1]
ax.plot(k, baseb, "o", color="#161613", ms=5, label=f"binomial, var {mean_var(k, baseb)[1]:.2f}")
ax.plot(k, mixb, "-", color="#e8531d", lw=2.2, label=f"mild or severe, var {mean_var(k, mixb)[1]:.2f}")
ax.set_title("Binomial → beta-binomial")
ax.set_xlabel("recoveries out of 10")
ax.legend(fontsize=8, frameon=False, loc="lower center")

# --- Normal vs a two-school scale mixture (log scale shows the tails)
x = np.linspace(-90, 90, 600)
sds = [10.0, 25.0]
mixn = 0.5 * normal_pdf(x, 0, sds[0]) + 0.5 * normal_pdf(x, 0, sds[1])
sd_same = np.sqrt(np.mean(np.square(sds)))
basen = normal_pdf(x, 0, sd_same)
ax = axes[2]
ax.semilogy(x, basen, color="#161613", lw=1.8, label=f"normal, SD {sd_same:.1f}")
ax.semilogy(x, mixn, color="#e8531d", lw=2.2, label="two schools, same SD")
ax.set_ylim(1e-6, 0.05)
ax.set_title("Normal → Student-t")
ax.set_xlabel("score minus the common mean")
ax.set_ylabel("density (log scale)")
ax.legend(fontsize=8, frameon=False, loc="lower center")

fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()

The right panel shows why Lambert says the extra variability of the schools mixture “is not necessarily borne out in the variance”: we gave the normal the same SD on purpose, yet the mixture still has a taller peak and far heavier tails. The difference is in the fourth moment, the kurtosis. (Lambert 2018) A model that only matches the mean and variance can still badly underestimate how often extreme values happen.

A cautionary tale: the hedge fund

Lambert tells the story of a new analyst at a hedge fund asked to model a two-stock portfolio. If both stocks lose 10% or more on the same day, the portfolio goes bankrupt, and the boss has set a maximum acceptable risk of that happening. (Lambert 2018)

The analyst notices that the two returns move together and fits a bivariate normal by maximum likelihood, with two means, two variances and a correlation. A thousand simulated days from the fitted model show no bankruptcies, so the analyst recommends buying. The boss looks at the simulated returns next to the real ones and asks why the real data vary more than the fake data. The analyst refits with a bivariate Student-t, simulates again, and now several days land in the danger zone: the modelled risk is over the threshold. The recommendation reverses. (Lambert 2018)

The moral is Lambert’s: make sure the modelling choices “sufficiently account for extreme data,” and when real data show this behaviour (and, some would argue, even when they do not), there is a case for replacing normals with Student-t distributions. (Lambert 2018) The boss’s check, putting simulated data next to real data, is the posterior predictive check in miniature.

When parameters trade off against each other

Two-parameter likelihoods have a surface rather than a curve, and Lambert uses contour plots to show a recurring problem: ridges, where different parameter combinations fit the data equally well. (Lambert 2018)

  • Beta-binomial. The mean depends on the ratio of \alpha and \beta, so \alpha = \beta = 1 and \alpha = \beta = 2 give the same mean of 5 out of 10. The likelihood surface has a long diagonal ridge, and the two parameters are hard to identify separately. (Lambert 2018)
  • Gamma. In the (\alpha, \beta) parameterisation the mean is \alpha/\beta, so (2, 1) and (4, 2) both give a mean of 2. The estimates are strongly correlated, which is a problem in theory (identification) and in practice: many MCMC methods are slow to converge on such ridges. Reparameterising in terms of the mean and standard deviation reduces the correlation, and Lambert prefers that form because it is easier to interpret. (Lambert 2018)
  • Student-t. Both \sigma and \nu control spread, so with a small sample “there is not enough information in the data to differentiate between the effects of σ and ν.” The mean, by contrast, is nearly uncorrelated with either. (Lambert 2018)

The practical lesson: a ridge in the likelihood is something to plan around, not something to discover when the sampler stalls. A good parameterisation can make a slow model fast without changing what it says.

Priors for covariance matrices: LKJ over Wishart

Multivariate likelihoods such as the bivariate normal, and especially hierarchical models, need a prior on a whole covariance matrix. That is harder than it sounds, because only some matrices qualify: the diagonal must be positive, the matrix symmetric, and each squared covariance smaller than the product of the two variances. Formally, the matrix must be symmetric and positive definite. (Lambert 2018)

For a long time the default choice was the Wishart or inverse-Wishart, mainly because they are conjugate to the multivariate normal. Lambert recommends instead the LKJ distribution, combined with a decomposition: write the covariance matrix as a vector of scales \tau multiplying a correlation matrix \Omega, put a weakly informative prior on each scale, and an LKJ prior on \Omega. (Lambert 2018)

His example is comorbidity among four conditions (major depression, schizophrenia, OCD and anorexia), measured as four diagnostic scores for each of 1,000 patients. The LKJ prior has one parameter, \eta: (Lambert 2018)

  • \eta = 1: every admissible correlation matrix is equally likely. In more than two dimensions this still makes each individual correlation peak at zero, simply because it is easier to build a valid matrix when the correlations are small. The implied belief: the conditions probably occur independently, without ruling out comorbidity.
  • \eta > 1: matrices closer to the identity are favoured, so the correlations are pulled towards zero. Little comorbidity.
  • \eta < 1: weight moves towards strong correlations. At \eta = 0.1 for a 4 × 4 matrix, each correlation is roughly uniform between −1 and 1. Comorbidity is plausible.

The inverse-Wishart’s problem is that its joint behaviour can be strange even when its marginals look reasonable. With few degrees of freedom it puts a lot of weight on the corners: a strong negative correlation between depression and schizophrenia together with a strong positive one between schizophrenia and OCD, and equal weight on the exact opposite pattern. “It seems odd for us to specify a prior distribution that gives equal weight to these completely opposing views!” It also uses a single parameter to set both the variances and the correlations. The Wishart is better behaved but has the same coupling. Lambert’s advice is to use the LKJ unless conjugacy is needed for speed. (Lambert 2018)

The price of robustness

Lambert’s summary returns to one question: how much variability to allow for. He recommends the robust versions throughout (negative binomial over Poisson, Student-t over normal, LKJ over Wishart) and advises “always err on the side of robustness.” (Lambert 2018) The cost is real, though. Robust models are more complex and can make modern samplers run significantly longer. (Lambert 2018) How robust a model needs to be is a question for data, and the tools for answering it are model checking and comparison. Lambert also points out that when none of the standard distributions fits, a suitable one can often be built from these as building blocks, which is exactly how the cousins were made. (Lambert 2018)

Common pitfalls

WarningWhere this goes wrong

  • Defaulting to the distribution you know. Poisson for every count and normal for every continuous outcome is a choice, and often a bad one. Check the story against your data: are the units alike, are events independent, are there extremes? (Lambert 2018)

  • Matching the mean and variance and stopping. A mixture can share a normal’s mean and SD and still have much fatter tails. If extremes matter, as in a bankruptcy threshold, check the tails directly by simulating from the fitted model. (Lambert 2018)

  • Assuming heterogeneity always means overdispersion. The extra spread comes from a parameter that is shared within the group being counted: a village’s sociability, a trial group’s severity. Our note: if each individual independently has a different success probability, the count of successes is actually less variable than a binomial with the average probability. The cousin to reach for depends on where the heterogeneity sits.

  • Ignoring ridges in the likelihood. Beta-binomial (\alpha, \beta), gamma (\alpha, \beta) and Student-t (\sigma, \nu) all have parameter pairs that trade off. Expect wide, correlated posteriors and slow samplers; reparameterise where you can. (Lambert 2018)

  • Using an inverse-Wishart prior without looking at it. Its marginals can look harmless while the joint distribution favours extreme and contradictory correlations. Simulate from the prior, or use LKJ with separate scales. (Lambert 2018)

  • Treating “robust” as free. Extra parameters cost computation and can be weakly identified. Erring towards robustness is good advice; checking whether the data needed it is the rest of the job. (Lambert 2018)

Appendix · the mathematics

All three cousins follow from the law of total variance, \operatorname{Var}(X) = \mathbb E[\operatorname{Var}(X\mid\theta)] + \operatorname{Var}(\mathbb E[X\mid\theta]): the first term is the parent distribution’s own noise, the second is the extra spread contributed by heterogeneity. (The derivations below are ours; Lambert states the resulting distributions.)

Negative binomial. Let X\mid\theta \sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda\theta) with \theta \sim \Gamma(\kappa, 1/\kappa), so \mathbb E[\theta] = 1 and \operatorname{Var}(\theta) = 1/\kappa. (Lambert 2018) Then

\mathbb E[X] = \lambda, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(X) = \lambda + \lambda^2\operatorname{Var}(\theta) = \lambda + \frac{\lambda^2}{\kappa} .

As \kappa\to\infty the second term vanishes and the Poisson returns. In the three-village example, \operatorname{Var}(\theta) = (0.35^2 + 0 + 0.35^2)/3 \approx 0.082, so the variance is about 10 + 100(0.082) \approx 18.2 instead of 10.

Beta-binomial. Let X\mid\theta \sim \text{Binomial}(n,\theta) with \theta\sim\text{Beta}(\alpha,\beta), and write \mu = \alpha/(\alpha+\beta). Then

\mathbb E[X] = n\mu, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(X) = n\mu(1-\mu)\,\frac{\alpha+\beta+n}{\alpha+\beta+1} .

The factor on the right is at least 1, equals 1 when n = 1, and tends to 1 as \alpha+\beta\to\infty (the beta concentrates on \mu). The mean depends only on \alpha/(\alpha+\beta), which is the ridge Lambert describes.

Student-t as a scale mixture. Let X\mid\sigma^2 \sim \mathcal N(\mu, s^2\sigma^2) with \sigma^2 \sim \text{Inv-}\Gamma(\nu/2, \nu/2). (Lambert 2018) Integrating out \sigma^2 gives a Student-t with location \mu, scale s and \nu degrees of freedom. For \nu > 2 its variance is s^2\nu/(\nu-2); for \nu \le 1 the mean does not exist (the Cauchy case).

Kurtosis of the two-school mixture. For an equal mixture of \mathcal N(0,\sigma_1^2) and \mathcal N(0,\sigma_2^2), the variance is \bar v = (\sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2)/2 and the fourth moment is 3(\sigma_1^4+\sigma_2^4)/2, so the kurtosis is 3(\sigma_1^4+\sigma_2^4)/(2\bar v^2) \ge 3, with equality only when \sigma_1 = \sigma_2. For SDs 10 and 25 it is about 4.6, against 3 for the normal.

Gamma parameterisations. For \Gamma(\alpha,\beta) with rate \beta, the mean is \alpha/\beta and the variance \alpha/\beta^2. Solving for (\alpha,\beta) in terms of the mean \mu and SD \sigma gives \alpha = \mu^2/\sigma^2, \beta = \mu/\sigma^2, which is the reparameterisation that reduces the correlation between the two parameters. (Lambert 2018)

LKJ. With \Sigma = \operatorname{diag}(\tau)\,\Omega\,\operatorname{diag}(\tau), the LKJ density on a correlation matrix is

p(\Omega\mid\eta) \propto \det(\Omega)^{\eta-1} .

The determinant is largest (1) for the identity and shrinks towards 0 as correlations grow, so \eta > 1 favours weak correlations, \eta < 1 strong ones, and \eta = 1 is uniform over valid matrices. (Lambert 2018) Lambert reads the determinant as a “generalised variance” (or generalised correlation, for \Omega): high when the components are free to vary independently.

::: {.lead-kernel}
A likelihood is a **claim about how your data were generated**, and Lambert calls choosing it *"the most
important decision in Bayesian inference."* The familiar distributions each come with a checklist (discrete
or continuous? a fixed number of trials? independent events?). Each of the three workhorses, the binomial,
the Poisson and the normal, also has an **overdispersed cousin** built by letting a parameter vary across
groups. Heterogeneity in the population becomes extra spread in the data, and a model without it will be
too sure of itself.
:::

## Why it matters

[The machinery](bayesian-machinery.qmd) explained what a likelihood *is*: the data-generating model, read
backwards to weigh parameter values. This entry is about *which one to write down*. Lambert argues that
texts often assume a familiarity with distributions *"appropriate for statistics fanatics"*, and that this
makes Bayesian work look harder than it is. [@lambert_bayes] The fix is to learn each distribution as a
**story with a checklist** and to see how they are related. The choice is not cosmetic. The likelihood
defines which parameters you will estimate, and if it allows too little variation, every interval you
report will be too narrow. [@lambert_bayes]

The same choice sits under every [maximum-likelihood](maximum-likelihood.qmd) fit and every
[generalised linear model](../03-regression/generalized-linear-models.qmd). Only the prior is specific to
Bayesian inference.

## Every distribution is a story with a checklist

Lambert introduces each likelihood through a checklist and a small story. [@lambert_bayes] The stories are
worth keeping, because they are how you recognise the distribution in your own data.

| Distribution | Checklist | Lambert's story |
|---|---|---|
| **Bernoulli** | discrete; one trial; two outcomes | will this horse win its race? |
| **Binomial** | a fixed number of independent trials, same success probability, count the successes | 10 volunteers given a flu drug; how many recover in a week? 5 recoveries can arise in $\binom{10}{5} = 252$ ways |
| **Poisson** | counts of events in a fixed time or space; events independent, at a constant rate | new Legionella outbreaks worldwide per year |
| **Multinomial** | a fixed number of trials, $k \ge 2$ categories | blood types A, B and O in a sample |
| **Normal** | continuous, practically unbounded, the sum of many additive factors | body temperature, shaped by exercise, weather, water drunk, genes… |
| **Exponential** | non-negative waiting times between independent events at a constant rate | years between new Ebola outbreaks |
| **Gamma** | non-negative; more flexible than the exponential; time for $n$ events | the total time three lottery winners take to claim prizes |
| **Multivariate normal** | a vector of continuous outcomes with means and a covariance matrix | the daily returns of two stocks |

: The likelihood checklists, condensed from Lambert Ch 8. {#tbl-likelihood-checklists}

Several of the stories are about independence, and the checklist is where you notice when it fails. The
Poisson Legionella count goes wrong if some "new" outbreaks were actually spread from an existing one. The
binomial flu trial goes wrong if some volunteers have asthma and are unlikely to respond to the drug at all.
[@lambert_bayes]

Lambert also stresses that the distributions are **all related**: some are special cases of others,
some are limits, some are transformations. [@lambert_bayes] The Bernoulli is a binomial with one trial. The
exponential is a gamma for one event, and its rate is the same $\lambda$ as the Poisson rate for that
process. The normal approximates the binomial and the Poisson when counts are large
([common distributions](../01-foundations/common-distributions.qmd) walks through these links). Knowing
the family tree makes the distributions easier to remember and helps when you need to move from one to
another. [@lambert_bayes]

## The overdispersed cousins: heterogeneity becomes spread

The binomial, Poisson and normal each carry a strong restriction. The Poisson forces the **variance to
equal the mean**. The binomial fixes the variance once you know $n$ and $\theta$. The normal has thin tails.
Real social data often break these restrictions for one reason: **the units are not all alike.** Lambert's
three cousins are each built in the same way, by letting a parameter vary from group to group and
averaging over it. [@lambert_bayes]

**Poisson → negative binomial.** Lambert imagines flu in three villages of equal size with a common
infection rate $\lambda = 10$, scaled by how sociable each village is: a party-going student village
($\theta = 1.35$), a commuter village ($\theta = 1$) and an academic village ($\theta = 0.65$, everyone at home
writing papers). The average $\theta$ is 1, so the mean is still $\lambda$. But the distribution of cases
across villages is **wider than a Poisson with the same mean.** Let $\theta$ vary across many villages
according to a gamma distribution with mean 1 and you get the **negative binomial**, sometimes called the
*overdispersed Poisson*. Its extra parameter $\kappa$ is an **inverse** dispersion: the larger $\kappa$,
the closer it is to Poisson. [@lambert_bayes] Lambert's uses: everything the Poisson does, plus measles
cases on an island or bank failures in a financial crisis, where one event makes the next more likely.
[@lambert_bayes] (The frequentist version of this problem, and the false precision of Poisson standard
errors on clumped counts, is in [count-data models](../03-regression/count-data-models.qmd).)

**Binomial → beta-binomial.** *"The beta-binomial is to the binomial what the negative binomial is to the
Poisson."* [@lambert_bayes] Lambert's example is a depression drug tested on patients with mild and
severe forms of the condition, where the drug works better for mild cases. If a trial group may come from
either population, the distribution of recoveries is an average of two binomials, and it is wider than a
single binomial with the same mean. Let the success probability vary continuously, drawn from a beta
distribution, and you have the beta-binomial. [@lambert_bayes] Uses: breast-cancer prevalence across
heterogeneous patient groups, or Republican votes pooled across many states. [@lambert_bayes]

**Normal → Student-t.** Two neighbouring schools, the fee-paying *Privilege High* and the free *Danum
Comprehensive*, have the same mean arithmetic score but different spreads. Merge them and the combined
distribution's *"head will rise, its shoulders will fall and its tails will fatten."* [@lambert_bayes]
Combine many schools whose variances follow an inverse-gamma distribution and the result is exactly a
**Student-t**, with degrees of freedom $\nu$ controlling how fat the tails are. As $\nu$ grows it becomes a
normal. At $\nu = 1$ it is the **Cauchy**, whose mean and variance do not exist. Lambert notes that it is
nonetheless a robust choice when extreme observations occur, and that Taleb's *The Black Swan* argues for
this kind of distribution. [@lambert_bayes]

```{python}
#| label: fig-overdispersed-cousins
#| fig-cap: "Each cousin is a mixture over heterogeneous groups (our computation, using Lambert's three stories; the depression-drug success rates and school SDs are illustrative). Left: flu cases in Lambert's three villages (λ = 10, θ = 1.35, 1, 0.65) against a Poisson with the same mean. Middle: recoveries out of 10 when a trial group is equally likely to be mild (θ = 0.8) or severe (θ = 0.3), against a binomial with the same mean. Right: test scores from two schools with the same mean and SDs 10 and 25, against a normal with the same mean and SD, on a log scale so the tails show. In every panel the mixture (orange) is wider than its parent (black)."
#| code-fold: true
#| code-summary: "Show the figure code"

import math
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def poisson_pmf(k, lam):
    return np.array([math.exp(-lam) * lam ** int(x) / math.factorial(int(x)) for x in k])

def binom_pmf(k, n, p):
    return np.array([math.comb(n, int(x)) * p ** int(x) * (1 - p) ** (n - int(x)) for x in k])

def normal_pdf(x, mu, sd):
    return np.exp(-0.5 * ((x - mu) / sd) ** 2) / (sd * np.sqrt(2 * np.pi))

def mean_var(support, pmf):
    m = (support * pmf).sum()
    return m, ((support - m) ** 2 * pmf).sum()

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 3, figsize=(11.2, 3.9))

# --- Poisson vs the three-village mixture
k = np.arange(0, 31)
lam, thetas = 10.0, [1.35, 1.0, 0.65]
mix = np.mean([poisson_pmf(k, lam * t) for t in thetas], axis=0)
base = poisson_pmf(k, lam)
ax = axes[0]
ax.plot(k, base, "o", color="#161613", ms=4, label=f"Poisson, var {mean_var(k, base)[1]:.1f}")
ax.plot(k, mix, "-", color="#e8531d", lw=2.2, label=f"three villages, var {mean_var(k, mix)[1]:.1f}")
ax.set_title("Poisson → negative binomial")
ax.set_xlabel("flu cases in a village")
ax.set_ylabel("probability")
ax.legend(fontsize=8, frameon=False)

# --- Binomial vs a mild/severe mixture
k = np.arange(0, 11)
mixb = 0.5 * binom_pmf(k, 10, 0.8) + 0.5 * binom_pmf(k, 10, 0.3)
pbar = 0.55
baseb = binom_pmf(k, 10, pbar)
ax = axes[1]
ax.plot(k, baseb, "o", color="#161613", ms=5, label=f"binomial, var {mean_var(k, baseb)[1]:.2f}")
ax.plot(k, mixb, "-", color="#e8531d", lw=2.2, label=f"mild or severe, var {mean_var(k, mixb)[1]:.2f}")
ax.set_title("Binomial → beta-binomial")
ax.set_xlabel("recoveries out of 10")
ax.legend(fontsize=8, frameon=False, loc="lower center")

# --- Normal vs a two-school scale mixture (log scale shows the tails)
x = np.linspace(-90, 90, 600)
sds = [10.0, 25.0]
mixn = 0.5 * normal_pdf(x, 0, sds[0]) + 0.5 * normal_pdf(x, 0, sds[1])
sd_same = np.sqrt(np.mean(np.square(sds)))
basen = normal_pdf(x, 0, sd_same)
ax = axes[2]
ax.semilogy(x, basen, color="#161613", lw=1.8, label=f"normal, SD {sd_same:.1f}")
ax.semilogy(x, mixn, color="#e8531d", lw=2.2, label="two schools, same SD")
ax.set_ylim(1e-6, 0.05)
ax.set_title("Normal → Student-t")
ax.set_xlabel("score minus the common mean")
ax.set_ylabel("density (log scale)")
ax.legend(fontsize=8, frameon=False, loc="lower center")

fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

The right panel shows why Lambert says the extra variability of the schools mixture *"is not necessarily
borne out in the variance"*: we gave the normal the **same** SD on purpose, yet the mixture still has a
taller peak and far heavier tails. The difference is in the fourth moment, the kurtosis.
[@lambert_bayes] A model that only matches the mean and variance can still badly underestimate how often
extreme values happen.

## A cautionary tale: the hedge fund

Lambert tells the story of a new analyst at a hedge fund asked to model a two-stock portfolio. If both
stocks lose 10% or more on the same day, the portfolio goes bankrupt, and the boss has set a maximum
acceptable risk of that happening. [@lambert_bayes]

The analyst notices that the two returns move together and fits a **bivariate normal** by maximum
likelihood, with two means, two variances and a correlation. A thousand simulated days from the fitted
model show **no** bankruptcies, so the analyst recommends buying. The boss looks at the simulated returns
next to the real ones and asks why the real data vary more than the fake data. The analyst refits with a
**bivariate Student-t**, simulates again, and now several days land in the danger zone: the modelled risk is
over the threshold. The recommendation reverses. [@lambert_bayes]

The moral is Lambert's: make sure the modelling choices *"sufficiently account for extreme data,"* and
when real data show this behaviour (and, some would argue, even when they do not), there is a case for
replacing normals with Student-t distributions. [@lambert_bayes] The boss's check, putting simulated data
next to real data, is the [posterior predictive check](bayesian-model-comparison.qmd) in miniature.

## When parameters trade off against each other

Two-parameter likelihoods have a surface rather than a curve, and Lambert uses contour plots to show a
recurring problem: **ridges**, where different parameter combinations fit the data equally well.
[@lambert_bayes]

- **Beta-binomial.** The mean depends on the *ratio* of $\alpha$ and $\beta$, so $\alpha = \beta = 1$ and
  $\alpha = \beta = 2$ give the same mean of 5 out of 10. The likelihood surface has a long diagonal ridge,
  and the two parameters are hard to identify separately. [@lambert_bayes]
- **Gamma.** In the $(\alpha, \beta)$ parameterisation the mean is $\alpha/\beta$, so $(2, 1)$ and $(4, 2)$
  both give a mean of 2. The estimates are strongly correlated, which is a problem in theory
  (identification) and in practice: many MCMC methods are **slow to converge** on such ridges.
  Reparameterising in terms of the mean and standard deviation reduces the correlation, and Lambert
  prefers that form because it is easier to interpret. [@lambert_bayes]
- **Student-t.** Both $\sigma$ and $\nu$ control spread, so with a small sample *"there is not enough
  information in the data to differentiate between the effects of σ and ν."* The mean, by contrast, is
  nearly uncorrelated with either. [@lambert_bayes]

The practical lesson: a ridge in the likelihood is something to plan around, not something to discover
when the sampler stalls. A good [parameterisation](mcmc-in-depth.qmd) can make a slow model fast without
changing what it says.

## Priors for covariance matrices: LKJ over Wishart

Multivariate likelihoods such as the bivariate normal, and especially [hierarchical
models](hierarchical-bayesian-models.qmd), need a prior on a whole **covariance matrix**. That is harder than
it sounds, because only some matrices qualify: the diagonal must be positive, the matrix symmetric, and each
squared covariance smaller than the product of the two variances. Formally, the matrix must be symmetric and
positive definite. [@lambert_bayes]

For a long time the default choice was the **Wishart** or **inverse-Wishart**, mainly because they are
conjugate to the multivariate normal. Lambert recommends instead the **LKJ** distribution, combined with a
decomposition: write the covariance matrix as a vector of scales $\tau$ multiplying a **correlation**
matrix $\Omega$, put a weakly informative prior on each scale, and an LKJ prior on $\Omega$. [@lambert_bayes]

His example is comorbidity among four conditions (major depression, schizophrenia, OCD and anorexia),
measured as four diagnostic scores for each of 1,000 patients. The LKJ prior has one parameter, $\eta$:
[@lambert_bayes]

- **$\eta = 1$**: every admissible correlation matrix is equally likely. In more than two dimensions this
  still makes each individual correlation **peak at zero**, simply because it is easier to build a valid
  matrix when the correlations are small. The implied belief: the conditions probably occur
  independently, without ruling out comorbidity.
- **$\eta > 1$**: matrices closer to the identity are favoured, so the correlations are pulled towards zero.
  Little comorbidity.
- **$\eta < 1$**: weight moves towards strong correlations. At $\eta = 0.1$ for a 4 × 4 matrix, each
  correlation is roughly uniform between −1 and 1. Comorbidity is plausible.

The inverse-Wishart's problem is that its **joint** behaviour can be strange even when its marginals look
reasonable. With few degrees of freedom it puts a lot of weight on the corners: a strong negative
correlation between depression and schizophrenia together with a strong positive one between schizophrenia
and OCD, and *equal* weight on the exact opposite pattern. *"It seems odd for us to specify a prior
distribution that gives equal weight to these completely opposing views!"* It also uses a single parameter
to set both the variances and the correlations. The Wishart is better behaved but has the same coupling.
Lambert's advice is to use the LKJ unless conjugacy is needed for speed. [@lambert_bayes]

## The price of robustness

Lambert's summary returns to one question: **how much variability to allow for.** He recommends the robust
versions throughout (negative binomial over Poisson, Student-t over normal, LKJ over Wishart) and advises
*"always err on the side of robustness."* [@lambert_bayes] The cost is real, though. Robust models are
more complex and can make modern samplers run significantly longer. [@lambert_bayes] How robust a model needs
to be is a question for data, and the tools for answering it are [model checking and
comparison](bayesian-model-comparison.qmd). Lambert also points out that when none of the standard
distributions fits, a suitable one can often be **built from these as building blocks**, which is exactly
how the cousins were made. [@lambert_bayes]

## Common pitfalls

::: {.callout-warning title="Where this goes wrong"}

- **Defaulting to the distribution you know.** Poisson for every count and normal for every continuous
  outcome is a choice, and often a bad one. Check the story against your data: are the units alike, are
  events independent, are there extremes? [@lambert_bayes]

- **Matching the mean and variance and stopping.** A mixture can share a normal's mean and SD and still
  have much fatter tails. If extremes matter, as in a bankruptcy threshold, check the tails directly by
  simulating from the fitted model. [@lambert_bayes]

- **Assuming heterogeneity always means overdispersion.** The extra spread comes from a parameter that is
  **shared within the group being counted**: a village's sociability, a trial group's severity. Our note:
  if each individual independently has a different success probability, the count of successes is
  actually *less* variable than a binomial with the average probability. The cousin to reach for depends on
  where the heterogeneity sits.

- **Ignoring ridges in the likelihood.** Beta-binomial $(\alpha, \beta)$, gamma $(\alpha, \beta)$ and
  Student-t $(\sigma, \nu)$ all have parameter pairs that trade off. Expect wide, correlated posteriors and
  slow samplers; reparameterise where you can. [@lambert_bayes]

- **Using an inverse-Wishart prior without looking at it.** Its marginals can look harmless while the joint
  distribution favours extreme and contradictory correlations. Simulate from the prior, or use LKJ with
  separate scales. [@lambert_bayes]

- **Treating "robust" as free.** Extra parameters cost computation and can be weakly identified. Erring
  towards robustness is good advice; checking whether the data needed it is the rest of the job.
  [@lambert_bayes]

:::

## Appendix · the mathematics {.unnumbered}

::: {.math-appendix}

All three cousins follow from the **law of total variance**,
$\operatorname{Var}(X) = \mathbb E[\operatorname{Var}(X\mid\theta)] + \operatorname{Var}(\mathbb E[X\mid\theta])$:
the first term is the parent distribution's own noise, the second is the extra spread contributed by
heterogeneity. (The derivations below are ours; Lambert states the resulting distributions.)

**Negative binomial.** Let $X\mid\theta \sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda\theta)$ with
$\theta \sim \Gamma(\kappa, 1/\kappa)$, so $\mathbb E[\theta] = 1$ and $\operatorname{Var}(\theta) = 1/\kappa$.
[@lambert_bayes] Then

$$
\mathbb E[X] = \lambda, \qquad
\operatorname{Var}(X) = \lambda + \lambda^2\operatorname{Var}(\theta) = \lambda + \frac{\lambda^2}{\kappa} .
$$

As $\kappa\to\infty$ the second term vanishes and the Poisson returns. In the three-village example,
$\operatorname{Var}(\theta) = (0.35^2 + 0 + 0.35^2)/3 \approx 0.082$, so the variance is about
$10 + 100(0.082) \approx 18.2$ instead of 10.

**Beta-binomial.** Let $X\mid\theta \sim \text{Binomial}(n,\theta)$ with $\theta\sim\text{Beta}(\alpha,\beta)$,
and write $\mu = \alpha/(\alpha+\beta)$. Then

$$
\mathbb E[X] = n\mu, \qquad
\operatorname{Var}(X) = n\mu(1-\mu)\,\frac{\alpha+\beta+n}{\alpha+\beta+1} .
$$

The factor on the right is at least 1, equals 1 when $n = 1$, and tends to 1 as $\alpha+\beta\to\infty$
(the beta concentrates on $\mu$). The mean depends only on $\alpha/(\alpha+\beta)$, which is the ridge
Lambert describes.

**Student-t as a scale mixture.** Let $X\mid\sigma^2 \sim \mathcal N(\mu, s^2\sigma^2)$ with
$\sigma^2 \sim \text{Inv-}\Gamma(\nu/2, \nu/2)$. [@lambert_bayes] Integrating out $\sigma^2$ gives a
Student-t with location $\mu$, scale $s$ and $\nu$ degrees of freedom. For $\nu > 2$ its variance is
$s^2\nu/(\nu-2)$; for $\nu \le 1$ the mean does not exist (the Cauchy case).

**Kurtosis of the two-school mixture.** For an equal mixture of $\mathcal N(0,\sigma_1^2)$ and
$\mathcal N(0,\sigma_2^2)$, the variance is $\bar v = (\sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2)/2$ and the fourth moment is
$3(\sigma_1^4+\sigma_2^4)/2$, so the kurtosis is $3(\sigma_1^4+\sigma_2^4)/(2\bar v^2) \ge 3$, with equality
only when $\sigma_1 = \sigma_2$. For SDs 10 and 25 it is about 4.6, against 3 for the normal.

**Gamma parameterisations.** For $\Gamma(\alpha,\beta)$ with rate $\beta$, the mean is $\alpha/\beta$ and the
variance $\alpha/\beta^2$. Solving for $(\alpha,\beta)$ in terms of the mean $\mu$ and SD $\sigma$ gives
$\alpha = \mu^2/\sigma^2$, $\beta = \mu/\sigma^2$, which is the reparameterisation that reduces the
correlation between the two parameters. [@lambert_bayes]

**LKJ.** With $\Sigma = \operatorname{diag}(\tau)\,\Omega\,\operatorname{diag}(\tau)$, the LKJ density on a
correlation matrix is

$$
p(\Omega\mid\eta) \propto \det(\Omega)^{\eta-1} .
$$

The determinant is largest (1) for the identity and shrinks towards 0 as correlations grow, so $\eta > 1$
favours weak correlations, $\eta < 1$ strong ones, and $\eta = 1$ is uniform over valid matrices.
[@lambert_bayes] Lambert reads the determinant as a "generalised variance" (or generalised correlation,
for $\Omega$): high when the components are free to vary independently.

:::
