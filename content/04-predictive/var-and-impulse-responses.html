Vector Autoregression: VAR, Granger & Impulse Responses

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source

Stop privileging one variable as ‘the’ outcome. Let every series explain every other, then ask the system how a shock ripples through it.

ARIMA models one series from its own past. But macro and social systems are webs: output, inflation, and interest rates each move the others. Vector autoregression (VAR) treats them symmetrically — every variable is regressed on the lagged values of all variables, none privileged as exogenous. From that one democratic system come three tools: Granger causality (does the past of X help predict Y?), impulse responses (how does a shock propagate?), and variance decomposition (which shocks drive which series?).

Why it matters

Old large-scale macro models hard-wired which variables were “causes” and which “effects” — restrictions Christopher Sims (1980) attacked as “incredible,” lacking real theoretical basis (Enders 2014). His atheoretical alternative: treat every variable as endogenous and let the data speak. The VAR became the workhorse of empirical macro and a staple of political science and finance — and its innovation accounting (impulse responses + variance decomposition) is how researchers read dynamic cause-and-effect within a system without imposing a dubious structural story.

The VAR(p) system

A VAR of order p models a vector of n variables \mathbf{x}_t as a linear function of its own p lags — each equation containing the lags of every variable (Enders 2014). The crux is the distinction between two forms:

Structural (primitive) VAR — includes contemporaneous effects among variables, with pure, uncorrelated structural shocks \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_t:

B\mathbf{x}_t = \Gamma_0 + \Gamma_1 \mathbf{x}_{t-1} + \cdots + \Gamma_p \mathbf{x}_{t-p} + \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_t .

Reduced-form VAR — premultiply by B^{-1} to get the estimable system:

\mathbf{x}_t = A_0 + \sum_{i=1}^p A_i\,\mathbf{x}_{t-i} + \mathbf{e}_t .

The catch: the reduced-form errors \mathbf{e}_t = B^{-1}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_t are composites of the structural shocks, so they are correlated across equations and have no direct economic interpretation (Enders 2014). That correlation is the whole identification problem (below).

Estimation and lag length

Because every equation has the identical set of regressors (the same lags of all variables), OLS equation-by-equation is consistent and asymptotically efficient — no system estimator needed (Enders 2014). The real choice is the lag length p: too few causes misspecification, too many wastes degrees of freedom (Enders 2014). Pick p to minimise an information criterion, \text{AIC} = T\ln|\Sigma| + 2N or \text{SBC} = T\ln|\Sigma| + N\ln T (with N total parameters), or test nested lag lengths with a likelihood-ratio statistic (T-c)\,(\ln|\Sigma_r| - \ln|\Sigma_u|), which is \chi^2 (Enders 2014).

Granger causality: predictive precedence, not cause

In a VAR, Granger causality measures predictive precedence, not philosophical causation (Enders 2014). Variable y Granger-causes z if past values of y help predict z beyond what z’s own past predicts (Enders 2014). You test it with an F-test that the coefficients on all lags of y in the z-equation are jointly zero; block exogeneity is the multivariate version, asking whether a block of variables helps predict the rest (Enders 2014).

Warning‘Granger-causes’ is a loaded name

It means temporal predictability, full stop. y can Granger-cause z with no causal link — both driven by a third variable, or by anticipation (stock prices “Granger-cause” earnings because they price in the future). Granger causality is necessary-ish for, but very far from sufficient for, the causal claims of Part V. (Enders 2014)

Impulse-response functions

The impulse-response function (IRF) traces the effect of a one-time shock to one innovation on the current and future values of all variables (Enders 2014). It comes from the vector moving-average (VMA) representation — the VAR rewritten as an infinite sum of past shocks:

\mathbf{x}_t = \boldsymbol{\mu} + \sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\Phi_i\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t-i}.

The matrices \Phi_i are the impulse responses: element (j,k) of \Phi_i is the response of variable j, i periods after a shock to variable k. Plotting them shows how a shock propagates through the system and decays (provided the VAR is stationary) (Enders 2014).

Identification: why the ordering matters

Here is the subtlety. Because the reduced-form residuals \mathbf{e}_t are correlated, a “shock to one equation” can’t be cleanly attributed to one variable — you must impose identifying restrictions (Enders 2014). The standard device is the Choleski (Cholesky) decomposition: a recursive ordering that makes the contemporaneous-effects matrix triangular, imposing exactly (n^2-n)/2 restrictions (Enders 2014). Its meaning: the variable ordered first affects all others contemporaneously, but is itself moved by the others only with a lag (Enders 2014).

The consequence is unavoidable: when residuals are highly correlated, changing the ordering changes the impulse responses and variance decompositions — the Sims–Bernanke concern (Enders 2014). The ordering is an economic assumption, not a technicality.

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

A = np.array([[0.5, 0.3],            # VAR(1) coefficient matrix (stable)
              [0.2, 0.4]])
Sigma = np.array([[1.0, 0.8],        # reduced-form residual covariance (corr 0.8)
                  [0.8, 1.0]])
H = 12

def orth_irf(A, Sigma, order):
    """Orthogonalized IRF to a 1-SD shock in the variable placed FIRST under `order`."""
    P = np.eye(2)[order]                       # permutation to chosen order
    Sig_o = P @ Sigma @ P.T
    L = np.linalg.cholesky(Sig_o)              # lower-triangular factor
    shock = P.T @ (L @ np.array([1.0, 0.0]))   # 1-SD shock to first-ordered var, back to original axes
    resp = np.empty((H, 2)); cur = shock.copy()
    for h in range(H):
        resp[h] = cur
        cur = A @ cur                          # Phi_i = A^i applied to the shock
    return resp

T = 600
eps = rng.normal(size=(T + 100, 2)); x = np.zeros((T + 100, 2))

    x[t] = A_bq @ x[t - 1] + C0 @ eps[t]
x = x[100:]                            # drop burn-in

Xl = np.c_[np.ones(T - 1), x[:-1]]     # OLS equation by equation (same regressors)
coef = np.linalg.lstsq(Xl, x[1:], rcond=None)[0]
A_hat = coef[1:].T

Sig = e.T @ e / len(e)
F = np.linalg.inv(np.eye(2) - A_hat)              # long-run multiplier of the reduced form

C0_hat = (np.eye(2) - A_hat) @ P                  # back out the impact matrix
if C0_hat[0, 1] < 0:                              # BQ fixes zeros, not signs: normalise so
    C0_hat[:, 1] *= -1                            # demand raises output on impact

def level_irf(A, C0, H=21):

    return np.cumsum(out, axis=0)                 # cumulate growth -> output LEVEL

Variance decomposition

The forecast-error variance decomposition (FEVD) is the IRF’s complement: it partitions the variance of each variable’s forecast error into the share attributable to each structural shock (Enders 2014). It answers “what % of the movement in z comes from z’s own shocks versus shocks to other variables?” Typically a variable explains most of its own variance at short horizons, with other variables’ shocks growing more important at longer horizons as effects diffuse through the system (Enders 2014). IRFs and FEVD together are Sims’s “innovation accounting” (Enders 2014).

Beyond Cholesky: structural VARs

The atheoretical VAR has an awkward ending. It refuses to impose “incredible” restrictions on the dynamics, and then, to draw impulse responses, it imposes an arbitrary recursive ordering on the shocks. Enders puts it plainly: given the “somewhat ad hoc nature” of the Choleski decomposition, “the beauty of the approach seems diminished,” and the VAR has been “criticized as being devoid of any economic content” (Enders 2014). A structural VAR replaces the arbitrary ordering with restrictions taken from economic theory. The lag coefficients are still estimated freely. Theory only constrains how the shocks hit the variables within the period.

Why bother? Setting b_{21}=0 (the Cholesky choice) assumes that a y innovation has no same-period effect on z. Unless something justifies that, the shocks are misidentified and the impulse responses can be “quite misleading.” Trying every ordering is no escape. A four-variable model already has 4! = 24 of them (Enders 2014). Sims (1986) and Bernanke (1986) proposed the alternative: model the contemporaneous relations among the innovations with an economic argument (Enders 2014).

Counting is the whole game. OLS hands you \Sigma, which has only (n^2+n)/2 distinct elements. The structural model has n^2 unknowns: n^2-n off-diagonal elements of B and n shock variances. So you need (n^2-n)/2 extra restrictions. The Cholesky triangle supplies exactly that many, and that is the only reason it “works” (Enders 2014). But counting is only a necessary condition. Because the equations are nonlinear in the b_{ij}, there is “no simple rule” that guarantees exact identification (Enders 2014).

One residual series, five identifications

Enders makes the point with five hand-sized residuals, e_1 = (1, -0.5, 0, -1, 0.5) and e_2 = (0.5, -1, 0, -0.5, 1). They give \sigma_1^2=\sigma_2^2=0.5 and \sigma_{12}=0.4, a correlation of 0.8. That leaves three equations in four unknowns. Each extra restriction below identifies the system, and each gives a different set of “structural” shocks (Enders 2014). (We re-derived every row from \Sigma_\varepsilon = B\Sigma B'; they all reproduce.)

Restriction Solution What it claims
Cholesky, b_{12}=0 b_{21}=-0.8; var(\varepsilon_1)=0.5, var(\varepsilon_2)=0.18 shock 1 moves both variables at once; shock 2 reaches x_1 only with a lag
Cholesky, b_{21}=0 b_{12}=-0.8; var(\varepsilon_1)=0.18, var(\varepsilon_2)=0.5 the reverse: same data, the “typical” shock 1 shrinks from 0.5 to 0.18 in variance
Coefficient, b_{12}=1 b_{21}=-1; var 1.8 and 0.2 a known unit effect, no triangle needed
Variance, var(\varepsilon_1)=1.8 two solutions: b_{12}=1 or b_{12}=-2.6 (b_{21}=-5/3, var(\varepsilon_2)=5/9) variance restrictions always give multiple roots
Symmetry, b_{12}=b_{21} two solutions: -0.5 (variances 0.225) or -2.0 (variances 0.9) equal cross-effects, common in open-economy models

The fourth family is sign restrictions, a newer line of work (Mountford and Uhlig 2008). Instead of setting coefficients, you require that, say, an oil shock leave GDP unaffected for two quarters, or that a monetary shock raise inflation (Enders 2014). One more note on the table: with a residual correlation of 0.8, the choice of ordering alone decides which shock looks big. That is the same lesson the figure’s top panels draw from impulse responses.

When theory says more: over-identification

Sometimes theory supplies more than (n^2-n)/2 restrictions. Then the restrictions become testable. You estimate the unrestricted VAR as usual (restrictions on B do not change the lag coefficients). You then maximise the likelihood over the free elements of B and \Sigma_\varepsilon, and compare restricted and unrestricted fit with a \chi^2 test whose degrees of freedom equal the number of over-identifying restrictions (Enders 2014). Sims also warned that the standard errors may be inaccurate, and Waggoner and Zha (1997) showed that the normalisation can matter for inference (Enders 2014).

NoteTwo over-identified systems
  • What drove the 2000s grain-price boom? By December 2011 the World Bank food price index was almost three times its 2000 level. Enders and Holt (2013) fitted a monthly VAR (January 1974 to December 2011) in real energy prices, the real dollar, the real T-bill rate, and real grain prices. The SBC chose 2 lags and general-to-specific chose 11; they used 7, because grain prices could not plausibly adjust in two months. Exact identification needs 6 restrictions. They first tried 9, making the three macro shocks mutually independent. The test rejected it (\chi^2 = 13.53, 3 df, p = 0.003). Freeing the exchange-rate–interest-rate link (8 restrictions) was not rejected (\chi^2 = 4.57, 2 df, p = 0.102). Under that scheme, an energy shock leaves grain prices unmoved on impact but has raised them by nearly 40% by six months. A stronger dollar and higher interest rates both push grain prices down (Enders 2014).
  • Sims’s money market. Sims (1986) fitted a six-variable quarterly VAR (1948Q1–1979Q3). A Cholesky ordering produced a money-supply shock with little effect on prices, output, or interest rates, which is hard to square with money demand. He replaced it with 17 zero restrictions, against the 15 needed for exact identification. They give a money-supply equation (r_t = 71.20\,m_t + \varepsilon_{rt}), a money-demand equation that rises with income and prices and falls with the interest rate, and autonomous investment. Under this scheme, money-supply shocks do move prices, income, and rates (Enders 2014).

Long-run restrictions: Blanchard–Quah

Blanchard and Quah (1989) found a different kind of theory to lean on, and it does not concern the same period at all. Under the natural-rate hypothesis, demand shocks have no long-run effect on real output, while supply (productivity) shocks can move it permanently (Enders 2014). That single long-run claim is enough to identify a bivariate VAR.

The set-up takes an I(1) series y_t, the log of real GNP, and enters it in first differences. A second, stationary series z_t (unemployment in their case) is driven by the same two shocks. Unlike Sims–Bernanke, the shocks are not attached to particular equations. They are the economist’s exogenous forces, and both variables respond to both (Enders 2014). The restriction says the demand shock’s effects on \Delta y must sum to zero over all horizons, so the output level returns to where it was. Three moments of \Sigma plus that one long-run zero give four equations for the four impact coefficients (Enders 2014). The bottom panel of Figure 1 runs the whole procedure by hand on simulated data. It recovers a supply shock that ratchets output to a new level and a demand shock whose effect on output dies out.

The method has preconditions. At least one series must actually have a unit root, since an I(0) variable has no permanent component to decompose. If y_t is stationary, “there is no reason to proceed.” Both series must enter the VAR in stationary form, and the residuals should pass the usual white-noise checks (Enders 2014). That makes unit-root testing the first step, not an optional one.

NoteWhat Blanchard and Quah found, and an exchange-rate replay

  • Output and unemployment, 1950Q2–1987Q4 (8 lags, four VAR variants). Demand shocks have hump-shaped, mirror-image effects. Output rises as unemployment falls, both peak after about four quarters, and both then return to baseline. Supply shocks have cumulative effects on output. Unemployment rises slightly at first, then falls, staying below its long-run level for nearly five years. The variance decomposition (output-growth dummy, detrended unemployment) gives the share of forecast-error variance due to demand shocks (Enders 2014):

    Horizon (quarters) 1 4 12 40
    Output 99.0 97.9 67.6 39.3
    Unemployment 51.9 80.2 86.2 85.6

    Demand dominates short-run output movements, which contradicts the Beveridge–Nelson decomposition. Its share must fall towards zero at long horizons, because its effects are temporary by construction (Enders 2014).

  • Real and nominal exchange rates (Enders and Lee 1997, updated to 2013Q1). For the Canadian–US dollar, the restriction is that nominal shocks cannot move the real exchange rate permanently. If Canada doubles its money supply, prices and the exchange rate both double and the real rate is unchanged. A likelihood-ratio test of 3 lags against 1 gives (157-7)(-16.823+16.934) = 16.63, which is significant at 0.034 with 8 df. The AIC agrees, but the SBC picks 1 lag, so a careful researcher would run both (Enders 2014). The identification changes the story. Under Cholesky, real shocks explain 100% of the real rate’s one-quarter forecast-error variance and 73.93% of the nominal rate’s. Under BQ the shares are 88.31% and 40.11% (Enders 2014). A nominal shock moves the real rate by at most about 0.4 standard deviations, and only temporarily, because prices adjust to offset it (Enders 2014).

Limits. BQ can identify at most as many distinct shocks as there are variables. Blanchard and Quah’s own propositions for the case of three or more shocks amount to “there are really only two.” So avoid the decomposition when several important disturbances are suspected, or build a model with enough long-run restrictions, as Clarida and Gali (1994) did with three variables (Enders 2014). The restrictions are also quadratic. They fix zeros but not signs: our figure code has to choose the sign of the demand shock by convention. Larger systems can have many solutions, and Taylor (2003) recommends over-identifying restrictions or theory-based normalisations to choose among them (Enders 2014).

TipVAR hygiene (Enders after Todd 1990)

VAR results can be fragile. Adding a trend, changing the lag length, dropping a variable, switching from monthly to quarterly data, or swapping one plausible interest-rate measure for another can all change the impulse responses. Todd re-ran Sims’s results with three money measures, two interest rates, and with and without a trend. Enders’s advice is to choose variables carefully, know whether each series is stationary, trend-stationary, or difference-stationary, and try several reasonable specifications. “Maintain a healthy skepticism” when they disagree (Enders 2014).

Common pitfalls

WarningWhere this goes wrong
  • Reading Granger causality as causation. It is predictive precedence; a significant F-test is not a causal license. (Enders 2014)
  • Ignoring the Cholesky ordering. Highly correlated residuals make IRFs/FEVD ordering-dependent; justify the ordering or report robustness across orderings. (Enders 2014)
  • Running a VAR on non-stationary levels. If the series are I(1) and cointegrated, you need a VECM, not a plain VAR; if I(1) and not cointegrated, model the differences. (Enders 2014)
  • Over-lagging. Each extra lag costs n^2 parameters; let AIC/SBC and LR tests discipline p. (Enders 2014)
  • Over-interpreting structural shocks from a reduced form. Reduced-form residuals have no economic meaning until identified. (Enders 2014)
  • Treating the order condition as sufficient. Having (n^2-n)/2 restrictions is necessary, not sufficient. Variance and symmetry restrictions produced two valid solutions each in Enders’s five-residual example, and nothing in the data picks between them. (Enders 2014)
  • Running Blanchard–Quah on a stationary series. With no unit root there is no permanent component, so “demand versus supply” is not identified by the long-run zero. Test first. BQ is also limited to as many shocks as variables, so a two-variable BQ in a three-shock world mislabels what it finds. (Enders 2014)

Appendix · the mathematics

From VAR to VMA. Write the VAR(1) \mathbf{x}_t = A\mathbf{x}_{t-1} + \mathbf{e}_t (demeaned). Back- substituting, \mathbf{x}_t = \sum_{i=0}^{\infty} A^i \mathbf{e}_{t-i}, so the reduced-form impulse matrices are A^i. Stationarity requires every eigenvalue of A to lie inside the unit circle, which makes A^i \to 0 and the responses decay. A VAR(p) is recast as a VAR(1) in companion form to apply the same result.

Orthogonalizing the shocks. With reduced-form covariance \Sigma = \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{e}_t\mathbf{e}_t'], the Cholesky factor L (lower-triangular, LL'=\Sigma) defines orthogonal shocks \mathbf{u}_t = L^{-1}\mathbf{e}_t with identity covariance. The orthogonalized IRF to a unit shock in the k-th orthogonal innovation is \Phi_i L\,\mathbf{s}_k (with \mathbf{s}_k the k-th unit vector). Triangularity is the recursive assumption — variable 1 reacts to none of the others contemporaneously, and so on — which is exactly why a different ordering yields a different L and different responses.

Counting restrictions. Identifying n^2 structural parameters from the n(n+1)/2 distinct elements of \Sigma requires (n^2 - n)/2 extra restrictions; a recursive (triangular) B supplies exactly that many, which is why Cholesky just identifies the system.

The structural map and the two-variable count. With B\mathbf{x}_t=\Gamma_0+\Gamma_1\mathbf{x}_{t-1}+ \boldsymbol\varepsilon_t and \mathbf{e}_t=B^{-1}\boldsymbol\varepsilon_t, the variances satisfy \Sigma_\varepsilon = B\,\Sigma\,B' \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \Sigma = B^{-1}\Sigma_\varepsilon (B^{-1})'. For n=2 with B=\begin{pmatrix}1&b_{12}\\ b_{21}&1\end{pmatrix} and Enders’s \Sigma=\begin{pmatrix}0.5&0.4\\0.4&0.5\end{pmatrix}, multiplying out gives \text{var}(\varepsilon_1)=0.5+0.8b_{12}+0.5b_{12}^2,\qquad 0=0.4+0.5b_{12}+0.5b_{21}+0.4b_{12}b_{21},\qquad \text{var}(\varepsilon_2)=0.5+0.8b_{21}+0.5b_{21}^2 . That is three equations in four unknowns. Setting b_{12}=0 forces b_{21}=-0.8 and var(\varepsilon_2)=0.18. Under the symmetry restriction b_{12}=b_{21}=b, the middle equation becomes the quadratic 0.4b^2+b+0.4=0, with roots b=-0.5 and b=-2: two observationally equivalent structures.

Testing over-identification. The concentrated log likelihood is -\tfrac T2\ln|\Sigma| up to constants, so restricted and unrestricted fits are compared through T(\ln|\Sigma_R|-\ln|\Sigma|), which is asymptotically \chi^2_R with R = the number of restrictions beyond (n^2-n)/2. (Enders writes the statistic loosely as |\Sigma_R|-|\Sigma|; the log-determinant form is the likelihood-ratio statistic used for lag-length tests above.)

Blanchard–Quah in matrix form. Let \mathbf{x}_t=(\Delta y_t,z_t)' follow the VAR \mathbf{x}_t=A(L)\mathbf{x}_{t-1}+\mathbf{e}_t and let \mathbf{e}_t=C_0\boldsymbol\varepsilon_t with \text{var}(\boldsymbol\varepsilon_t)=I. The cumulated (long-run) response of \mathbf{x} to the shocks is C(1)=[I-A(1)]^{-1}C_0,\qquad A(1)=\textstyle\sum_k A_k . The BQ restriction is that the demand column of C(1) has a zero in the output row. Ordering the permanent shock first, C(1) is lower-triangular, and C(1)C(1)'=[I-A(1)]^{-1}\Sigma\,[I-A(1)]^{-1\,\prime}. So C(1) is the Cholesky factor of the long-run covariance, and C_0=[I-A(1)]\,C(1). That is the four-line computation in the figure code. Enders’s scalar version of the fourth restriction, c_{11}(0)[1-\sum a_{22}(k)]+c_{21}(0)\sum a_{12}(k)=0, is the corresponding element of [I-A(1)]^{-1}C_0 written out (up to the determinant, which is nonzero). Cholesky factors are unique only up to column signs, which is the formal version of “BQ does not identify signs.”

::: {.lead-kernel}
[ARIMA](arima-forecasting.qmd) models *one* series from its own past. But macro and social systems are
**webs**: output, inflation, and interest rates each move the others. **Vector autoregression (VAR)**
treats them symmetrically — every variable is regressed on the lagged values of *all* variables, none
privileged as exogenous. From that one democratic system come three tools: **Granger causality** (does the
past of X help predict Y?), **impulse responses** (how does a shock propagate?), and **variance
decomposition** (which shocks drive which series?).
:::

## Why it matters

Old large-scale macro models hard-wired which variables were "causes" and which "effects" — restrictions
Christopher **Sims (1980)** attacked as **"incredible,"** lacking real theoretical basis
[@enders_time_series]. His **atheoretical** alternative: treat *every* variable as endogenous and let the
data speak. The VAR became the workhorse of empirical macro and a staple of political science and
finance — and its **innovation accounting** (impulse responses + variance decomposition) is how
researchers read dynamic cause-and-effect *within* a system without imposing a dubious structural story.

## The VAR(p) system

A VAR of order $p$ models a vector of $n$ variables $\mathbf{x}_t$ as a linear function of its own $p$
lags — each equation containing the lags of **every** variable [@enders_time_series]. The crux is the
distinction between two forms:

**Structural (primitive) VAR** — includes *contemporaneous* effects among variables, with **pure,
uncorrelated structural shocks** $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_t$:

$$
B\mathbf{x}_t = \Gamma_0 + \Gamma_1 \mathbf{x}_{t-1} + \cdots + \Gamma_p \mathbf{x}_{t-p} + \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_t .
$$

**Reduced-form VAR** — premultiply by $B^{-1}$ to get the estimable system:

$$
\mathbf{x}_t = A_0 + \sum_{i=1}^p A_i\,\mathbf{x}_{t-i} + \mathbf{e}_t .
$$

The catch: the reduced-form errors $\mathbf{e}_t = B^{-1}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_t$ are **composites** of the
structural shocks, so they are **correlated across equations** and have **no direct economic
interpretation** [@enders_time_series]. That correlation is the whole identification problem (below).

## Estimation and lag length

Because **every equation has the identical set of regressors** (the same lags of all variables), **OLS
equation-by-equation** is consistent and asymptotically efficient — no system estimator needed
[@enders_time_series]. The real choice is the **lag length $p$**: too few causes misspecification, too
many wastes degrees of freedom [@enders_time_series]. Pick $p$ to minimise an information criterion,
$\text{AIC} = T\ln|\Sigma| + 2N$ or $\text{SBC} = T\ln|\Sigma| + N\ln T$ (with $N$ total parameters), or
test nested lag lengths with a **likelihood-ratio** statistic $(T-c)\,(\ln|\Sigma_r| - \ln|\Sigma_u|)$,
which is $\chi^2$ [@enders_time_series].

## Granger causality: predictive precedence, not cause

In a VAR, **Granger causality** measures **predictive precedence**, *not* philosophical causation
[@enders_time_series]. Variable $y$ **Granger-causes** $z$ if **past values of $y$ help predict $z$ beyond
what $z$'s own past predicts** [@enders_time_series]. You test it with an **$F$-test** that the
coefficients on *all* lags of $y$ in the $z$-equation are **jointly zero**; **block exogeneity** is the
multivariate version, asking whether a *block* of variables helps predict the rest [@enders_time_series].

::: {.callout-warning title="'Granger-causes' is a loaded name"}
It means *temporal predictability*, full stop. $y$ can Granger-cause $z$ with no causal link — both driven
by a third variable, or by anticipation (stock prices "Granger-cause" earnings because they price in the
future). Granger causality is necessary-ish for, but very far from sufficient for, the
[causal claims of Part V](../05-causal-inference/index.qmd). [@enders_time_series]
:::

## Impulse-response functions

The **impulse-response function (IRF)** traces the effect of a **one-time shock** to one innovation on the
**current and future values of all variables** [@enders_time_series]. It comes from the **vector
moving-average (VMA)** representation — the VAR rewritten as an infinite sum of past shocks:

$$
\mathbf{x}_t = \boldsymbol{\mu} + \sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\Phi_i\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t-i}.
$$

The matrices $\Phi_i$ **are** the impulse responses: element $(j,k)$ of $\Phi_i$ is the response of
variable $j$, $i$ periods after a shock to variable $k$. Plotting them shows how a shock **propagates
through the system and decays** (provided the VAR is stationary) [@enders_time_series].

## Identification: why the ordering matters

Here is the subtlety. Because the reduced-form residuals $\mathbf{e}_t$ are **correlated**, a "shock to one
equation" can't be cleanly attributed to one variable — you must impose **identifying restrictions**
[@enders_time_series]. The standard device is the **Choleski (Cholesky) decomposition**: a **recursive
ordering** that makes the contemporaneous-effects matrix triangular, imposing exactly $(n^2-n)/2$
restrictions [@enders_time_series]. Its meaning: **the variable ordered first affects all others
contemporaneously, but is itself moved by the others only with a lag** [@enders_time_series].

The consequence is unavoidable: when residuals are highly correlated, **changing the ordering changes the
impulse responses and variance decompositions** — the Sims–Bernanke concern [@enders_time_series]. The
ordering is an *economic assumption*, not a technicality.

Each panel shows both variables' response to a one-standard-deviation shock to the variable ordered FIRST. Note the contemporaneous (h=0) response of the second variable: it is forced to react in one ordering and forced to zero in the other. Same data, same VAR, different causal story, purely from the ordering. This is the Sims–Bernanke warning made visible. Bottom: the Blanchard–Quah alternative, also by hand (our own simulation, not from the sources). We simulate output growth and a stationary second series driven by a 'supply' shock with a permanent effect on the output level and a 'demand' shock with none, estimate a VAR(1) by OLS on 600 periods, and identify the shocks only from the long-run restriction. import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

A = np.array([[0.5, 0.3],            # VAR(1) coefficient matrix (stable)
              [0.2, 0.4]])
Sigma = np.array([[1.0, 0.8],        # reduced-form residual covariance (corr 0.8)
                  [0.8, 1.0]])
H = 12

def orth_irf(A, Sigma, order):
    """Orthogonalized IRF to a 1-SD shock in the variable placed FIRST under `order`."""
    P = np.eye(2)[order]                       # permutation to chosen order
    Sig_o = P @ Sigma @ P.T
    L = np.linalg.cholesky(Sig_o)              # lower-triangular factor
    shock = P.T @ (L @ np.array([1.0, 0.0]))   # 1-SD shock to first-ordered var, back to original axes
    resp = np.empty((H, 2)); cur = shock.copy()
    for h in range(H):
        resp[h] = cur
        cur = A @ cur                          # Phi_i = A^i applied to the shock
    return resp

fig, axd = plt.subplot_mosaic([["L", "R"], ["B", "B"]], figsize=(8.6, 7.4),
                               gridspec_kw={"height_ratios": [1, 1.05]})
for ax, order, lab in [(axd["L"], [0, 1], "Ordering X → Y"),
                       (axd["R"], [1, 0], "Ordering Y → X")]:
    r = orth_irf(A, Sigma, order)
    ax.axhline(0, color="#b7b6a8", lw=0.8)
    ax.plot(range(H), r[:, 0], "o-", color="#161613", lw=2, ms=4, label="response of X")
    ax.plot(range(H), r[:, 1], "s-", color="#e8531d", lw=2, ms=4, label="response of Y")
    ax.set_title(lab, fontsize=10); ax.set_xlabel("horizon (periods after shock)")
    ax.legend(fontsize=8, frameon=False)
axd["R"].sharey(axd["L"])
axd["L"].set_ylabel("response to a 1-SD shock")

# ---- Blanchard–Quah: identify shocks from a long-run restriction ----
rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
A_bq = np.array([[0.5, -0.4],          # x = (output growth, second series), stable
                 [0.1, 0.8]])
C_lr = np.array([[1.0, 0.0],           # true long-run multipliers (I - A)^-1 C0:
                 [-0.3, 0.6]])         # col 0 supply, col 1 demand (0 = no long-run output effect)
C0 = (np.eye(2) - A_bq) @ C_lr         # true impact matrix implied by that restriction
T = 600
eps = rng.normal(size=(T + 100, 2)); x = np.zeros((T + 100, 2))
for t in range(1, T + 100):
    x[t] = A_bq @ x[t - 1] + C0 @ eps[t]
x = x[100:]                            # drop burn-in

Xl = np.c_[np.ones(T - 1), x[:-1]]     # OLS equation by equation (same regressors)
coef = np.linalg.lstsq(Xl, x[1:], rcond=None)[0]
A_hat = coef[1:].T
e = x[1:] - Xl @ coef
Sig = e.T @ e / len(e)
F = np.linalg.inv(np.eye(2) - A_hat)              # long-run multiplier of the reduced form
P = np.linalg.cholesky(F @ Sig @ F.T)             # lower-triangular long-run impact
C0_hat = (np.eye(2) - A_hat) @ P                  # back out the impact matrix
if C0_hat[0, 1] < 0:                              # BQ fixes zeros, not signs: normalise so
    C0_hat[:, 1] *= -1                            # demand raises output on impact

def level_irf(A, C0, H=21):
    out, M = np.empty((H, 2)), C0.copy()
    for h in range(H):
        out[h] = M[0]; M = A @ M                  # row 0 = response of output GROWTH
    return np.cumsum(out, axis=0)                 # cumulate growth -> output LEVEL

true_lvl, est_lvl = level_irf(A_bq, C0), level_irf(A_hat, C0_hat)

## Variance decomposition

The **forecast-error variance decomposition (FEVD)** is the IRF's complement: it **partitions the variance
of each variable's forecast error into the share attributable to each structural shock**
[@enders_time_series]. It answers "what % of the movement in $z$ comes from $z$'s *own* shocks versus
shocks to other variables?" Typically a variable explains most of its *own* variance at **short horizons**,
with other variables' shocks growing more important at **longer horizons** as effects diffuse through the
system [@enders_time_series]. IRFs and FEVD together are Sims's **"innovation accounting"**
[@enders_time_series].

## Beyond Cholesky: structural VARs

The atheoretical VAR has an awkward ending. It refuses to impose "incredible" restrictions on the
dynamics, and then, to draw impulse responses, it imposes an arbitrary recursive ordering on the shocks.
Enders puts it plainly: given the "somewhat ad hoc nature" of the Choleski decomposition, "the beauty of
the approach seems diminished," and the VAR has been "criticized as being devoid of any economic
content" [@enders_time_series]. A **structural VAR** replaces the arbitrary ordering with restrictions
taken from economic theory. The lag coefficients are still estimated freely. Theory only constrains how
the shocks hit the variables **within the period**.

Why bother? Setting $b_{21}=0$ (the Cholesky choice) **assumes** that a $y$ innovation has no same-period
effect on $z$. Unless something justifies that, the shocks are misidentified and the impulse responses can
be "quite misleading." Trying every ordering is no escape. A four-variable model already has
$4! = 24$ of them [@enders_time_series]. Sims (1986) and Bernanke (1986) proposed the alternative: model
the contemporaneous relations among the innovations with an economic argument [@enders_time_series].

**Counting is the whole game.** OLS hands you $\Sigma$, which has only $(n^2+n)/2$ distinct elements. The
structural model has $n^2$ unknowns: $n^2-n$ off-diagonal elements of $B$ and $n$ shock variances. So
you need $(n^2-n)/2$ extra restrictions. The Cholesky triangle supplies exactly that many, and that is
the only reason it "works" [@enders_time_series]. But counting is only a **necessary** condition. Because
the equations are nonlinear in the $b_{ij}$, there is "no simple rule" that guarantees exact
identification [@enders_time_series].

### One residual series, five identifications

Enders makes the point with five hand-sized residuals, $e_1 = (1, -0.5, 0, -1, 0.5)$ and
$e_2 = (0.5, -1, 0, -0.5, 1)$. They give $\sigma_1^2=\sigma_2^2=0.5$ and $\sigma_{12}=0.4$, a correlation of
**0.8**. That leaves three equations in four unknowns. Each extra restriction below identifies the system,
and each gives a **different** set of "structural" shocks [@enders_time_series]. (We re-derived every
row from $\Sigma_\varepsilon = B\Sigma B'$; they all reproduce.)

| Restriction | Solution | What it claims |
|---|---|---|
| Cholesky, $b_{12}=0$ | $b_{21}=-0.8$; var$(\varepsilon_1)=0.5$, var$(\varepsilon_2)=0.18$ | shock 1 moves both variables at once; shock 2 reaches $x_1$ only with a lag |
| Cholesky, $b_{21}=0$ | $b_{12}=-0.8$; var$(\varepsilon_1)=0.18$, var$(\varepsilon_2)=0.5$ | the reverse: same data, the "typical" shock 1 shrinks from 0.5 to 0.18 in variance |
| Coefficient, $b_{12}=1$ | $b_{21}=-1$; var 1.8 and 0.2 | a known unit effect, no triangle needed |
| Variance, var$(\varepsilon_1)=1.8$ | **two** solutions: $b_{12}=1$ or $b_{12}=-2.6$ ($b_{21}=-5/3$, var$(\varepsilon_2)=5/9$) | variance restrictions always give multiple roots |
| Symmetry, $b_{12}=b_{21}$ | **two** solutions: $-0.5$ (variances 0.225) or $-2.0$ (variances 0.9) | equal cross-effects, common in open-economy models |

The fourth family is **sign restrictions**, a newer line of work (Mountford and Uhlig 2008). Instead of
setting coefficients, you require that, say, an oil shock leave GDP unaffected for two quarters, or that
a monetary shock raise inflation [@enders_time_series]. One more note on the table: with a residual
correlation of 0.8, the choice of ordering alone decides which shock looks big. That is the same lesson
the figure's top panels draw from impulse responses.

### When theory says more: over-identification

Sometimes theory supplies **more** than $(n^2-n)/2$ restrictions. Then the restrictions become
**testable**. You estimate the unrestricted VAR as usual (restrictions on $B$ do not change the lag
coefficients). You then maximise the likelihood over the free elements of $B$ and $\Sigma_\varepsilon$, and
compare restricted and unrestricted fit with a $\chi^2$ test whose degrees of freedom equal the number of
over-identifying restrictions [@enders_time_series]. Sims also warned that the standard errors may be
inaccurate, and Waggoner and Zha (1997) showed that the normalisation can matter for inference
[@enders_time_series].

::: {.callout-note title="Two over-identified systems"}
- **What drove the 2000s grain-price boom?** By December 2011 the World Bank food price index was
  almost three times its 2000 level. Enders and Holt (2013) fitted a monthly VAR (January 1974 to
  December 2011) in real energy prices, the real dollar, the real T-bill rate, and real grain prices.
  The SBC chose 2 lags and general-to-specific chose 11; they used 7, because grain prices could not
  plausibly adjust in two months. Exact identification needs 6 restrictions. They first tried 9, making
  the three macro shocks mutually independent. The test rejected it ($\chi^2 = 13.53$, 3 df,
  $p = 0.003$). Freeing the exchange-rate–interest-rate link (8 restrictions) was not rejected
  ($\chi^2 = 4.57$, 2 df, $p = 0.102$). Under that scheme, an energy shock leaves grain prices unmoved
  on impact but has raised them by nearly 40% by six months. A stronger dollar and higher interest
  rates both push grain prices down [@enders_time_series].
- **Sims's money market.** Sims (1986) fitted a six-variable quarterly VAR (1948Q1–1979Q3). A Cholesky
  ordering produced a money-supply shock with little effect on prices, output, or interest rates, which
  is hard to square with money demand. He replaced it with **17** zero restrictions, against the
  **15** needed for exact identification. They give a money-supply equation ($r_t = 71.20\,m_t +
  \varepsilon_{rt}$), a money-demand equation that rises with income and prices and falls with the
  interest rate, and autonomous investment. Under this scheme, money-supply shocks do move prices,
  income, and rates [@enders_time_series].
:::

## Long-run restrictions: Blanchard–Quah

Blanchard and Quah (1989) found a different kind of theory to lean on, and it does not concern the
same period at all. Under the natural-rate hypothesis, **demand shocks have no long-run effect on real
output**, while **supply (productivity) shocks can move it permanently** [@enders_time_series]. That
single long-run claim is enough to identify a bivariate VAR.

The set-up takes an I(1) series $y_t$, the log of real GNP, and enters it in first differences. A second,
stationary series $z_t$ (unemployment in their case) is driven by the same two shocks. Unlike
Sims–Bernanke, the shocks are **not** attached to particular equations. They are the economist's
exogenous forces, and both variables respond to both [@enders_time_series]. The restriction says the
demand shock's effects on $\Delta y$ must **sum to zero** over all horizons, so the output **level**
returns to where it was. Three moments of $\Sigma$ plus that one long-run zero give four equations
for the four impact coefficients [@enders_time_series]. The bottom panel of
@fig-irf-ordering runs the whole procedure by hand on simulated data. It recovers a supply shock that
ratchets output to a new level and a demand shock whose effect on output dies out.

The method has preconditions. **At least one series must actually have a unit root**, since an I(0)
variable has no permanent component to decompose. If $y_t$ is stationary, "there is no reason to
proceed." Both series must enter the VAR in stationary form, and the residuals should pass the usual
white-noise checks [@enders_time_series]. That makes [unit-root testing](unit-roots-and-stationarity.qmd)
the first step, not an optional one.

::: {.callout-note title="What Blanchard and Quah found, and an exchange-rate replay"}
- **Output and unemployment, 1950Q2–1987Q4 (8 lags, four VAR variants).** Demand shocks have
  **hump-shaped**, mirror-image effects. Output rises as unemployment falls, both peak after about four
  quarters, and both then return to baseline. Supply shocks have **cumulative** effects on output.
  Unemployment rises slightly at first, then falls, staying below its long-run level for nearly five
  years. The variance decomposition (output-growth dummy, detrended unemployment) gives the share of
  forecast-error variance due to **demand** shocks [@enders_time_series]:

  | Horizon (quarters) | 1 | 4 | 12 | 40 |
  |---|---|---|---|---|
  | Output | 99.0 | 97.9 | 67.6 | 39.3 |
  | Unemployment | 51.9 | 80.2 | 86.2 | 85.6 |

  Demand dominates short-run output movements, which contradicts the Beveridge–Nelson decomposition. Its
  share must fall towards zero at long horizons, because its effects are temporary by construction
  [@enders_time_series].
- **Real and nominal exchange rates (Enders and Lee 1997, updated to 2013Q1).** For the Canadian–US
  dollar, the restriction is that **nominal shocks cannot move the real exchange rate permanently**. If
  Canada doubles its money supply, prices and the exchange rate both double and the real rate is
  unchanged. A likelihood-ratio test of 3 lags against 1 gives $(157-7)(-16.823+16.934) = 16.63$, which is
  significant at 0.034 with 8 df. The AIC agrees, but the SBC picks 1 lag, so a careful researcher
  would run both [@enders_time_series]. The identification changes the story. Under Cholesky, real
  shocks explain 100% of the real rate's one-quarter forecast-error variance and 73.93% of the nominal
  rate's. Under BQ the shares are 88.31% and **40.11%** [@enders_time_series]. A nominal shock moves the
  real rate by at most about 0.4 standard deviations, and only temporarily, because prices adjust to
  offset it [@enders_time_series].
:::

**Limits.** BQ can identify at most as many distinct shocks as there are variables. Blanchard and Quah's
own propositions for the case of three or more shocks amount to "there are really only two." So avoid
the decomposition when several important disturbances are suspected, or build a model with enough
long-run restrictions, as Clarida and Gali (1994) did with three variables [@enders_time_series]. The
restrictions are also **quadratic**. They fix zeros but not **signs**: our figure code has to choose the
sign of the demand shock by convention. Larger systems can have many solutions, and Taylor (2003)
recommends over-identifying restrictions or theory-based normalisations to choose among them
[@enders_time_series].

::: {.callout-tip title="VAR hygiene (Enders after Todd 1990)"}
VAR results can be fragile. Adding a trend, changing the lag length, dropping a variable, switching from
monthly to quarterly data, or swapping one plausible interest-rate measure for another can all change
the impulse responses. Todd re-ran Sims's results with three money measures, two interest rates, and
with and without a trend. Enders's advice is to choose variables carefully, know whether each series is
stationary, trend-stationary, or difference-stationary, and try several reasonable specifications.
"Maintain a healthy skepticism" when they disagree [@enders_time_series].
:::

## Common pitfalls

::: {.callout-warning title="Where this goes wrong"}
- **Reading Granger causality as causation.** It is predictive precedence; a significant $F$-test is not a
  causal license. [@enders_time_series]
- **Ignoring the Cholesky ordering.** Highly correlated residuals make IRFs/FEVD ordering-dependent;
  justify the ordering or report robustness across orderings. [@enders_time_series]
- **Running a VAR on non-stationary levels.** If the series are I(1) and *cointegrated*, you need a
  [VECM](cointegration-and-error-correction.qmd), not a plain VAR; if I(1) and not cointegrated, model the
  differences. [@enders_time_series]
- **Over-lagging.** Each extra lag costs $n^2$ parameters; let AIC/SBC and LR tests discipline $p$.
  [@enders_time_series]
- **Over-interpreting structural shocks from a reduced form.** Reduced-form residuals have no economic
  meaning until identified. [@enders_time_series]
- **Treating the order condition as sufficient.** Having $(n^2-n)/2$ restrictions is necessary, not
  sufficient. Variance and symmetry restrictions produced **two** valid solutions each in Enders's
  five-residual example, and nothing in the data picks between them. [@enders_time_series]
- **Running Blanchard–Quah on a stationary series.** With no unit root there is no permanent component,
  so "demand versus supply" is not identified by the long-run zero. Test first. BQ is also limited to as
  many shocks as variables, so a two-variable BQ in a three-shock world mislabels what it finds.
  [@enders_time_series]
:::

## Appendix · the mathematics {.unnumbered}

::: {.math-appendix}

**From VAR to VMA.** Write the VAR(1) $\mathbf{x}_t = A\mathbf{x}_{t-1} + \mathbf{e}_t$ (demeaned). Back-
substituting, $\mathbf{x}_t = \sum_{i=0}^{\infty} A^i \mathbf{e}_{t-i}$, so the reduced-form impulse
matrices are $A^i$. Stationarity requires every eigenvalue of $A$ to lie **inside the unit circle**, which
makes $A^i \to 0$ and the responses decay. A VAR($p$) is recast as a VAR(1) in *companion form* to apply
the same result.

**Orthogonalizing the shocks.** With reduced-form covariance $\Sigma = \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{e}_t\mathbf{e}_t']$,
the Cholesky factor $L$ (lower-triangular, $LL'=\Sigma$) defines orthogonal shocks
$\mathbf{u}_t = L^{-1}\mathbf{e}_t$ with identity covariance. The **orthogonalized IRF** to a unit shock in
the $k$-th orthogonal innovation is $\Phi_i L\,\mathbf{s}_k$ (with $\mathbf{s}_k$ the $k$-th unit vector).
Triangularity is the recursive assumption — variable 1 reacts to none of the others contemporaneously, and
so on — which is exactly why a different ordering yields a different $L$ and different responses.

**Counting restrictions.** Identifying $n^2$ structural parameters from the $n(n+1)/2$ distinct elements of
$\Sigma$ requires $(n^2 - n)/2$ extra restrictions; a recursive (triangular) $B$ supplies exactly that
many, which is why Cholesky *just* identifies the system.

**The structural map and the two-variable count.** With $B\mathbf{x}_t=\Gamma_0+\Gamma_1\mathbf{x}_{t-1}+
\boldsymbol\varepsilon_t$ and $\mathbf{e}_t=B^{-1}\boldsymbol\varepsilon_t$, the variances satisfy
$$
\Sigma_\varepsilon = B\,\Sigma\,B' \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \Sigma = B^{-1}\Sigma_\varepsilon (B^{-1})'.
$$
For $n=2$ with $B=\begin{pmatrix}1&b_{12}\\ b_{21}&1\end{pmatrix}$ and Enders's $\Sigma=\begin{pmatrix}0.5&0.4\\0.4&0.5\end{pmatrix}$,
multiplying out gives
$$
\text{var}(\varepsilon_1)=0.5+0.8b_{12}+0.5b_{12}^2,\qquad
0=0.4+0.5b_{12}+0.5b_{21}+0.4b_{12}b_{21},\qquad
\text{var}(\varepsilon_2)=0.5+0.8b_{21}+0.5b_{21}^2 .
$$
That is three equations in four unknowns. Setting $b_{12}=0$ forces $b_{21}=-0.8$ and
var$(\varepsilon_2)=0.18$. Under the symmetry restriction $b_{12}=b_{21}=b$, the middle equation becomes the
quadratic $0.4b^2+b+0.4=0$, with roots $b=-0.5$ and $b=-2$: two observationally equivalent structures.

**Testing over-identification.** The concentrated log likelihood is $-\tfrac T2\ln|\Sigma|$ up to
constants, so restricted and unrestricted fits are compared through $T(\ln|\Sigma_R|-\ln|\Sigma|)$, which is
asymptotically $\chi^2_R$ with $R$ = the number of restrictions beyond $(n^2-n)/2$. (Enders writes the
statistic loosely as $|\Sigma_R|-|\Sigma|$; the log-determinant form is the likelihood-ratio statistic used
for lag-length tests above.)

**Blanchard–Quah in matrix form.** Let $\mathbf{x}_t=(\Delta y_t,z_t)'$ follow the VAR
$\mathbf{x}_t=A(L)\mathbf{x}_{t-1}+\mathbf{e}_t$ and let $\mathbf{e}_t=C_0\boldsymbol\varepsilon_t$ with
$\text{var}(\boldsymbol\varepsilon_t)=I$. The cumulated (long-run) response of $\mathbf{x}$ to the shocks is
$$
C(1)=[I-A(1)]^{-1}C_0,\qquad A(1)=\textstyle\sum_k A_k .
$$
The BQ restriction is that the demand column of $C(1)$ has a zero in the output row. Ordering the
permanent shock first, $C(1)$ is lower-triangular, and $C(1)C(1)'=[I-A(1)]^{-1}\Sigma\,[I-A(1)]^{-1\,\prime}$.
So $C(1)$ is the Cholesky factor of the **long-run** covariance, and
$C_0=[I-A(1)]\,C(1)$. That is the four-line computation in the figure code. Enders's scalar
version of the fourth restriction, $c_{11}(0)[1-\sum a_{22}(k)]+c_{21}(0)\sum a_{12}(k)=0$, is the
corresponding element of $[I-A(1)]^{-1}C_0$ written out (up to the determinant, which is nonzero).
Cholesky factors are unique only up to column signs, which is the formal version of "BQ does not
identify signs."

:::
