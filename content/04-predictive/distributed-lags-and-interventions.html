Distributed Lags, Interventions & Transfer Functions

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source

A cause in time rarely lands all at once. Measure the whole path: the jolt on impact, the echo that follows, and where things settle.

When a policy switches on or an input moves, the outcome series responds over time, not in one instant. Distributed-lag, intervention and transfer-function models are one family of single-equation tools. Each asks how a movement in an outside variable z gets spread across the future path of y, and each separates the impact effect (what happens now) from the long-run effect (where y ends up).

Why it matters

In January 1973 the United States began putting metal detectors in its airports. Did skyjackings fall, and by how much? The obvious answer is to compare the average number of skyjackings per quarter before 1973 with the average after. Enders calls that test poorly designed. Successive quarters are serially correlated, so the old regime “carries over” into the new one: attacks already being planned are not all deterred at once (Enders 2014). A before/after mean treats the whole post-period as a single level, when in fact y is still moving towards its new level.

The same problem turns up across the social sciences. A tax change that affects fertility only after a year or two, terrorism that lowers tourism revenue for three years, a retaliatory strike followed by a burst of reprisals that then fades. Each is a question about the time path of an effect. The tools here have one equation and one outside input. They sit between the univariate ARIMA model and the fully symmetric VAR, and they only work if that input really is outside the system (see below).

Interventions: a dummy variable with memory

Enders, Sandler & Cauley (1990) modelled quarterly skyjackings y_t as an AR(1) plus an intervention dummy z_t, which is 0 before 1973Q1 and 1 from then on:

y_t = a_0 + a_1 y_{t-1} + c_0 z_t + \varepsilon_t, \qquad |a_1|<1 .

The dummy shifts the intercept, and the autoregressive term turns that shift into a path (Enders 2014):

  • Impact effect is c_0: the jump in the first quarter, tested with an ordinary t-test.
  • Long-run effect is c_0/(1-a_1): the new long-run mean minus the old one.
  • Transition. After j quarters the effect is c_0(1 + a_1 + \dots + a_1^{\,j}). With 0<a_1<1 the effect grows in size as it approaches the long-run value. With -1<a_1<0 it overshoots and oscillates towards it.

The estimates. For U.S. domestic skyjackings, the best model was pure noise (a_1=0), so the whole effect was immediate: −5.62 incidents per quarter. Transnational skyjackings had a_1=0.276, so the impact of −1.29 grew to a long-run −1.78. “Other” skyjackings had a_1 = 0.237, with an impact of −3.90 and a long run of −5.11 (Enders 2014).

The shape of the intervention is itself a modelling choice

The dummy does not have to be a clean step. Enders lists four standard shapes (Enders 2014):

Shape z_t looks like Use it for
Pure jump 0 … 0, 1, 1, 1 … a permanent regime change (metal detectors)
Pulse 0 … 0, 1, 0, 0 … a one-off event (its effect can still persist through a_1)
Gradual ¼, ½, ¾, 1, 1 … a policy phased in (detectors took about a year to reach some foreign airports)
Prolonged pulse 1, 1, then decaying a programme switched on, then wound down (sky marshals)

Effects can also start after a delay d, which you model by using z_{t-d}. When theory does not settle the shape or the delay, fit the plausible options and let AIC/SBC and residual diagnostics choose between them (Enders 2014). The skyjacking study did this. A gradually increasing dummy over 1973 fitted transnational and “other” skyjackings slightly better, which suggests detectors spread more slowly outside the United States (Enders 2014).

When the choice decides the answer: the 1986 Libya raid. After the U.S. bombing of Libya in April 1986, the same research team (Enders and co-authors) modelled monthly transnational attacks on U.S. and U.K. targets. The pre-raid data fitted an AR model with lags 1 and 5. Coded as a permanent jump, the raid had no significant effect (t = 0.84). Coded as a one-month pulse, it had a large and highly significant one: about 38.9 extra attacks in the month of the raid, and both AIC and SBC preferred this version. Since the AR terms imply convergence, the estimated long-run effect was zero. The raid set off a wave of reprisals that faded away, and it did not bring terrorism down (Enders 2014).

TipThe three-step routine (Enders)
  1. Identify the ARIMA model on the longest clean stretch of data, before or after the intervention. Check it is stationary first (see unit roots).
  2. Estimate the model over the full sample with the intervention term included.
  3. Diagnose. Coefficients should be significant and imply convergence, residuals should be white noise (ARCH is also possible), and the model should beat plausible rivals such as other shapes or delays (Enders 2014).

Enders adds a warning about unit roots. If y has one, a pulse has a permanent effect on its level and a jump acts like a drift term. An intervention has only a temporary effect on a unit-root series if the values of z sum to zero (Enders 2014).

Distributed lags: the effect of an ordinary variable over time

Now replace the dummy with a real variable, such as the value of a tax break. Wooldridge’s finite distributed lag (FDL) model lets current and past values of z enter (Wooldridge 2019):

y_t = \alpha_0 + \delta_0 z_t + \delta_1 z_{t-1} + \dots + \delta_q z_{t-q} + u_t .

The coefficients describe two thought experiments:

  • Temporary change. Raise z by one unit for one period only. Then \delta_0 is the immediate change in y (the impact propensity, or impact multiplier). \delta_1 is the change one period later, \delta_2 two periods later, and after q periods y is back where it started. Plotting \delta_j against j gives the lag distribution (Wooldridge 2019).
  • Permanent change. Raise z by one unit for good. After h periods y has moved by \delta_0+\dots+\delta_h (the cumulative effect). Once all lags have worked through, it has moved by the long-run propensity \text{LRP} = \delta_0+\delta_1+\dots+\delta_q (Wooldridge 2019). In a log–log model, the impact propensity is the short-run elasticity and the LRP the long-run elasticity.

Individual lags can be imprecise while their sum is precise

Wooldridge’s Example 10.4 uses U.S. data from 1913 to 1984. It asks whether the general fertility rate (births per 1,000 women of childbearing age) responds to the real value of the personal tax exemption pe, controlling for World War II and the arrival of the pill. With pe and two lags, no single lag coefficient is significant. This is because pe_t, pe_{t-1} and pe_{t-2} are strongly correlated with each other (multicollinearity). The lags are still jointly significant (p = .012). The estimated LRP is about 0.101, and its standard error, 0.030, gives t\approx 3.4 and a 95% interval of roughly 0.041 to 0.160 (Wooldridge 2019).

The standard error comes from a reparameterisation. Write \theta_0 = \delta_0+\delta_1+\delta_2, substitute \delta_0 = \theta_0 - \delta_1 - \delta_2, and regress gfr_t on pe_t, (pe_{t-1}-pe_t) and (pe_{t-2}-pe_t). The coefficient on pe_t is then the LRP, and the software reports its standard error directly (Wooldridge 2019). The general point: the data often can’t tell you when an effect arrives, but they can tell you how big it is in total. Report the LRP, not a row of insignificant lag coefficients.

Infinite lags: the Koyck geometric decay

If you don’t want to pick a cut-off q, let the lags run on forever but make them decay geometrically, \delta_j = \gamma\rho^{\,j} with |\rho|<1. This is the geometric (Koyck) distributed lag. Its impact propensity is \gamma and its LRP is \gamma/(1-\rho) (Wooldridge 2019). Subtracting \rho times last period’s equation collapses the infinite sum into something you can estimate:

y_t = \alpha_0 + \gamma z_t + \rho\, y_{t-1} + (u_t - \rho u_{t-1}).

It looks like an ordinary regression with a lagged dependent variable, but the new error u_t-\rho u_{t-1} is correlated with y_{t-1}, so OLS is inconsistent (Wooldridge 2019). There are two fixes. The first is to instrument y_{t-1} with z_{t-1}, which needs strict exogeneity of z. The second is to assume u_t is AR(1) with the same \rho. The equation is then dynamically complete, so OLS works, but Wooldridge advises testing that strong assumption (Wooldridge 2019). The Koyck lag is the simplest rational distributed lag, where the lag weights are a ratio of two lag polynomials.

Transfer functions and the ADL

Enders brings the two ideas together in the autoregressive distributed lag (ADL) model. It has the outcome’s own lags plus a lag polynomial in the input (Enders 2014):

y_t = a_0 + A(L)\,y_{t-1} + C(L)\,z_t + \varepsilon_t .

C(L) is the transfer function: it shows how a movement in z is transferred into the path of y, and its coefficients are the transfer-function weights. If c_0=0, then z is a leading indicator: you can forecast y_{t+1} from what you already know about z (Enders 2014).

To find which lags matter, Enders uses the cross-correlogram between y_t and z_{t-i}. When z is white noise, the cross-covariances are zero until the first non-zero weight c_d, spike at each lag where z acts directly, and then decay at the rate set by A(L). That decay has the same shape as an ARMA autocorrelation function (Enders 2014). Real inputs are rarely white noise, though, so the procedure is:

  1. Fit an AR model to z, D(L)z_t = \varepsilon_{zt}. Its residuals are the “pure innovations” in z.
  2. Filter y with the same polynomial, y^f_t = D(L)y_t. Cross-correlate y^f_t with those innovations to identify C(L). Correlating raw y with raw z can be misleading: an effect that is really contemporaneous can show up as a lag.
  3. Regress y on the chosen z lags. The ACF of the residuals suggests the form of A(L).
  4. Estimate everything jointly, then check that the residuals are white noise and uncorrelated with z (Enders 2014).

Differencing changes the meaning of the model. If y is in levels, a shock to z fades. If the model is for \Delta y, the effect on the level of y never fades. If only \Delta z enters, a pulse in z has only a temporary effect on the level of y (Enders 2014).

Worked example: terrorism and tourism in Italy. Enders, Sandler & Parise (1992) used quarterly data from 1971 to 1988. They modelled Italy’s (logged, deseasonalised) share of tourism revenue among 12 countries against transnational terrorist incidents in Italy. The incidents looked like white noise (mean 4.2 per quarter, no significant autocorrelation), so no prewhitening was needed. The cross-correlogram showed a negative relationship starting at lags 1–2. AIC preferred the model with both lags and SBC the model with lag 2 alone. The joint model, which added AR and seasonal AR terms, was

(1-0.694L)(1-0.394L^4)\,y_t = -0.0030\,z_{t-1} - 0.0040\,z_{t-2} + \varepsilon_t .

The simulated impulse response has tourism dropping sharply after a one-quarter delay, with a seasonal oscillation, and recovering after about three years. Adding up over all incidents gave losses of more than 861 million 1988 SDRs at a 5% discount rate, about 6% of Italy’s annual tourism revenue (Enders 2014). In the published version, the authors used a rational transfer function E(L)/F(L), so that one denominator lag spread the effect over many quarters without a long list of coefficients (Enders 2014).

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

H = 13

# Left: pure jump, y_t = a1*y_{t-1} + c0*z_t, z = 1 from t = 0
a1, c0 = 0.276, -1.29
step = np.zeros(H)
for t in range(H):
    step[t] = (a1 * step[t - 1] if t > 0 else 0.0) + c0
lr = c0 / (1 - a1)

# Right: pulse, y_t = 0.327*y_{t-1} + 0.157*y_{t-5} + 38.9*z_t, z = 1 at t = 0 only
pulse = np.zeros(H)
for t in range(H):
    prev1 = pulse[t - 1] if t >= 1 else 0.0
    prev5 = pulse[t - 5] if t >= 5 else 0.0
    pulse[t] = 0.327 * prev1 + 0.157 * prev5 + (38.9 if t == 0 else 0.0)

The assumption everything rests on: no feedback

All of this depends on z being exogenous. It has to evolve independently of y, and it must be uncorrelated with the shocks to y at every lead and lag (Enders 2014). Wooldridge makes the same point formally. Strict exogeneity rules out feedback from today’s y to future z, which is unrealistic when z is a policy that reacts to past outcomes, such as money growth set with an eye on inflation (Wooldridge 2019).

Enders illustrates this with a thermostat. Turning up the heat truly does warm the room. But if you adjust the thermostat in response to the room temperature, the data can mislead. Perfect control leaves the temperature flat and apparently unrelated to the dial. Under-reacting to a cold forecast produces a negative co-movement between dial and temperature. Over-reacting makes the two rise together. You recover the true effect only if the dial is moved without reference to the room temperature (Enders 2014). As a practical check, Enders suggests looking at cross-correlations between y_t and future values z_{t+i}. If they are significant, y may be driving z (Enders 2014).

Two problems remain: feedback, and the judgement calls needed to pare down a transfer function (two equally skilled researchers will reach different models). Together they led Sims to drop the exogenous/endogenous split altogether and treat every variable symmetrically. That is the VAR (Enders 2014). Enders also notes an asymmetry. An over-parameterised transfer function still gives unbiased estimates as long as z is exogenous. Setting coefficients to zero that should not be zero does not (Enders 2014).

Common pitfalls

WarningWhere this goes wrong
  • Comparing before/after means. Serial correlation carries the old regime into the new one. Model the dynamics, and report both the impact and the long-run effect. (Enders 2014)
  • Treating the intervention shape as a fixed fact. In the Libya case, jump or pulse turned a “no effect” finding into “38.9 extra attacks.” Compare shapes and delays with information criteria and diagnostics, and say which you chose and why. (Enders 2014)
  • Reading significance lag by lag. Correlated lags make each \hat\delta_j noisy. Test the lags jointly and report the LRP with its own standard error. (Wooldridge 2019)
  • Estimating the Koyck form by plain OLS. The transformed error is correlated with y_{t-1}. Use IV, or justify and test the same-\rho AR(1) assumption. (Wooldridge 2019)
  • Assuming the input is exogenous when it is a policy. Policies respond to outcomes, which is the thermostat problem. Check cross-correlations with leads of z, and if feedback is likely use a VAR. (Enders 2014; Wooldridge 2019)
  • Cross-correlating non-stationary series. Cross-correlograms mean nothing if y or z has a unit root. Test first, and remember that differencing changes what the coefficients mean. (Enders 2014)
  • Calling an intervention estimate “causal” too quickly. A single treated series with no control group identifies the effect only if nothing else changed at the same time. The synthetic-control and difference-in-differences designs exist to supply that missing comparison.

Infinite distributed lags: strict exogeneity vs the weaker current-and-past version that allows policy feedback; the geometric (Koyck) lag with LRP \gamma/(1-\rho); why OLS on the transformed equation is inconsistent; IV with z_{t-1}, or the same-\rho AR(1) error; rational distributed lags.

Appendix · the mathematics

Intervention paths from the lag operator. Write (1-a_1L)y_t = a_0 + c_0 z_t + \varepsilon_t, so

y_t = \frac{a_0}{1-a_1} + c_0\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}a_1^{\,i} z_{t-i} + \sum_{i=0}^{\infty}a_1^{\,i}\varepsilon_{t-i}.

For a jump at t (z_{t+j}=1 for all j\ge 0), \partial y_{t+j}/\partial z_t = c_0\sum_{i=0}^{j}a_1^{\,i} \to c_0/(1-a_1). For a pulse (z_t=1 only), the response is c_0 a_1^{\,j}\to 0. With higher-order A(L) the weights are the coefficients of c_0/[1-A(L)L], which is how the figure’s lag-5 echo arises. A pulse on a unit-root series (a_1=1) never decays: c_0\cdot 1^{j} = c_0.

LRP reparameterisation. In y_t=\alpha_0+\delta_0 z_t+\delta_1 z_{t-1}+\delta_2 z_{t-2}+u_t, put \theta_0=\delta_0+\delta_1+\delta_2:

y_t = \alpha_0 + \theta_0 z_t + \delta_1(z_{t-1}-z_t) + \delta_2(z_{t-2}-z_t) + u_t ,

so OLS gives \hat\theta_0 and \mathrm{se}(\hat\theta_0) directly. This is the same algebra as \mathrm{Var}(\hat\delta_0+\hat\delta_1+\hat\delta_2) including all the covariance terms, which is why it can be small when each \mathrm{Var}(\hat\delta_j) is large.

Koyck transformation. With \delta_j=\gamma\rho^{\,j}, y_t=\alpha+\gamma\sum_{j\ge0}\rho^{\,j}z_{t-j}+u_t. Subtracting \rho y_{t-1} gives y_t=\alpha(1-\rho)+\gamma z_t+\rho y_{t-1}+v_t with v_t=u_t-\rho u_{t-1}. If u_t is serially uncorrelated, \mathrm{Cov}(v_t,y_{t-1})=-\rho\sigma_u^2\neq 0 and v_t is MA(1), so OLS is inconsistent. If instead u_t=\rho u_{t-1}+e_t, then v_t=e_t and OLS is consistent. \text{LRP}=\gamma\sum\rho^{\,j} =\gamma/(1-\rho).

The cross-covariance pattern. For y_t=a_1y_{t-1}+c_dz_{t-d}+\varepsilon_t with white-noise z independent of \varepsilon, solving gives y_t=c_d\sum_{i\ge0}a_1^{\,i}z_{t-d-i}+\dots, so

\mathbb{E}[y_t z_{t-i}] = \begin{cases} 0 & i<d \\ c_d\, a_1^{\,i-d}\,\sigma_z^2 & i\ge d \end{cases}

The pattern is zeros until the delay, a spike, then geometric decay at the AR rate. If z is itself AR with D(L)z_t=\varepsilon_{zt}, applying D(L) to both sides gives y^f_t = D(L)a_0 + A(L)y^f_{t-1} + C(L)\varepsilon_{zt} + D(L)\varepsilon_t. The filtered outcome against the white-noise innovations brings back the same readable pattern for C(L).

::: {.lead-kernel}
When a policy switches on or an input moves, the outcome series responds **over time**, not in one
instant. **Distributed-lag**, **intervention** and **transfer-function** models are one family of
single-equation tools. Each asks how a movement in an outside variable $z$ gets *spread across* the
future path of $y$, and each separates the **impact effect** (what happens now) from the **long-run
effect** (where $y$ ends up).
:::

## Why it matters

In January 1973 the United States began putting metal detectors in its airports. Did skyjackings fall,
and by how much? The obvious answer is to compare the average number of skyjackings per quarter before
1973 with the average after. Enders calls that test **poorly designed**. Successive quarters are
**serially correlated**, so the old regime "carries over" into the new one: attacks already being planned
are not all deterred at once [@enders_time_series]. A before/after mean treats the whole post-period as a
single level, when in fact $y$ is still moving towards its new level.

The same problem turns up across the social sciences. A tax change that affects fertility only after a
year or two, terrorism that lowers tourism revenue for three years, a retaliatory strike followed by a
burst of reprisals that then fades. Each is a question about the **time path** of an effect. The tools
here have one equation and one outside input. They sit between the univariate
[ARIMA](arima-forecasting.qmd) model and the fully symmetric [VAR](var-and-impulse-responses.qmd), and
they only work if that input really is **outside** the system (see below).

## Interventions: a dummy variable with memory

Enders, Sandler & Cauley (1990) modelled quarterly skyjackings $y_t$ as an AR(1) plus an **intervention
dummy** $z_t$, which is 0 before 1973Q1 and 1 from then on:

$$
y_t = a_0 + a_1 y_{t-1} + c_0 z_t + \varepsilon_t, \qquad |a_1|<1 .
$$

The dummy shifts the intercept, and the autoregressive term turns that shift into a path
[@enders_time_series]:

- **Impact effect** is $c_0$: the jump in the first quarter, tested with an ordinary $t$-test.
- **Long-run effect** is $c_0/(1-a_1)$: the new long-run mean minus the old one.
- **Transition.** After $j$ quarters the effect is $c_0(1 + a_1 + \dots + a_1^{\,j})$. With $0<a_1<1$ the
  effect *grows* in size as it approaches the long-run value. With $-1<a_1<0$ it overshoots and
  oscillates towards it.

**The estimates.** For U.S. domestic skyjackings, the best model was pure noise ($a_1=0$), so the whole
effect was immediate: **−5.62 incidents per quarter**. Transnational skyjackings had $a_1=0.276$, so the
impact of −1.29 grew to a **long-run −1.78**. "Other" skyjackings had $a_1 = 0.237$, with an impact of
−3.90 and a long run of **−5.11** [@enders_time_series].

### The shape of the intervention is itself a modelling choice

The dummy does not have to be a clean step. Enders lists four standard shapes [@enders_time_series]:

| Shape | $z_t$ looks like | Use it for |
|---|---|---|
| **Pure jump** | 0 … 0, 1, 1, 1 … | a permanent regime change (metal detectors) |
| **Pulse** | 0 … 0, 1, 0, 0 … | a one-off event (its effect can still persist through $a_1$) |
| **Gradual** | ¼, ½, ¾, 1, 1 … | a policy phased in (detectors took about a year to reach some foreign airports) |
| **Prolonged pulse** | 1, 1, then decaying | a programme switched on, then wound down (sky marshals) |

Effects can also start after a **delay** $d$, which you model by using $z_{t-d}$. When theory does not
settle the shape or the delay, fit the plausible options and let AIC/SBC and residual diagnostics choose
between them [@enders_time_series]. The skyjacking study did this. A *gradually increasing* dummy over
1973 fitted transnational and "other" skyjackings slightly better, which suggests detectors spread more
slowly outside the United States [@enders_time_series].

**When the choice decides the answer: the 1986 Libya raid.** After the U.S. bombing of Libya in April
1986, the same research team (Enders and co-authors) modelled monthly transnational attacks on U.S. and U.K. targets. The
pre-raid data fitted an AR model with lags 1 and 5. Coded as a *permanent jump*, the raid had no
significant effect ($t = 0.84$). Coded as a *one-month pulse*, it had a large and highly significant one:
**about 38.9 extra attacks** in the month of the raid, and both AIC and SBC preferred this version. Since
the AR terms imply convergence, the estimated **long-run effect was zero**. The raid set off a wave of
reprisals that faded away, and it did not bring terrorism down [@enders_time_series].

::: {.callout-tip title="The three-step routine (Enders)"}
1. **Identify** the ARIMA model on the longest clean stretch of data, before or after the intervention.
   Check it is stationary first (see [unit roots](unit-roots-and-stationarity.qmd)).
2. **Estimate** the model over the full sample with the intervention term included.
3. **Diagnose.** Coefficients should be significant and imply convergence, residuals should be white
   noise (ARCH is also possible), and the model should beat plausible rivals such as other shapes or
   delays [@enders_time_series].
:::

Enders adds a warning about **unit roots**. If $y$ has one, a *pulse* has a permanent effect on its level
and a *jump* acts like a drift term. An intervention has only a temporary effect on a unit-root series if
the values of $z$ sum to zero [@enders_time_series].

## Distributed lags: the effect of an ordinary variable over time

Now replace the dummy with a real variable, such as the value of a tax break. Wooldridge's
**finite distributed lag (FDL)** model lets current and past values of $z$ enter [@wooldridge_intro]:

$$
y_t = \alpha_0 + \delta_0 z_t + \delta_1 z_{t-1} + \dots + \delta_q z_{t-q} + u_t .
$$

The coefficients describe two thought experiments:

- **Temporary change.** Raise $z$ by one unit for one period only. Then $\delta_0$ is the immediate
  change in $y$ (the **impact propensity**, or impact multiplier). $\delta_1$ is the change one period
  later, $\delta_2$ two periods later, and after $q$ periods $y$ is back where it started. Plotting
  $\delta_j$ against $j$ gives the **lag distribution** [@wooldridge_intro].
- **Permanent change.** Raise $z$ by one unit for good. After $h$ periods $y$ has moved by
  $\delta_0+\dots+\delta_h$ (the **cumulative effect**). Once all lags have worked through, it has moved
  by the **long-run propensity** $\text{LRP} = \delta_0+\delta_1+\dots+\delta_q$ [@wooldridge_intro]. In a
  log–log model, the impact propensity is the short-run elasticity and the LRP the long-run elasticity.

### Individual lags can be imprecise while their sum is precise

Wooldridge's Example 10.4 uses U.S. data from 1913 to 1984. It asks whether the **general fertility rate**
(births per 1,000 women of childbearing age) responds to the real value of the **personal tax exemption**
$pe$, controlling for World War II and the arrival of the pill. With $pe$ and two lags, *no single lag
coefficient is significant*. This is because $pe_t$, $pe_{t-1}$ and $pe_{t-2}$ are strongly correlated with
each other (multicollinearity). The lags are still **jointly** significant ($p = .012$). The estimated LRP
is about **0.101**, and its standard error, **0.030**, gives $t\approx 3.4$ and a 95% interval of roughly
**0.041 to 0.160** [@wooldridge_intro].

The standard error comes from a **reparameterisation**. Write $\theta_0 = \delta_0+\delta_1+\delta_2$,
substitute $\delta_0 = \theta_0 - \delta_1 - \delta_2$, and regress $gfr_t$ on $pe_t$,
$(pe_{t-1}-pe_t)$ and $(pe_{t-2}-pe_t)$. The coefficient on $pe_t$ is then the LRP, and the software reports
its standard error directly [@wooldridge_intro]. The general point: **the data often can't tell you
*when* an effect arrives, but they can tell you *how big* it is in total.** Report the LRP, not a
row of insignificant lag coefficients.

### Infinite lags: the Koyck geometric decay

If you don't want to pick a cut-off $q$, let the lags run on forever but make them decay geometrically,
$\delta_j = \gamma\rho^{\,j}$ with $|\rho|<1$. This is the **geometric (Koyck) distributed lag**. Its impact
propensity is $\gamma$ and its LRP is $\gamma/(1-\rho)$ [@wooldridge_intro]. Subtracting $\rho$ times last
period's equation collapses the infinite sum into something you can estimate:

$$
y_t = \alpha_0 + \gamma z_t + \rho\, y_{t-1} + (u_t - \rho u_{t-1}).
$$

It looks like an ordinary regression with a lagged dependent variable, but the new error
$u_t-\rho u_{t-1}$ is correlated with $y_{t-1}$, so **OLS is inconsistent** [@wooldridge_intro]. There are two
fixes. The first is to instrument $y_{t-1}$ with $z_{t-1}$, which needs strict exogeneity of $z$. The second
is to assume $u_t$ is AR(1) *with the same* $\rho$. The equation is then dynamically complete, so OLS
works, but Wooldridge advises *testing* that strong assumption [@wooldridge_intro]. The Koyck lag is the
simplest **rational distributed lag**, where the lag weights are a ratio of two lag polynomials.

## Transfer functions and the ADL

Enders brings the two ideas together in the **autoregressive distributed lag (ADL)** model. It has the
outcome's own lags plus a lag polynomial in the input [@enders_time_series]:

$$
y_t = a_0 + A(L)\,y_{t-1} + C(L)\,z_t + \varepsilon_t .
$$

$C(L)$ is the **transfer function**: it shows how a movement in $z$ is *transferred* into the path of $y$,
and its coefficients are the transfer-function weights. If $c_0=0$, then $z$ is a **leading indicator**:
you can forecast $y_{t+1}$ from what you already know about $z$ [@enders_time_series].

To find which lags matter, Enders uses the **cross-correlogram** between $y_t$ and $z_{t-i}$. When $z$ is
white noise, the cross-covariances are zero until the first non-zero weight $c_d$, **spike** at each
lag where $z$ acts directly, and then **decay at the rate set by $A(L)$**. That decay has the same shape as
an ARMA autocorrelation function [@enders_time_series]. Real inputs are rarely white noise, though, so the
procedure is:

1. Fit an AR model to $z$, $D(L)z_t = \varepsilon_{zt}$. Its residuals are the "pure innovations" in $z$.
2. **Filter** $y$ with the same polynomial, $y^f_t = D(L)y_t$. Cross-correlate $y^f_t$ with those
   innovations to identify $C(L)$. Correlating raw $y$ with raw $z$ can be misleading: an effect that is
   really contemporaneous can show up as a lag.
3. Regress $y$ on the chosen $z$ lags. The ACF of the residuals suggests the form of $A(L)$.
4. Estimate everything jointly, then check that the residuals are white noise and uncorrelated with $z$
   [@enders_time_series].

Differencing changes the meaning of the model. If $y$ is in levels, a shock to $z$ fades. If the model is
for $\Delta y$, the effect on the *level* of $y$ never fades. If only $\Delta z$ enters, a pulse in $z$ has
only a temporary effect on the level of $y$ [@enders_time_series].

**Worked example: terrorism and tourism in Italy.** Enders, Sandler & Parise (1992) used quarterly data
from 1971 to 1988. They modelled Italy's (logged, deseasonalised) share of tourism revenue among 12
countries against transnational terrorist incidents in Italy. The incidents looked like **white noise**
(mean 4.2 per quarter, no significant autocorrelation), so no prewhitening was needed. The cross-correlogram
showed a negative relationship starting at **lags 1–2**. AIC preferred the model with both lags and SBC
the model with lag 2 alone. The joint model, which added AR and **seasonal** AR terms, was

$$
(1-0.694L)(1-0.394L^4)\,y_t = -0.0030\,z_{t-1} - 0.0040\,z_{t-2} + \varepsilon_t .
$$

The simulated impulse response has tourism dropping sharply after a one-quarter delay, with a seasonal
oscillation, and **recovering after about three years**. Adding up over all incidents gave losses of
**more than 861 million 1988 SDRs at a 5% discount rate, about 6% of Italy's annual tourism revenue**
[@enders_time_series]. In the published version, the authors used a *rational* transfer function
$E(L)/F(L)$, so that one denominator lag spread the effect over many quarters without a long list of
coefficients [@enders_time_series].

```{python}
#| label: fig-intervention-paths

The effect builds quarter by quarter towards the long-run value c₀/(1−a₁) ≈ −1.78 (orange). Right: attacks on U.S./U.K. targets after the 1986 Libya raid, modelled as a one-month pulse (+38.9) in an AR model with lags 1 and 5. fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(8.8, 3.8))

ax = axes[0]
```

## The assumption everything rests on: no feedback

All of this depends on $z$ being **exogenous**. It has to evolve independently of $y$, and it must be
uncorrelated with the shocks to $y$ at *every* lead and lag [@enders_time_series]. Wooldridge makes the same
point formally. Strict exogeneity rules out **feedback from today's $y$ to future $z$**, which is
unrealistic when $z$ is a policy that reacts to past outcomes, such as money growth set with an eye on
inflation [@wooldridge_intro].

Enders illustrates this with a **thermostat**. Turning up the heat truly does warm the room. But if you
adjust the thermostat *in response to* the room temperature, the data can mislead. Perfect control
leaves the temperature flat and apparently unrelated to the dial. Under-reacting to a cold forecast
produces a *negative* co-movement between dial and temperature. Over-reacting makes the two rise
together. You recover the true effect only if the dial is moved **without reference to** the room
temperature [@enders_time_series]. As a practical check, Enders suggests looking at cross-correlations
between $y_t$ and **future** values $z_{t+i}$. If they are significant, $y$ may be driving $z$
[@enders_time_series].

Two problems remain: feedback, and the judgement calls needed to pare down a transfer function (two
equally skilled researchers will reach different models). Together they led Sims to drop the
exogenous/endogenous split altogether and treat every variable symmetrically. That is the
[VAR](var-and-impulse-responses.qmd) [@enders_time_series]. Enders also notes an asymmetry. An
*over*-parameterised transfer function still gives unbiased estimates as long as $z$ is exogenous. Setting
coefficients to zero that should not be zero does not [@enders_time_series].

## Common pitfalls

::: {.callout-warning title="Where this goes wrong"}
- **Comparing before/after means.** Serial correlation carries the old regime into the new one. Model the
  dynamics, and report both the impact and the long-run effect. [@enders_time_series]
- **Treating the intervention shape as a fixed fact.** In the Libya case, jump or pulse turned a "no
  effect" finding into "38.9 extra attacks." Compare shapes and delays with information criteria and
  diagnostics, and say which you chose and why. [@enders_time_series]
- **Reading significance lag by lag.** Correlated lags make each $\hat\delta_j$ noisy. Test the lags
  jointly and report the LRP with its own standard error. [@wooldridge_intro]
- **Estimating the Koyck form by plain OLS.** The transformed error is correlated with $y_{t-1}$. Use
  IV, or justify and test the same-$\rho$ AR(1) assumption. [@wooldridge_intro]
- **Assuming the input is exogenous when it is a policy.** Policies respond to outcomes, which is the
  thermostat problem. Check cross-correlations with *leads* of $z$, and if feedback is likely use a
  [VAR](var-and-impulse-responses.qmd). [@enders_time_series; @wooldridge_intro]
- **Cross-correlating non-stationary series.** Cross-correlograms mean nothing if $y$ or $z$ has a unit
  root. Test first, and remember that differencing changes what the coefficients mean.
  [@enders_time_series]
- **Calling an intervention estimate "causal" too quickly.** A single treated series with no control
  group identifies the effect only if nothing else changed at the same time. The
  [synthetic-control](../05-causal-inference/synthetic-control.qmd) and
  [difference-in-differences](../05-causal-inference/difference-in-differences.qmd) designs exist to
  supply that missing comparison.
:::

## Appendix · the mathematics {.unnumbered}

::: {.math-appendix}

**Intervention paths from the lag operator.** Write $(1-a_1L)y_t = a_0 + c_0 z_t + \varepsilon_t$, so

$$
y_t = \frac{a_0}{1-a_1} + c_0\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}a_1^{\,i} z_{t-i} + \sum_{i=0}^{\infty}a_1^{\,i}\varepsilon_{t-i}.
$$

For a jump at $t$ ($z_{t+j}=1$ for all $j\ge 0$), $\partial y_{t+j}/\partial z_t = c_0\sum_{i=0}^{j}a_1^{\,i}
\to c_0/(1-a_1)$. For a pulse ($z_t=1$ only), the response is $c_0 a_1^{\,j}\to 0$. With higher-order
$A(L)$ the weights are the coefficients of $c_0/[1-A(L)L]$, which is how the figure's lag-5 echo arises.
A pulse on a unit-root series ($a_1=1$) never decays: $c_0\cdot 1^{j} = c_0$.

**LRP reparameterisation.** In $y_t=\alpha_0+\delta_0 z_t+\delta_1 z_{t-1}+\delta_2 z_{t-2}+u_t$, put
$\theta_0=\delta_0+\delta_1+\delta_2$:

$$
y_t = \alpha_0 + \theta_0 z_t + \delta_1(z_{t-1}-z_t) + \delta_2(z_{t-2}-z_t) + u_t ,
$$

so OLS gives $\hat\theta_0$ and $\mathrm{se}(\hat\theta_0)$ directly. This is the same algebra as
$\mathrm{Var}(\hat\delta_0+\hat\delta_1+\hat\delta_2)$ including all the covariance terms, which is why it
can be small when each $\mathrm{Var}(\hat\delta_j)$ is large.

**Koyck transformation.** With $\delta_j=\gamma\rho^{\,j}$,
$y_t=\alpha+\gamma\sum_{j\ge0}\rho^{\,j}z_{t-j}+u_t$. Subtracting $\rho y_{t-1}$ gives
$y_t=\alpha(1-\rho)+\gamma z_t+\rho y_{t-1}+v_t$ with $v_t=u_t-\rho u_{t-1}$. If $u_t$ is serially
uncorrelated, $\mathrm{Cov}(v_t,y_{t-1})=-\rho\sigma_u^2\neq 0$ and $v_t$ is MA(1), so OLS is inconsistent.
If instead $u_t=\rho u_{t-1}+e_t$, then $v_t=e_t$ and OLS is consistent. $\text{LRP}=\gamma\sum\rho^{\,j}
=\gamma/(1-\rho)$.

**The cross-covariance pattern.** For $y_t=a_1y_{t-1}+c_dz_{t-d}+\varepsilon_t$ with white-noise $z$
independent of $\varepsilon$, solving gives
$y_t=c_d\sum_{i\ge0}a_1^{\,i}z_{t-d-i}+\dots$, so

$$
\mathbb{E}[y_t z_{t-i}] = \begin{cases} 0 & i<d \\ c_d\, a_1^{\,i-d}\,\sigma_z^2 & i\ge d \end{cases}
$$

The pattern is zeros until the delay, a spike, then geometric decay at the AR rate. If $z$ is itself
AR with $D(L)z_t=\varepsilon_{zt}$, applying $D(L)$ to both sides gives
$y^f_t = D(L)a_0 + A(L)y^f_{t-1} + C(L)\varepsilon_{zt} + D(L)\varepsilon_t$. The filtered outcome
against the white-noise innovations brings back the same readable pattern for $C(L)$.
