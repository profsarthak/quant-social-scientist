Prediction with Latent Variables

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source

When the predictors, the outcome or both are constructs measured by several items, you can score them first or keep them latent. The choice changes how much you can predict, how honestly you can test it, and what a penalty can do.

Social-science predictors and outcomes are often constructs (distress, grit, conscientiousness) measured by several items. You can turn each construct into a score and then run an ordinary prediction model, or you can keep it latent inside a structural equation model. Scoring first is simple but throws away explained variance. Keeping things latent recovers that variance, but you then need extra care to test the model on new data, to stop the outcome from changing what the construct means, and to penalise the model the way you would a large regression.

Why it matters

The measurement-error entry asked whether you should feed a model the items or a scale score. This entry asks the next question. Suppose you take the measurement model seriously and want to keep it: how do you then predict with it, and how do you check the prediction out of sample?

Jacobucci, Grimm and Zhang frame this as moving structural equation modelling (SEM) away from purely explanatory use and towards prediction (Jacobucci et al. 2023). SEM was built to reproduce a covariance matrix, not to forecast individual cases, and the authors note that some SEMs cannot produce case-by-case predictions at all. That is one reason partial least squares SEM has been promoted as a predictive alternative (see model selection & dimension reduction for PLS itself) (Jacobucci et al. 2023). Their chapter does not try to turn SEM into a pure prediction machine. It shows how far ideas from machine learning (holdout testing, regularisation, flexible nonlinear terms) can be carried into latent-variable models, and what they cost there.

If you have ever summed a questionnaire into one number and then regressed it on thirty covariates, this entry is about what that habit loses.

Three ways to use a block of items

When several predictors come from the same scale, or are thought to measure the same thing, the authors list the options (Jacobucci et al. 2023):

  • Stay in SEM. Model each construct as a latent variable measured by its items and regress the outcome on the latent variables directly.
  • Factor scores. Fit the measurement model, compute each person’s estimated score on each factor, and enter the scores into an ordinary (or logistic) regression.
  • Sum or mean scores. Add up the items that belong together and use the totals.

If the factor structure is uncertain, you first find it, either with an exploratory factor analysis or by staying inside SEM with exploratory SEM (below). Which route is sensible depends on three things: the sample size, the number of predictors, and how much prior evidence supports the factor structure (Jacobucci et al. 2023). With many predictors and a small sample, latent-variable models overfit just as regressions do.

A worked example: predicting suicidal thoughts

The book’s first example uses 2,000 respondents from the 2014 National Survey on Drug Use and Health, balanced so that half reported suicidal thoughts in the previous year, split 50/50 into training and test sets (Jacobucci et al. 2023). Fifteen items describe mental health in the previous year, plus four demographic variables.

  • Finding the structure. One factor fit badly (RMSEA 0.199). Two factors fit much better (0.082). Three fit a little better again (0.066), but the third factor had only small loadings and made little sense, so the authors kept two: distress (the six “DST” items) and impairment (the nine “IMP” items), correlated −0.57 after an oblique rotation.
  • One item belongs to both. “How many days this week did you have difficulties?” loaded about equally on both factors (0.35 and −0.32). A confirmatory model would have to let it cross-load, or assign it to one factor and accept some misfit. The authors put it on impairment.
  • The SEM did slightly worse at prediction. With the two latent variables and the demographics predicting the binary outcome inside one SEM, the training AUC was 0.80, a little below the factor-score and observed-variable versions (Jacobucci et al. 2023).

Their explanation for the last point is the key idea of this section. SEM estimation tries to make every part of the model fit at once, including the measurement of each item. A logistic regression on factor scores has only one job, to predict the outcome. If what you care about is the whole model, SEM is the right tool. If you care only about predicting one variable, you may give up a little accuracy by using it (Jacobucci et al. 2023).

Interpretational confounding: the outcome joins the factor

In machine learning there are inputs and an outcome. In SEM there are three roles: indicators of a latent variable, predictors, and outcomes. The authors point out that the line between indicators and outcomes is fuzzy (Jacobucci et al. 2023). A path from a latent variable to an observed outcome has exactly the same form as a factor loading. In the suicidal-thoughts model, the outcome is, mathematically, one more indicator of impairment.

This is called interpretational confounding. The outcome helps define the latent variable, so the “impairment” you are predicting with is partly built from the thing you are predicting. That can make the prediction look better than it is (Jacobucci et al. 2023). The usual fixes are two-step procedures: estimate the factor scores from the indicators alone, then put them into the structural model, so the construct is defined only by its items (Jacobucci et al. 2023). The measurement-error entry warns that two-step scores can bias estimates and standard errors. Here the authors present the same two-step move as the remedy for a different problem. Both statements are true, and you have to choose which risk matters more for your question.

Predicting a latent outcome: the MIMIC model

Outcomes are constructs too. “Depression” is usually the sum of a dozen questionnaire items, and the authors note that summing makes an extremely restrictive assumption about each item (Jacobucci et al. 2023). The SEM alternative is to keep the outcome latent and point the predictors at it.

A model in which a latent variable has both indicators (factor loadings) and causes (regressions from covariates) is a multiple indicators, multiple causes model, or MIMIC (Jöreskog & Goldberger, 1975) (Jacobucci et al. 2023). The literature separates reflective models, where the latent variable causes its items (loadings only), from formative ones, where observed variables combine to make the construct (regressions only). MIMIC combines the two. One consequence is easy to miss: adding the covariates can change the loadings, because the covariates now help define what the latent variable is (Jacobucci et al. 2023).

The book’s example predicts the two factors of the 12-item Grit scale, “persistence” (Grit1) and “hard work” (Grit2), from 50 Big Five personality items (ten per trait) plus 13 demographic measures (Jacobucci et al. 2023). Inside the SEM, the predictors explain:

Route Grit1 (persistence) Grit2 (hard work)
MIMIC, training set 69.8% 90.0%
Factor scores, then regression, training set 58.9% 73.6%

The two-step route explains less, because a factor score is a noisy estimate of the factor: some of its variance is measurement error that no predictor can explain (Jacobucci et al. 2023). The figure’s first panel rebuilds this effect.

Testing a latent-variable model on new data

With factor scores you can compute a prediction for every person in a test set and score it, as in any cross-validation. With latent variables this is harder, because the latent variables must be estimated again in the test data (Jacobucci et al. 2023). The danger is that the test-set model quietly builds a slightly different construct.

The authors’ solution borrows from the psychometric idea of measurement invariance: forcing the latent variable to be the same construct in both datasets by constraining its parameters to be equal (Jacobucci et al. 2023). In practice:

  1. Fit the model freely on the training data.
  2. In the test data, fix the loadings, regressions, means and variances at their training values.
  3. Leave free only the variances and covariance of the latent outcomes (and the outcome intercept). For a latent outcome, its variance is the residual variance, the part the predictors do not explain. Freeing it lets you read off a test-set R^2 without changing how the construct is formed. In the Grit model that leaves four free parameters (Jacobucci et al. 2023).

The results show how much the training numbers had flattered the model (Jacobucci et al. 2023):

Route Grit1 test R^2 Grit2 test R^2
MIMIC, parameters fixed at training values 0.419 0.588
Factor scores (training model, scored on test) 0.196 0.414

SEM still wins, but both routes lose a lot out of sample. For Grit2 the latent R^2 falls from 0.900 to 0.588, a drop of 0.312. The authors blame the large number of predictors, which inflated the training fit (Jacobucci et al. 2023). Because both sets come from the same sample, a big drop here signals overfitting. If the two sets came from different settings or groups, a big drop could instead signal that the construct is measured differently there (Jacobucci et al. 2023).

One clarification from the authors: “fit” in this procedure means SEM fit indices such as RMSEA or CFI, or an R^2 computed from model parameters. Without factor scores you cannot get case-by-case predictions of a latent variable in the test set, so casewise metrics such as RMSE are not available (Jacobucci et al. 2023).

Latent predictors and latent outcomes together

Sixty-three manifest predictors are hard to interpret. The natural next step is to model the predictors’ structure as well: five latent Big Five traits, each measured by its ten items, pointing at the two latent Grit factors (Jacobucci et al. 2023). Now there are only ten trait-to-Grit paths to read (plus the demographics). On a larger sample:

  • Conscientiousness dominates both outcomes, with standardised coefficients of 0.57 on persistence and −0.60 on hard work. (The book reports the opposite signs without comment.)
  • The fit is mixed: RMSEA 0.051, but CFI 0.74 and TLI 0.73.
  • Some prediction is lost. The latent-predictor model explains 45.8% and 60.2% of the Grit variance, against 48.7% and 63.8% with the manifest items (Jacobucci et al. 2023).

Simplifying the predictors makes the model easier to read but less predictive. This is the same trade-off between items and scales seen in the measurement-error entry, now on the predictor side. The next two sections try to recover some of the loss.

Regularised SEM

Regularisation can enter in two places: in the second step of a factor-score analysis (an ordinary penalised regression), or inside the SEM estimation. The second option is regularised SEM, RegSEM (Jacobucci, Grimm & McArdle, 2016), also called penalised likelihood SEM (Huang et al., 2017); the two give the same results when they use the same penalty (Jacobucci et al. 2023). The idea is the familiar one: add a penalty to the maximum-likelihood fit function,

F_{\text{regsem}} = F_{\text{ML}} + \lambda\,P(\cdot),

In plain words: fit the model as usual, but pay a price \lambda for every unit of path size. At \lambda = 0 you get ordinary SEM; as \lambda grows, the penalised paths are pushed to zero.

P is the lasso (sum of absolute values) or ridge (sum of squares) of the chosen parameters. Two differences from penalised regression matter in practice (Jacobucci et al. 2023):

  • Penalise only some parameters. Loadings and residual variances depend on one another, so it is usual to penalise only directed paths, often just the regressions you are unsure about. In the Grit MIMIC model that means the 124 regression paths from the covariates to the two Grit factors.
  • Choosing \lambda. You still need a grid of penalties and a rule for picking one. The little research that exists supports the BIC (Jacobucci et al., 2016; Huang et al., 2017). Cross-validation or the bootstrap are alternatives, at a much higher computational cost.

The example used 20 lasso penalties from 0 to 0.19. As the penalty increased, BIC generally fell and most coefficients shrank steadily towards zero, some reaching zero early and some never. Large SEMs tend not to converge under heavy penalties, so the grid was cut off at the highest penalty that still converged, and 18 of the 20 models converged (Jacobucci et al. 2023). The out-of-sample result was mixed:

  • For persistence (Grit1), more penalty lowered R^2 in both training and test data. Shrinkage did not help.
  • For hard work (Grit2), more penalty slightly raised test-set R^2. Shrinking the paths gave a more generalisable prediction (Jacobucci et al. 2023).

In both cases the penalty moved the test-set R^2 less than the training R^2. As with any lasso, which variables are selected is a fragile yes/no decision; see selection stability before reading the surviving paths as findings.

Exploratory SEM

A confirmatory measurement model forces every item onto one factor, and such models often fit badly. An exploratory factor analysis lets every item load on every factor, but it cannot be combined with regressions or other SEM paths, so it is of no direct use for prediction (Jacobucci et al. 2023). Exploratory SEM (ESEM; Asparouhov & Muthén, 2009) combines the two. It estimates an EFA-style measurement block for chosen items inside a model that also has regressions. When predicting with latent variables, ESEM has done well when there are small cross-loadings that a CFA would leave out (Mai, Zhang & Wen, 2018) (Jacobucci et al. 2023).

Replacing the five simple-structure Big Five factors with exploratory ones gives (Jacobucci et al. 2023):

Exploratory factors from the personality items Grit1 R^2 Grit2 R^2
3 32.4% 39.6%
5 46.9% 59.9%
10 52.3% 60.4%
(manifest items, for reference) 69.8% 90.0%

Five exploratory factors predict about as well as five simple-structure ones. Ten help persistence somewhat and fit well (RMSEA 0.036, CFI 0.89, TLI 0.86), but the gain is small for the extra parameters, and ten factors with many loadings each are hard to interpret. Latent predictors without loading constraints will usually predict a little worse than the raw items. The compensation is a smaller predictor space that you can explain (Jacobucci et al. 2023). The authors read ESEM as a method that mixes explanatory and predictive aims, consistent with their view that the two are not opposites.

Nonlinear effects among latent variables

Everything above is linear. Interactions and squares of observed variables are easy: you create them before fitting, though with many variables the number of candidate terms grows quickly and may itself call for regularisation (Jacobucci et al. 2023). Interactions and squares of latent variables are harder. The established approaches are product-indicator methods (Kenny & Judd, 1984) and structural equation mixture models (Bauer & Curran, 2004). The latent products are non-normal and the methods are computationally expensive, and many of the traditional methods handle only one to five such terms. Local SEM and moderated nonlinear factor analysis test nonlinearity one parameter at a time, which does not scale to large models (Jacobucci et al. 2023).

The authors’ preferred route is Bayesian SEM. It does not rely on reproducing a covariance matrix, so complicated functional forms are easier to write down, and it scales to more product terms (Jacobucci et al. 2023). Their demonstration, run in Stan on the Big Five → Grit model, compares three models:

  1. Linear effects of the five latent traits plus 12 demographics, with diffuse N(0,1) priors. The estimates were almost identical to maximum likelihood, as expected with a large sample and weak priors.
  2. All linear, quadratic and interaction terms among the five traits, with N(0,1) priors. The frequentist nlsem package failed to converge on a model this size.
  3. The same terms with a hyperlasso prior, a sparsity-inducing shrinkage prior, on the latent regression paths.
Model Grit1 R^2 Grit2 R^2
Maximum likelihood, linear 46% 60%
Bayesian, linear, diffuse priors 47% 62%
Bayesian, nonlinear, diffuse priors 51% 67%
Bayesian, nonlinear, hyperlasso 50% 65%

The nonlinear terms added about 4 and 5 points, which the authors judge not negligible (Jacobucci et al. 2023). There were quadratic effects of conscientiousness on hard work (positive, so the negative relationship flattens at high conscientiousness), of neuroticism on persistence (negative, so the relationship turns negative at high neuroticism) and of openness on hard work, and several interactions involving conscientiousness. The hyperlasso did not behave like a variable-selection tool here. It shrank the coefficients fairly uniformly and narrowed their credible intervals, but set few of them near zero. The authors used it as an overfitting check: a restrictive prior should push many coefficients to zero if the model has too many, and it did not. Test-set R^2 values for both priors fell inside the training-set 95% credible intervals (Jacobucci et al. 2023).

The R^2 of a latent outcome in a Bayesian model is computed from the sampled factor scores: the variance of the predicted mean divided by that variance plus the residual variance (Jacobucci et al. 2023). See the appendix.

Why a “hyperlasso” and not a lasso? Shrinkage priors

Jacobucci et al. name the hyperlasso without explaining it. Murphy explains why such priors exist (Murphy 2012). The lasso is MAP estimation under a Laplace prior, and that prior has two weaknesses. It has too little probability near zero, so it does not suppress noise strongly enough. It also has too little probability far from zero, so it shrinks real, large coefficients: the lasso’s well-known bias. Priors with a sharper spike at zero and heavier tails fix both problems, at the price of a non-convex optimisation in which the global optimum can no longer be guaranteed. Such methods often beat the lasso both in prediction and in finding the relevant variables (Murphy 2012).

Murphy gives two routes:

  • Bridge regression penalises \sum_j |w_j|^b. With b = 2 this is ridge, with b = 1 the lasso, and as b \to 0 it becomes best-subset selection. Below b = 1 the problem is non-convex. Above it, the penalty no longer produces exact zeros. That is why the lasso is the tightest convex approximation to best-subset selection (Murphy 2012).
  • Hierarchical priors give each coefficient its own penalty. In the hierarchical adaptive lasso (HAL; Lee et al., 2010) each coefficient’s prior variance has its own tuning parameter, itself drawn from a prior. Tuning a separate penalty for every coefficient by cross-validation would be impossible. For a Bayesian it is just one more level of the model. Fitted by EM, HAL becomes a reweighted lasso: each round, a coefficient that came out large gets a small penalty next time, and a small one gets a large penalty. The result is usually sparser than the lasso and less biased (Murphy 2012). One EM step with flat hyperpriors is closely related to the adaptive lasso (Zou, 2006).

The figure’s second panel shows the difference on a single coefficient. The lasso subtracts a constant from every surviving estimate. HAL kills small estimates and leaves large ones almost untouched, which is close to hard thresholding (Murphy 2012).

Many such priors exist. Murphy tabulates them all as scale mixtures of normals: each coefficient is normal with its own variance, and different priors on that variance give the Laplace (lasso), the normal–gamma, the normal–exponential–gamma, Student-t and the horseshoe (Carvalho et al., 2010), among others (Murphy 2012). The improper normal–Jeffreys prior even has no tuning parameter, which means nothing to tune but also no way to adjust how sparse the result is (Murphy 2012). This is also why Jacobucci et al. say that Bayesian regularisation needs only one model run. The hierarchy learns how much to shrink, instead of a grid of \lambda values (Jacobucci et al. 2023). (The horseshoe in a regression setting is covered in selection stability.)

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(8)

# --- (a) attenuation of R^2 when a latent outcome is scored first -------------
p, R2 = 10, 0.70
g = rng.normal(size=p)
spread = np.linspace(-0.15, 0.15, 6)

def reliabilities(m):
    lam = m + spread
    th = 1 - lam**2                                   # standardised items
    S = np.outer(lam, lam) + np.diag(th)              # implied item covariance
    rho_fs = lam @ np.linalg.solve(S, lam)            # regression factor score
    rho_sum = lam.sum()**2 / (lam.sum()**2 + th.sum())
    return lam, th, rho_fs, rho_sum

def r2(y, X):
    Z = np.column_stack([np.ones(len(X)), X])
    b = np.linalg.lstsq(Z, y, rcond=None)[0]
    return 1 - np.var(y - Z @ b) / np.var(y)

grid = np.linspace(0.30, 0.80, 51)
fs_line = [reliabilities(m)[2] * R2 for m in grid]
sum_line = [reliabilities(m)[3] * R2 for m in grid]

pts = np.linspace(0.30, 0.80, 6)
fs_sim, sum_sim = [], []
for m in pts:
    lam, th, _, _ = reliabilities(m)
    n = 2000
    X = rng.normal(size=(n, p))
    signal = X @ g / np.sqrt(g @ g)
    eta = np.sqrt(R2) * signal + np.sqrt(1 - R2) * rng.normal(size=n)
    Y = eta[:, None] * lam + rng.normal(size=(n, 6)) * np.sqrt(th)
    S = np.outer(lam, lam) + np.diag(th)
    fs_sim.append(r2(Y @ np.linalg.solve(S, lam), X))
    sum_sim.append(r2(Y.sum(1), X))

# --- (b) thresholding rules for one coefficient -------------------------------
a, b = 1.0, 0.1
w = np.linspace(0, 12, 120001)
def hal(v):
    return w[np.argmin(0.5 * (v - w)**2 + (a + 1) * np.log1p(w / b))]
w_mle = np.linspace(0, 10, 401)
w_hal = np.array([hal(v) for v in w_mle])
thr = w_mle[np.argmax(w_hal > 0)]                    # HAL's threshold
w_lasso = np.maximum(w_mle - thr, 0)
w_ridge = w_mle / 1.5

Common pitfalls

WarningWhere this goes wrong
  • Comparing a latent R^2 with a score-based R^2 as if they measured the same thing. The latent version is free of measurement error in the outcome. The score-based one is capped by the score’s reliability. A higher latent R^2 is partly just a different denominator. (Jacobucci et al. 2023)
  • Letting the outcome define the predictor. A path from a latent predictor to an observed outcome is formally another loading, so the outcome shapes the construct and flatters the prediction (interpretational confounding). Consider a two-step approach when the prediction is the point. (Jacobucci et al. 2023)
  • Re-estimating the latent variables freely in the test set. Then you test a different construct. Fix the measurement and structural parameters at their training values and free only the latent (residual) variances. (Jacobucci et al. 2023)
  • Reporting training-set R^2 from a large MIMIC model. With 63 predictors the hard-work R^2 fell from 0.90 to 0.59 in the holdout. (Jacobucci et al. 2023)
  • Penalising every parameter in a RegSEM. Loadings and residual variances depend on each other. Penalise only directed paths, usually the uncertain ones. (Jacobucci et al. 2023)
  • Reading a hyperlasso or horseshoe posterior as a selection list. In the Grit example the hyperlasso shrank coefficients uniformly and zeroed few. And under a Laplace prior only the posterior mode is sparse. (Jacobucci et al. 2023; Murphy 2012)
  • Assuming the lasso’s estimates are unbiased once selected. The lasso shrinks the large, real coefficients too. Adaptive or hierarchical penalties reduce that bias. (Murphy 2012)

Appendix · the mathematics

MIMIC model. With items y, latent variables \eta and covariates x, y = \Lambda\eta + \varepsilon, \qquad \eta = \Gamma x + \zeta . The first equation is the reflective measurement model and the second the formative, “multiple causes” part. The latent R^2 for factor k is \operatorname{Var}(\Gamma_k x)/\operatorname{Var}(\eta_k), computed from the model parameters, not from scores.

Why scoring first attenuates R^2 (the figure’s left panel; our derivation). Take one standardised latent outcome, \operatorname{Var}(\eta)=1, with item covariance \Sigma = \lambda\lambda' + \Theta. The regression factor score is \hat\eta = w'y with w = \Sigma^{-1}\lambda. Then \operatorname{Cov}(\hat\eta, x) = (w'\lambda)\operatorname{Cov}(\eta, x) and \operatorname{Var}(\hat\eta) = w'\Sigma w = w'\lambda \equiv \rho_{\hat\eta}, the score’s reliability. So R^2_{\hat\eta \mid x} = \rho_{\hat\eta}\, R^2_{\eta \mid x}. > In plain words: regressing a score on the covariates recovers the latent R^2 multiplied by the > score’s reliability.

The same argument with w = \mathbf 1 gives the sum score, whose reliability is (\sum\lambda_i)^2/[(\sum\lambda_i)^2 + \sum\theta_i]. In the figure at average loading 0.6 these are 0.80 and 0.77, giving R^2 of 0.56 and 0.54 against the latent 0.70. (In the book, the factor-score R^2 is 0.84 and 0.82 times the MIMIC values. Their scores came from a different estimation, so this is a consistency check of the direction, not a reproduction.)

Interpretational confounding. A path \beta from \eta to an observed outcome z, with z = \beta\eta + \delta, has the form of a loading. In the implied covariance matrix, z enters exactly as one more indicator of \eta, so its covariances with the items help determine \eta.

Regularised SEM. F_{\text{regsem}} = F_{\text{ML}} + \lambda P(\cdot), \qquad F_{\text{lasso}} = F_{\text{ML}} + \lambda\,\lVert (r_{11},\dots,r_{62,1},r_{12},\dots,r_{62,2}) \rVert_1 , with F_{\text{ML}} = \log\lvert\Sigma(\theta)\rvert + \operatorname{tr}\!\big(S\Sigma(\theta)^{-1}\big) - \log\lvert S\rvert - p the usual maximum-likelihood discrepancy (standard form). Note on the source: the book’s labels run r_{11}–r_{621} and r_{12}–r_{622}, i.e. 62 covariates per Grit factor and 124 paths, while the MIMIC figure caption counts 63 predictors (50 personality items plus 13 demographics). The entry reports both as printed.

R^2 of a latent outcome in Bayesian SEM (eqs 8.3–8.5): \mathrm{FS} \sim N(\mu, \psi^2), \qquad \mu = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X_1, \qquad R^2_{\mathrm{FS}} = \frac{\operatorname{Var}(\mu)}{\operatorname{Var}(\mu) + \psi^2}. The book counts 38 linear, quadratic and interaction paths among five latent predictors for the two outcomes. Five linear, five quadratic and ten pairwise interactions per outcome would make 40, so the book’s model presumably omitted two terms; the text does not say which.

Bridge regression (Murphy eq. 13.131): \hat w = \operatorname{NLL}(w) + \lambda\sum_j |w_j|^b, the MAP estimate under an exponential power prior.

Hierarchical adaptive lasso (eqs 13.133–13.143): \gamma_j \sim \mathrm{IG}(a, b), \qquad \tau_j^2 \mid \gamma_j \sim \mathrm{Ga}(1, \gamma_j^2/2), \qquad w_j \mid \tau_j^2 \sim N(0, \tau_j^2). Integrating out \tau_j^2 gives a Laplace prior whose width is set by \gamma_j; integrating out \gamma_j as well gives p(w_j \mid a, b) = \frac{a}{2b}\Big(1 + \frac{|w_j|}{b}\Big)^{-(a+1)}, \qquad \pi(w_j) = (a+1)\log\Big(1 + \frac{|w_j|}{b}\Big) + \text{const}. EM alternates s_j = \mathbb E[1/\gamma_j] = (a+1)/(b + |w_j^{(t)}|) with the weighted lasso \hat w^{(t+1)} = \arg\min_w \lVert y - Xw\rVert_2^2 + \sum_j s_j|w_j|. > In plain words: a coefficient estimated at 5 gets weight 2/5.1 \approx 0.39; one at 0.05 gets > 2/0.15 \approx 13.3, a penalty 34 times heavier (our arithmetic, a = 1, b = 0.1).

Thresholding (the figure’s right panel). With an orthogonal design the problem separates, and each coefficient solves \min_{w}\tfrac12(\hat w^{\text{mle}} - w)^2 + \pi(w) (eq. 13.146). Ridge with penalty \tfrac{\lambda}{2}w^2 gives \hat w^{\text{mle}}/(1+\lambda) (the figure uses \lambda = 0.5). The lasso gives soft thresholding, \operatorname{sign}(\hat w)\,(|\hat w| - \lambda)_+. HAL with a = 1, b = 0.1, minimised numerically on a fine grid, is zero up to a threshold just under 4 and then jumps, with an unpenalised estimate of 10 shrunk only to about 9.8, against the lasso’s 6.2 at the same threshold.

::: {.lead-kernel}
Social-science predictors and outcomes are often **constructs** (distress, grit, conscientiousness)
measured by several items. You can turn each construct into a score and then run an ordinary
prediction model, or you can keep it **latent** inside a structural equation model. Scoring first is
simple but throws away explained variance. Keeping things latent recovers that variance, but you then
need extra care to test the model on new data, to stop the outcome from changing what the construct
means, and to penalise the model the way you would a large regression.
:::

## Why it matters

The [measurement-error entry](measurement-and-ml.qmd) asked whether you should feed a model the items
or a scale score. This entry asks the next question. Suppose you take the measurement model seriously
and want to keep it: how do you then **predict** with it, and how do you check the prediction out of
sample?

Jacobucci, Grimm and Zhang frame this as moving structural equation modelling (SEM) away from purely
explanatory use and towards prediction [@jacobucci_ml]. SEM was built to reproduce a covariance
matrix, not to forecast individual cases, and the authors note that some SEMs cannot produce
case-by-case predictions at all. That is one reason partial least squares SEM has been promoted as a
predictive alternative (see [model selection & dimension reduction](../ml-track/model-selection-and-dimension-reduction.qmd)
for PLS itself) [@jacobucci_ml]. Their chapter does not try to turn SEM into a pure prediction
machine. It shows how far ideas from machine learning (holdout testing, regularisation, flexible
nonlinear terms) can be carried into latent-variable models, and what they cost there.

If you have ever summed a questionnaire into one number and then regressed it on thirty
covariates, this entry is about what that habit loses.

## Three ways to use a block of items {#sec-three-routes}

When several predictors come from the same scale, or are thought to measure the same thing, the
authors list the options [@jacobucci_ml]:

- **Stay in SEM.** Model each construct as a latent variable measured by its items and regress the
  outcome on the latent variables directly.
- **Factor scores.** Fit the measurement model, compute each person's estimated score on each factor,
  and enter the scores into an ordinary (or logistic) regression.
- **Sum or mean scores.** Add up the items that belong together and use the totals.

If the factor structure is uncertain, you first find it, either with an exploratory factor analysis
or by staying inside SEM with **exploratory SEM** (below). Which route is sensible depends on three
things: the sample size, the number of predictors, and how much prior evidence supports the factor
structure [@jacobucci_ml]. With many predictors and a small sample, latent-variable models overfit
just as regressions do.

### A worked example: predicting suicidal thoughts {#sec-nsduh}

The book's first example uses 2,000 respondents from the 2014 National Survey on Drug Use and Health,
balanced so that half reported suicidal thoughts in the previous year, split 50/50 into training and
test sets [@jacobucci_ml]. Fifteen items describe mental health in the previous year, plus four
demographic variables.

- **Finding the structure.** One factor fit badly (RMSEA 0.199). Two factors fit much better (0.082).
  Three fit a little better again (0.066), but the third factor had only small loadings and made little
  sense, so the authors kept two: **distress** (the six "DST" items) and **impairment** (the nine "IMP"
  items), correlated −0.57 after an oblique rotation.
- **One item belongs to both.** "How many days this week did you have difficulties?" loaded about
  equally on both factors (0.35 and −0.32). A confirmatory model would have to let it cross-load, or
  assign it to one factor and accept some misfit. The authors put it on impairment.
- **The SEM did slightly worse at prediction.** With the two latent variables and the demographics
  predicting the binary outcome inside one SEM, the training AUC was 0.80, a little below the
  factor-score and observed-variable versions [@jacobucci_ml].

Their explanation for the last point is the key idea of this section. SEM estimation tries to make
**every** part of the model fit at once, including the measurement of each item. A logistic regression
on factor scores has only one job, to predict the outcome. If what you care about is the whole model,
SEM is the right tool. If you care only about predicting one variable, you may give up a little accuracy
by using it [@jacobucci_ml].

## Interpretational confounding: the outcome joins the factor {#sec-confounding}

In machine learning there are inputs and an outcome. In SEM there are three roles: indicators of a
latent variable, predictors, and outcomes. The authors point out that the line between indicators and
outcomes is **fuzzy** [@jacobucci_ml]. A path from a latent variable to an observed outcome has exactly
the same form as a factor loading. In the suicidal-thoughts model, the outcome is, mathematically, one
more indicator of impairment.

This is called **interpretational confounding**. The outcome helps define the latent variable, so the
"impairment" you are predicting with is partly built from the thing you are predicting. That can make
the prediction look better than it is [@jacobucci_ml]. The usual fixes are **two-step** procedures:
estimate the factor scores from the indicators alone, then put them into the structural model, so the
construct is defined only by its items [@jacobucci_ml]. The [measurement-error entry](measurement-and-ml.qmd)
warns that two-step scores can bias estimates and standard errors. Here the authors present the same
two-step move as the remedy for a different problem. Both statements are true, and you have to choose
which risk matters more for your question.

## Predicting a latent outcome: the MIMIC model {#sec-mimic}

Outcomes are constructs too. "Depression" is usually the sum of a dozen questionnaire items, and the
authors note that summing makes an extremely restrictive assumption about each item [@jacobucci_ml].
The SEM alternative is to keep the outcome latent and point the predictors at it.

A model in which a latent variable has both **indicators** (factor loadings) and **causes**
(regressions from covariates) is a **multiple indicators, multiple causes** model, or **MIMIC**
(Jöreskog & Goldberger, 1975) [@jacobucci_ml]. The literature separates **reflective** models, where
the latent variable causes its items (loadings only), from **formative** ones, where observed
variables combine to make the construct (regressions only). MIMIC combines the two. One consequence is
easy to miss: adding the covariates can change the loadings, because the covariates now help define
what the latent variable is [@jacobucci_ml].

The book's example predicts the two factors of the 12-item **Grit** scale, "persistence" (Grit1) and
"hard work" (Grit2), from 50 Big Five personality items (ten per trait) plus 13 demographic measures
[@jacobucci_ml]. Inside the SEM, the predictors explain:

| Route | Grit1 (persistence) | Grit2 (hard work) |
|---|---|---|
| MIMIC, training set | 69.8% | 90.0% |
| Factor scores, then regression, training set | 58.9% | 73.6% |

The two-step route explains less, because a factor score is a noisy estimate of the factor: some
of its variance is measurement error that no predictor can explain [@jacobucci_ml]. The figure's first
panel rebuilds this effect.

## Testing a latent-variable model on new data {#sec-holdout}

With factor scores you can compute a prediction for every person in a test set and score it, as in any
[cross-validation](cross-validation.qmd). With latent variables this is harder, because the latent
variables must be **estimated again** in the test data [@jacobucci_ml]. The danger is that the test-set
model quietly builds a slightly different construct.

The authors' solution borrows from the psychometric idea of **measurement invariance**: forcing the
latent variable to be the same construct in both datasets by constraining its parameters to be equal
[@jacobucci_ml]. In practice:

1. Fit the model freely on the training data.
2. In the test data, **fix** the loadings, regressions, means and variances at their training values.
3. Leave free only the variances and covariance of the latent outcomes (and the outcome intercept).
   For a latent outcome, its variance is the **residual** variance, the part the predictors do not
   explain. Freeing it lets you read off a test-set $R^2$ without changing how the construct is
   formed. In the Grit model that leaves four free parameters [@jacobucci_ml].

The results show how much the training numbers had flattered the model [@jacobucci_ml]:

| Route | Grit1 test $R^2$ | Grit2 test $R^2$ |
|---|---|---|
| MIMIC, parameters fixed at training values | 0.419 | 0.588 |
| Factor scores (training model, scored on test) | 0.196 | 0.414 |

SEM still wins, but both routes lose a lot out of sample. For Grit2 the latent $R^2$ falls from 0.900 to
0.588, a drop of 0.312. The authors blame the large number of predictors, which inflated the training
fit [@jacobucci_ml]. Because both sets come from the same sample, a big drop here signals
**overfitting**. If the two sets came from different settings or groups, a big drop could instead signal
that the construct is **measured differently** there [@jacobucci_ml].

One clarification from the authors: "fit" in this procedure means SEM fit indices such as RMSEA or CFI,
or an $R^2$ computed from model parameters. Without factor scores you cannot get case-by-case
predictions of a latent variable in the test set, so casewise metrics such as RMSE are not available
[@jacobucci_ml].

## Latent predictors and latent outcomes together {#sec-both}

Sixty-three manifest predictors are hard to interpret. The natural next step is to model the
predictors' structure as well: five latent Big Five traits, each measured by its ten items, pointing at
the two latent Grit factors [@jacobucci_ml]. Now there are only ten trait-to-Grit paths to read
(plus the demographics). On a larger sample:

- **Conscientiousness dominates** both outcomes, with standardised coefficients of 0.57 on persistence
  and −0.60 on hard work. (The book reports the opposite signs without comment.)
- **The fit is mixed:** RMSEA 0.051, but CFI 0.74 and TLI 0.73.
- **Some prediction is lost.** The latent-predictor model explains 45.8% and 60.2% of the Grit
  variance, against 48.7% and 63.8% with the manifest items [@jacobucci_ml].

Simplifying the predictors makes the model easier to read but less predictive. This is the same
trade-off between items and scales seen in the [measurement-error entry](measurement-and-ml.qmd), now
on the predictor side. The next two sections try to recover some of the loss.

## Regularised SEM {#sec-regsem}

[Regularisation](regularization.qmd) can enter in two places: in the second step of a factor-score
analysis (an ordinary penalised regression), or **inside** the SEM estimation. The second option is
**regularised SEM**, RegSEM (Jacobucci, Grimm & McArdle, 2016), also called penalised likelihood SEM
(Huang et al., 2017); the two give the same results when they use the same penalty
[@jacobucci_ml]. The idea is the familiar one: add a penalty to the maximum-likelihood fit function,

$$
F_{\text{regsem}} = F_{\text{ML}} + \lambda\,P(\cdot),
$$

> **In plain words:** fit the model as usual, but pay a price $\lambda$ for every unit of path size. At
> $\lambda = 0$ you get ordinary SEM; as $\lambda$ grows, the penalised paths are pushed to zero.

$P$ is the lasso (sum of absolute values) or ridge (sum of squares) of the chosen parameters. Two
differences from penalised regression matter in practice [@jacobucci_ml]:

- **Penalise only some parameters.** Loadings and residual variances depend on one another, so it is
  usual to penalise only directed paths, often just the regressions you are unsure about. In the
  Grit MIMIC model that means the 124 regression paths from the covariates to the two Grit factors.
- **Choosing $\lambda$.** You still need a grid of penalties and a rule for picking one. The little
  research that exists supports the **BIC** (Jacobucci et al., 2016; Huang et al., 2017).
  Cross-validation or the bootstrap are alternatives, at a much higher computational cost.

The example used 20 lasso penalties from 0 to 0.19. As the penalty increased, BIC generally fell and
most coefficients shrank steadily towards zero, some reaching zero early and some never. Large SEMs
tend not to converge under heavy penalties, so the grid was cut off at the highest penalty that still
converged, and 18 of the 20 models converged [@jacobucci_ml]. The out-of-sample result was mixed:

- For **persistence (Grit1)**, more penalty lowered $R^2$ in both training and test data. Shrinkage
  did not help.
- For **hard work (Grit2)**, more penalty slightly **raised** test-set $R^2$. Shrinking the paths gave a
  more generalisable prediction [@jacobucci_ml].

In both cases the penalty moved the test-set $R^2$ less than the training $R^2$. As with any lasso,
which variables are selected is a fragile yes/no decision; see
[selection stability](selection-stability.qmd) before reading the surviving paths as findings.

## Exploratory SEM {#sec-esem}

A confirmatory measurement model forces every item onto one factor, and such models often fit badly.
An exploratory factor analysis lets every item load on every factor, but it cannot be combined with
regressions or other SEM paths, so it is of no direct use for prediction [@jacobucci_ml]. **Exploratory
SEM** (ESEM; Asparouhov & Muthén, 2009) combines the two. It estimates an EFA-style measurement block
for chosen items inside a model that also has regressions. When predicting with latent variables, ESEM
has done well when there are small cross-loadings that a CFA would leave out (Mai, Zhang & Wen, 2018)
[@jacobucci_ml].

Replacing the five simple-structure Big Five factors with exploratory ones gives [@jacobucci_ml]:

| Exploratory factors from the personality items | Grit1 $R^2$ | Grit2 $R^2$ |
|---|---|---|
| 3 | 32.4% | 39.6% |
| 5 | 46.9% | 59.9% |
| 10 | 52.3% | 60.4% |
| *(manifest items, for reference)* | *69.8%* | *90.0%* |

Five exploratory factors predict about as well as five simple-structure ones. Ten help persistence
somewhat and fit well (RMSEA 0.036, CFI 0.89, TLI 0.86), but the gain is small for the extra
parameters, and ten factors with many loadings each are hard to interpret. Latent predictors without
loading constraints will usually predict a little worse than the raw items. The compensation is a
smaller predictor space that you can explain [@jacobucci_ml]. The authors read ESEM as a method that
mixes explanatory and predictive aims, consistent with their view that the two are not opposites.

## Nonlinear effects among latent variables {#sec-nonlinear}

Everything above is linear. Interactions and squares of *observed* variables are easy: you create them
before fitting, though with many variables the number of candidate terms grows quickly and may itself
call for regularisation [@jacobucci_ml]. Interactions and squares of *latent* variables are harder. The
established approaches are product-indicator methods (Kenny & Judd, 1984) and structural equation
mixture models (Bauer & Curran, 2004). The latent products are non-normal and the methods are
computationally expensive, and many of the traditional methods handle only one to five such terms. Local SEM and
moderated nonlinear factor analysis test nonlinearity one parameter at a time, which does not scale to
large models [@jacobucci_ml].

The authors' preferred route is **Bayesian SEM**. It does not rely on reproducing a covariance matrix,
so complicated functional forms are easier to write down, and it scales to more product terms
[@jacobucci_ml]. Their demonstration, run in Stan on the Big Five → Grit model, compares three models:

1. Linear effects of the five latent traits plus 12 demographics, with diffuse $N(0,1)$ priors. The
   estimates were almost identical to maximum likelihood, as expected with a large sample and weak
   priors.
2. All linear, quadratic and interaction terms among the five traits, with $N(0,1)$ priors. The
   frequentist `nlsem` package failed to converge on a model this size.
3. The same terms with a **hyperlasso** prior, a sparsity-inducing shrinkage prior, on the latent
   regression paths.

| Model | Grit1 $R^2$ | Grit2 $R^2$ |
|---|---|---|
| Maximum likelihood, linear | 46% | 60% |
| Bayesian, linear, diffuse priors | 47% | 62% |
| Bayesian, nonlinear, diffuse priors | 51% | 67% |
| Bayesian, nonlinear, hyperlasso | 50% | 65% |

The nonlinear terms added about 4 and 5 points, which the authors judge not negligible
[@jacobucci_ml]. There were quadratic effects of conscientiousness on hard work (positive, so the
negative relationship flattens at high conscientiousness), of neuroticism on persistence (negative, so
the relationship turns negative at high neuroticism) and of openness on hard work, and several
interactions involving conscientiousness. The hyperlasso did **not** behave like a variable-selection
tool here. It shrank the coefficients fairly uniformly and narrowed their credible intervals, but set
few of them near zero. The authors used it as an overfitting check: a restrictive prior should push many
coefficients to zero if the model has too many, and it did not. Test-set $R^2$ values for both priors
fell inside the training-set 95% credible intervals [@jacobucci_ml].

The $R^2$ of a latent outcome in a Bayesian model is computed from the sampled factor scores: the
variance of the predicted mean divided by that variance plus the residual variance
[@jacobucci_ml]. See the [appendix](#sec-latent-math).

## Why a "hyperlasso" and not a lasso? Shrinkage priors {#sec-priors}

Jacobucci et al. name the hyperlasso without explaining it. Murphy explains why such priors exist
[@murphy_ml]. The lasso is MAP estimation under a **Laplace** prior, and that prior has two weaknesses.
It has too little probability near zero, so it does not suppress noise strongly enough. It also has
too little probability far from zero, so it shrinks real, large coefficients: the lasso's well-known
**bias**. Priors with a sharper spike at zero and **heavier tails** fix both problems, at the price of
a non-convex optimisation in which the global optimum can no longer be guaranteed. Such methods often
beat the lasso both in prediction and in finding the relevant variables [@murphy_ml].

Murphy gives two routes:

- **Bridge regression** penalises $\sum_j |w_j|^b$. With $b = 2$ this is ridge, with $b = 1$ the lasso,
  and as $b \to 0$ it becomes best-subset selection. Below $b = 1$ the problem is non-convex. Above it,
  the penalty no longer produces exact zeros. That is why the lasso is the tightest **convex**
  approximation to best-subset selection [@murphy_ml].
- **Hierarchical priors** give each coefficient its own penalty. In the **hierarchical adaptive lasso**
  (HAL; Lee et al., 2010) each coefficient's prior variance has its own tuning parameter, itself drawn
  from a prior. Tuning a separate penalty for every coefficient by cross-validation would be
  impossible. For a Bayesian it is just one more level of the model. Fitted by EM, HAL becomes a
  **reweighted lasso**: each round, a coefficient that came out large gets a small penalty next time,
  and a small one gets a large penalty. The result is usually sparser than the lasso and less biased
  [@murphy_ml]. One EM step with flat hyperpriors is closely related to the **adaptive lasso**
  (Zou, 2006).

The figure's second panel shows the difference on a single coefficient. The lasso subtracts a constant
from every surviving estimate. HAL kills small estimates and leaves large ones almost untouched, which
is close to **hard thresholding** [@murphy_ml].

Many such priors exist. Murphy tabulates them all as **scale mixtures of normals**: each coefficient is
normal with its own variance, and different priors on that variance give the Laplace (lasso), the
normal–gamma, the normal–exponential–gamma, Student-$t$ and the **horseshoe** (Carvalho et al., 2010),
among others [@murphy_ml]. The improper normal–Jeffreys prior even has no tuning parameter, which
means nothing to tune but also no way to adjust how sparse the result is [@murphy_ml]. This is also
why Jacobucci et al. say that Bayesian regularisation needs only **one** model run. The hierarchy
learns how much to shrink, instead of a grid of $\lambda$ values [@jacobucci_ml]. (The horseshoe in a
regression setting is covered in [selection stability](selection-stability.qmd).)

```{python}
#| label: fig-latent-prediction
#| fig-cap: "Left: scoring a latent outcome first costs explained variance. A latent outcome is predicted by ten covariates with true R² = 0.70 (ink dashed line, what a MIMIC model targets). It is measured by six items whose loadings spread ±0.15 around the average on the x-axis. Regressing a factor score (dark grey) or a sum score (light grey) on the same covariates recovers only the score's reliability × 0.70. Lines are the formula, dots are simulations with 2,000 cases. Right: what three penalties do to one coefficient (orthogonal design, after Murphy Fig. 13.19). Dotted: no penalty. Ridge shrinks every estimate proportionally, the lasso subtracts a constant from every survivor, and the hierarchical adaptive lasso (HAL, a = 1, b = 0.1; orange) zeroes small estimates and leaves large ones nearly untouched. The lasso threshold is set equal to HAL's so both drop the same coefficients."
#| code-fold: true
#| code-summary: "Show the figure code"

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(8)

# --- (a) attenuation of R^2 when a latent outcome is scored first -------------
p, R2 = 10, 0.70
g = rng.normal(size=p)
spread = np.linspace(-0.15, 0.15, 6)

def reliabilities(m):
    lam = m + spread
    th = 1 - lam**2                                   # standardised items
    S = np.outer(lam, lam) + np.diag(th)              # implied item covariance
    rho_fs = lam @ np.linalg.solve(S, lam)            # regression factor score
    rho_sum = lam.sum()**2 / (lam.sum()**2 + th.sum())
    return lam, th, rho_fs, rho_sum

def r2(y, X):
    Z = np.column_stack([np.ones(len(X)), X])
    b = np.linalg.lstsq(Z, y, rcond=None)[0]
    return 1 - np.var(y - Z @ b) / np.var(y)

grid = np.linspace(0.30, 0.80, 51)
fs_line = [reliabilities(m)[2] * R2 for m in grid]
sum_line = [reliabilities(m)[3] * R2 for m in grid]

pts = np.linspace(0.30, 0.80, 6)
fs_sim, sum_sim = [], []
for m in pts:
    lam, th, _, _ = reliabilities(m)
    n = 2000
    X = rng.normal(size=(n, p))
    signal = X @ g / np.sqrt(g @ g)
    eta = np.sqrt(R2) * signal + np.sqrt(1 - R2) * rng.normal(size=n)
    Y = eta[:, None] * lam + rng.normal(size=(n, 6)) * np.sqrt(th)
    S = np.outer(lam, lam) + np.diag(th)
    fs_sim.append(r2(Y @ np.linalg.solve(S, lam), X))
    sum_sim.append(r2(Y.sum(1), X))

# --- (b) thresholding rules for one coefficient -------------------------------
a, b = 1.0, 0.1
w = np.linspace(0, 12, 120001)
def hal(v):
    return w[np.argmin(0.5 * (v - w)**2 + (a + 1) * np.log1p(w / b))]
w_mle = np.linspace(0, 10, 401)
w_hal = np.array([hal(v) for v in w_mle])
thr = w_mle[np.argmax(w_hal > 0)]                    # HAL's threshold
w_lasso = np.maximum(w_mle - thr, 0)
w_ridge = w_mle / 1.5

fig, (ax1, ax2) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(10, 4.4))

ax1.axhline(R2, color="#161613", ls="--", lw=1.4, label="latent outcome (MIMIC target)")
ax1.plot(grid, fs_line, color="#5e5e54", lw=2, label="factor score, then regression")
ax1.plot(grid, sum_line, color="#b7b6a8", lw=2, label="sum score, then regression")
ax1.plot(pts, fs_sim, "o", color="#5e5e54", ms=5)
ax1.plot(pts, sum_sim, "s", color="#b7b6a8", ms=5)
ax1.set_xlabel("average loading of the six items")
ax1.set_ylabel("$R^2$ explained by the ten covariates")
ax1.set_ylim(0.2, 0.75)
ax1.legend(frameon=False, fontsize=8.5, loc="lower right")
ax1.set_title("Scoring first loses variance", fontsize=10)

ax2.plot(w_mle, w_mle, ":", color="#b7b6a8", lw=1.4, label="no penalty")
ax2.plot(w_mle, w_ridge, color="#5e5e54", lw=1.6, label="ridge")
ax2.plot(w_mle, w_lasso, color="#161613", lw=1.6, label="lasso")
ax2.plot(w_mle, w_hal, color="#e8531d", lw=2.2, label="hierarchical adaptive lasso")
ax2.set_xlabel("unpenalised estimate")
ax2.set_ylabel("penalised estimate")
ax2.legend(frameon=False, fontsize=8.5, loc="upper left")
ax2.set_title(f"Shrinkage of one coefficient (threshold {thr:.2f})", fontsize=10)

fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

## Common pitfalls

::: {.callout-warning title="Where this goes wrong"}
- **Comparing a latent $R^2$ with a score-based $R^2$ as if they measured the same thing.** The latent
  version is free of measurement error in the outcome. The score-based one is capped by the score's
  reliability. A higher latent $R^2$ is partly just a different denominator. [@jacobucci_ml]
- **Letting the outcome define the predictor.** A path from a latent predictor to an observed outcome is
  formally another loading, so the outcome shapes the construct and flatters the prediction
  (interpretational confounding). Consider a two-step approach when the prediction is the point.
  [@jacobucci_ml]
- **Re-estimating the latent variables freely in the test set.** Then you test a different construct.
  Fix the measurement and structural parameters at their training values and free only the latent
  (residual) variances. [@jacobucci_ml]
- **Reporting training-set $R^2$ from a large MIMIC model.** With 63 predictors the hard-work $R^2$ fell
  from 0.90 to 0.59 in the holdout. [@jacobucci_ml]
- **Penalising every parameter in a RegSEM.** Loadings and residual variances depend on each other.
  Penalise only directed paths, usually the uncertain ones. [@jacobucci_ml]
- **Reading a hyperlasso or horseshoe posterior as a selection list.** In the Grit example the
  hyperlasso shrank coefficients uniformly and zeroed few. And under a Laplace prior only the
  posterior *mode* is sparse. [@jacobucci_ml; @murphy_ml]
- **Assuming the lasso's estimates are unbiased once selected.** The lasso shrinks the large, real
  coefficients too. Adaptive or hierarchical penalties reduce that bias. [@murphy_ml]
:::

## Appendix · the mathematics {#sec-latent-math .unnumbered}

::: {.math-appendix}

**MIMIC model.** With items $y$, latent variables $\eta$ and covariates $x$,
$$
y = \Lambda\eta + \varepsilon, \qquad \eta = \Gamma x + \zeta .
$$
The first equation is the reflective measurement model and the second the formative, "multiple
causes" part. The latent $R^2$ for factor $k$ is $\operatorname{Var}(\Gamma_k x)/\operatorname{Var}(\eta_k)$,
computed from the model parameters, not from scores.

**Why scoring first attenuates $R^2$ (the figure's left panel; our derivation).** Take one standardised
latent outcome, $\operatorname{Var}(\eta)=1$, with item covariance $\Sigma = \lambda\lambda' + \Theta$.
The regression factor score is $\hat\eta = w'y$ with $w = \Sigma^{-1}\lambda$. Then
$\operatorname{Cov}(\hat\eta, x) = (w'\lambda)\operatorname{Cov}(\eta, x)$ and
$\operatorname{Var}(\hat\eta) = w'\Sigma w = w'\lambda \equiv \rho_{\hat\eta}$, the score's reliability. So
$$
R^2_{\hat\eta \mid x} = \rho_{\hat\eta}\, R^2_{\eta \mid x}.
$$
> **In plain words:** regressing a score on the covariates recovers the latent $R^2$ multiplied by the
> score's reliability.

The same argument with $w = \mathbf 1$ gives the sum score, whose reliability is
$(\sum\lambda_i)^2/[(\sum\lambda_i)^2 + \sum\theta_i]$. In the figure at average loading 0.6 these are
0.80 and 0.77, giving $R^2$ of 0.56 and 0.54 against the latent 0.70. (In the book, the factor-score
$R^2$ is 0.84 and 0.82 times the MIMIC values. Their scores came from a different estimation, so this is
a consistency check of the direction, not a reproduction.)

**Interpretational confounding.** A path $\beta$ from $\eta$ to an observed outcome $z$, with
$z = \beta\eta + \delta$, has the form of a loading. In the implied covariance matrix, $z$ enters exactly
as one more indicator of $\eta$, so its covariances with the items help determine $\eta$.

**Regularised SEM.**
$$
F_{\text{regsem}} = F_{\text{ML}} + \lambda P(\cdot), \qquad
F_{\text{lasso}} = F_{\text{ML}} + \lambda\,\lVert (r_{11},\dots,r_{62,1},r_{12},\dots,r_{62,2}) \rVert_1 ,
$$
with $F_{\text{ML}} = \log\lvert\Sigma(\theta)\rvert + \operatorname{tr}\!\big(S\Sigma(\theta)^{-1}\big) - \log\lvert S\rvert - p$ the usual
maximum-likelihood discrepancy (standard form). Note on the source: the book's labels run $r_{11}$–$r_{621}$
and $r_{12}$–$r_{622}$, i.e. 62 covariates per Grit factor and 124 paths, while the MIMIC figure caption
counts 63 predictors (50 personality items plus 13 demographics). The entry reports both as printed.

**$R^2$ of a latent outcome in Bayesian SEM** (eqs 8.3–8.5):
$$
\mathrm{FS} \sim N(\mu, \psi^2), \qquad \mu = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X_1, \qquad
R^2_{\mathrm{FS}} = \frac{\operatorname{Var}(\mu)}{\operatorname{Var}(\mu) + \psi^2}.
$$
The book counts 38 linear, quadratic and interaction paths among five latent predictors for the two
outcomes. Five linear, five quadratic and ten pairwise interactions per outcome would make 40, so the
book's model presumably omitted two terms; the text does not say which.

**Bridge regression** (Murphy eq. 13.131):
$\hat w = \operatorname{NLL}(w) + \lambda\sum_j |w_j|^b$, the MAP estimate under an exponential power
prior.

**Hierarchical adaptive lasso** (eqs 13.133–13.143):
$$
\gamma_j \sim \mathrm{IG}(a, b), \qquad \tau_j^2 \mid \gamma_j \sim \mathrm{Ga}(1, \gamma_j^2/2), \qquad
w_j \mid \tau_j^2 \sim N(0, \tau_j^2).
$$
Integrating out $\tau_j^2$ gives a Laplace prior whose width is set by $\gamma_j$; integrating out
$\gamma_j$ as well gives
$$
p(w_j \mid a, b) = \frac{a}{2b}\Big(1 + \frac{|w_j|}{b}\Big)^{-(a+1)}, \qquad
\pi(w_j) = (a+1)\log\Big(1 + \frac{|w_j|}{b}\Big) + \text{const}.
$$
EM alternates $s_j = \mathbb E[1/\gamma_j] = (a+1)/(b + |w_j^{(t)}|)$ with the weighted lasso
$\hat w^{(t+1)} = \arg\min_w \lVert y - Xw\rVert_2^2 + \sum_j s_j|w_j|$.
> **In plain words:** a coefficient estimated at 5 gets weight $2/5.1 \approx 0.39$; one at 0.05 gets
> $2/0.15 \approx 13.3$, a penalty 34 times heavier (our arithmetic, $a = 1$, $b = 0.1$).

**Thresholding (the figure's right panel).** With an orthogonal design the problem separates, and each
coefficient solves $\min_{w}\tfrac12(\hat w^{\text{mle}} - w)^2 + \pi(w)$ (eq. 13.146). Ridge with
penalty $\tfrac{\lambda}{2}w^2$ gives $\hat w^{\text{mle}}/(1+\lambda)$ (the figure uses $\lambda = 0.5$).
The lasso gives soft thresholding, $\operatorname{sign}(\hat w)\,(|\hat w| - \lambda)_+$. HAL with
$a = 1$, $b = 0.1$, minimised numerically on a fine grid, is zero up to a threshold just under 4 and
then jumps, with an unpenalised estimate of 10 shrunk only to about 9.8, against the lasso's 6.2 at the
same threshold.
