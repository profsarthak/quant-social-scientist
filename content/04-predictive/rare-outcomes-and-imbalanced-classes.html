Rare Outcomes: Imbalanced Classes, Lift & the Cost of Errors

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source

When the thing you are hunting for is 1 case in 100, the default classifier learns to say no, accuracy applauds it, and even the ROC curve flatters it. The fix is partly better metrics, partly a different training diet, and mostly an honest statement of what each kind of mistake costs.

The outcomes we most want to predict, like fraud, default, a suicide attempt or a purchase, are usually the rare ones. Rarity breaks the default classifier twice. It trains badly, because the common class swamps the rare one and the model learns to predict “no”. It is also scored badly, because “no” for everyone is highly accurate. The remedies come in three layers. Use metrics that look at the rare class (precision–recall, lift). Change the training diet (undersample, oversample, reweight, synthesise). Then pick the cutoff from the costs of the two errors, not from the number 0.5.

Why it matters

Classification introduced the confusion matrix and warned that accuracy misleads when one class is rare. This entry is about what to do about it, because in applied work imbalance is the normal case. Bruce & Bruce’s examples are legitimate versus fraudulent insurance claims and browsers versus buyers at a web store. In each the rare class is the one of interest, and missing it costs more than a false alarm: a caught fraudulent claim may save thousands of dollars, while a wrongly flagged honest one only costs a careful second look. (Bruce et al. 2020) In social and behavioural research the picture is the same and often worse. Clinical outcomes such as a history of suicide attempts or a cancer diagnosis often have fewer than 10% positive cases, and at that level of imbalance Jacobucci, Grimm & Zhang warn that “a host of problems” appear in training, validation and assessment alike. (Jacobucci et al. 2023)

How rarity breaks the default machinery

The scoreboard lies. If only 0.1% of a web store’s visitors ever buy, a model that predicts “nobody buys” is 99.9% accurate and useless. Bruce & Bruce would rather have a model that is less accurate overall but good at picking out the buyers, even if it wrongly flags some browsers along the way. (Bruce et al. 2020) The honest benchmark is therefore not 50% but the base rate. Jacobucci et al. call it the no-information rate: the accuracy you would get by labelling every case as the majority class. (Jacobucci et al. 2023) Their worked example predicts a depression diagnosis from Big Five personality scores. The prevalence is 0.229, so classifying everyone as not depressed is already 77% accurate. The logistic model reaches 80%, which sounds respectable, but its sensitivity is 0.26: it catches about one depressed person in four. (Jacobucci et al. 2023) Accuracy hid almost the whole failure.

Training goes wrong too. Bruce & Bruce’s loan data has a true default rate of about 19%. Fit a logistic regression to the full, imbalanced training set and only 0.39% of loans are predicted to default, less than a twelfth of the real number. (Bruce et al. 2020) Their explanation: with so many good loans in the data, and the usual noise in the predictors, even a loan that will default is likely to resemble some good loans by chance. The common class outvotes it. The same model trained on a balanced sample predicts default for roughly half the loans.

A predicted positive may be mostly negatives. When 1s are rare, even a decent classifier’s flagged cases can be mostly 0s. That is the screening-test puzzle, and Bruce & Bruce point to mammograms: because the condition is rare, a positive result usually does not mean cancer. (Bruce et al. 2020) It is Bayes’ theorem in its most practical form. Precision depends on the base rate, and sensitivity and specificity alone cannot tell you what a positive result means.

Metrics that look at the rare class

Once the base rate is in view, several metrics correct for it, each in its own way: (Jacobucci et al. 2023)

  • Balanced accuracy is the plain average of sensitivity and specificity, so each class counts equally. Ordinary accuracy weights the classes by how common they are, which is the problem.
  • Kappa compares observed accuracy with the accuracy expected by chance given the table’s marginal totals. It is the same statistic used for inter-rater agreement. Jacobucci et al. cite Kuhn & Johnson’s rule of thumb that 0.30–0.50 is reasonable agreement. Kappa itself depends on prevalence, so they recommend reporting the prevalence- and bias-adjusted kappa (PABAK) alongside it. In the depression example PABAK is 0.59, and the gap between the two shows how much the imbalance attenuates kappa.
  • F1 is the harmonic mean of precision and recall: one number for how well the positive class is handled.
  • The precision–recall (PR) curve and the area under it (AUPRC). These ignore true negatives altogether and track only how the positives are found. Bruce & Bruce single out PR curves as “especially useful” for highly unbalanced outcomes. (Bruce et al. 2020)

The difference between the ROC curve and the PR curve is the main idea of this entry. The ROC curve plots the true-positive rate against the false-positive rate. Each rate is computed within its own class, so the ROC curve does not change when the class balance changes. That is a strength when you want to compare models. It is also why the ROC curve can look excellent when the classifier would be useless in practice. Jacobucci et al. show this with a simulation. They take a dataset with 200 positives and 400 negatives (AUC 0.64) and add 1,600 extra negatives that are trivially easy to rule out. The AUC climbs to 0.84 even though nothing about finding positives improved, while the AUPRC stays at about 0.29. (Jacobucci et al. 2023) Their own conclusion is measured: that example is extreme, and in realistic imbalance the AUC has not proved problematic (Jacobucci & Li, 2022). The ROC still weights both classes equally, though, and with a rare outcome you usually care far more about the positives. Chance level also differs. A useless model scores 0.5 on AUC whatever the prevalence. On AUPRC it scores the prevalence P/(P+N), so an AUPRC is only meaningful next to that baseline. (Jacobucci et al. 2023) For the depression model the two numbers are AUC 0.78 and AUPRC 0.57. The second is harder to interpret on its own but more useful for comparing models.

Lift: rank first, choose the cutoff later

A rare outcome usually forces the cutoff well below 0.5. Otherwise nearly every record is labelled 0, and in effect you choose to over-identify 1s because they matter more. That raises the question of where the cutoff should go. Lift lets you postpone the answer. (Bruce et al. 2020) Sort the records by predicted probability and ask how much better the model does in the top slice than picking blindly. If the top decile responds at 0.3% when the overall rate is 0.1%, the model has a lift of 3 in that decile. The cumulative gains chart plots the share of all positives captured (recall) against the share of records contacted. The lift curve is the ratio of that curve to the diagonal of random selection.

Bruce & Bruce note that decile gains charts are among the oldest tools in predictive modelling, and that they come from direct mail: post is expensive, so you send it only to the likeliest responders. (Bruce et al. 2020) The logic carries over to any problem with a budget. Their example is a tax authority that can afford only so many audits and uses a lift chart to decide where to draw the line between returns that are audited and returns that are left alone. This approach needs only a ranking, so even a method whose probabilities are biased is usable here, as long as it puts the cases in the right order. (Bruce et al. 2020)

NoteUplift is a different thing

“Uplift” is sometimes just a synonym for lift. Its more specific meaning comes from an A/B test where the treatment is fed into a predictive model as a predictor. You score each person once with the treatment set to A and once set to B, and the difference is that person’s predicted uplift. Marketers and political campaigns use it to decide which message to send to whom. (Bruce et al. 2020) That is a causal question about individual effects, and it belongs with heterogeneous treatment effects and A/B testing, not with lift charts.

import math
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

Phi = np.vectorize(lambda z: 0.5 * (1 + math.erf(z / math.sqrt(2))))

mu = 1.8                                   # positives ~ N(mu, 1), negatives ~ N(0, 1)
t = np.linspace(8, -8, 2001)               # thresholds, high -> low
tpr = 1 - Phi(t - mu)                      # recall / sensitivity
fpr = 1 - Phi(t)                           # 1 - specificity
auc = Phi(mu / math.sqrt(2))               # exact AUC for two unit-variance normals

cases = [(0.50, "50%", "#161613"), (0.10, "10%", "#8a8a7e"), (0.01, "1%", "#e8531d")]

fig, (axA, axB, axC) = plt.subplots(1, 3, figsize=(10.2, 3.6))

# Left: ROC -- one curve, whatever the base rate.
for pi, lab, col in cases:
    axA.plot(fpr, tpr, color=col, lw=2.2 if pi == 0.50 else 1.4,
             ls="-" if pi == 0.50 else (0, (4, 3)) if pi == 0.10 else (0, (1, 2)),
             label=f"prevalence {lab}")
axA.plot([0, 1], [0, 1], color="#b7b6a8", lw=1)
axA.text(0.38, 0.45, f"AUC = {auc:.2f}\nat every base rate", fontsize=8.5, color="#161613")
axA.set_xlabel("false-positive rate"); axA.set_ylabel("true-positive rate (recall)")
axA.set_title("ROC: blind to the base rate", fontsize=10)
axA.set_xlim(0, 1); axA.set_ylim(0, 1.02)
axA.legend(fontsize=7.5, frameon=False, loc="lower right")

for pi, lab, col in cases:

axB.set_xlabel("recall"); axB.set_ylabel("precision")
axB.set_title("Precision–recall: the base rate bites", fontsize=10)
axB.set_xlim(0, 1); axB.set_ylim(0, 1.02)

# Right: cumulative gains for the 1% case.
pi = 0.01
share = pi * tpr + (1 - pi) * fpr          # share of all records flagged
axC.plot(share, tpr, color="#e8531d", lw=2.2, label="model")
axC.plot([0, 1], [0, 1], color="#b7b6a8", lw=1, label="random targeting")

Changing the training diet

The second family of fixes changes what the model is trained on, so that the rare class is not outvoted. (Bruce et al. 2020; Jacobucci et al. 2023)

  • Undersample (downsample) the common class. If there is plenty of data, discard some of the majority cases. Bruce & Bruce argue that the majority class contains many redundant records, and a smaller balanced set is quicker to prepare, explore and pilot. As a rough guide, tens of thousands of minority-class records are usually enough, and fewer are needed the more easily the classes separate. (Bruce et al. 2020)
  • Oversample (upsample) the rare class. The objection to undersampling is that it throws away information. When the rare class has only a few hundred or a few thousand records, draw extra copies of them with replacement (a bootstrap) instead. (Bruce et al. 2020)
  • Reweight. Most fitting routines accept case weights. Give each default the weight 1/p, where p\approx 0.19 is the default rate, and give each repaid loan weight 1. The two classes then carry roughly equal total weight. In Bruce & Bruce’s loan example, the share of loans predicted to default jumps from 0.39% to about 58%. (Bruce et al. 2020) Weighting can stand in for both up- and downsampling. It is also the practical way to change the loss function: the literature proposes rare-class losses, but modifying a complex algorithm is hard, whereas weights simply count errors on heavy records more and errors on light records less.
  • Generate synthetic cases (SMOTE). Instead of exact copies, create new minority records that are similar but not identical to real ones, so the algorithm has a richer set of examples to learn rules from. Bruce & Bruce compare the spirit of this to bagging and boosting. SMOTE (Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique; Chawla et al., 2002) finds a nearest neighbour of a minority record and creates a synthetic record that is a randomly weighted average of the two, with a separate weight for each predictor. (Bruce et al. 2020; Jacobucci et al. 2023)

Does any of it work? Jacobucci et al.’s reading of the evidence is cautious, and worth quoting in substance. Among the simple methods, random oversampling (ROS) generally beats random undersampling (RUS). On computer-science benchmark datasets, any form of oversampling tends to beat none, and recent SMOTE variants do best. Those benchmarks, however, look little like social and behavioural data. Two 2022 simulation studies designed for that setting found much less. Goorbergh et al. found that resampling damaged calibration and did not improve AUC. Jacobucci & Li found only slight gains in AUC and AUPRC, with ROS doing as well as or better than SMOTE, and ROSE (another oversampling variant) performing poorly in some conditions. Their recommendation is to use plain random oversampling if you rebalance at all. (Jacobucci et al. 2023)

They also suggest a cheaper safeguard: stratified k-fold cross-validation. With a 10% outcome, ordinary random folds can end up with far fewer positives than that by chance. Stratification arranges each fold to carry roughly the full-sample share of positives. The evidence on it is mixed: it helped in classification (Kohavi, 1995) but not in regression (Breiman & Spector, 1992). Oversampling removes the same concern. (Jacobucci et al. 2023)

The price of rebalancing is calibration. A model trained on a 50/50 world predicts as if the world were 50/50. Bruce & Bruce’s reweighted loan model flags 58% of loans when the true rate is 19%. The ranking may be fine, but the probabilities are no longer probabilities of anything real. Jacobucci et al.’s check is the calibration plot. Bin the predictions by decile and plot each bin’s observed event rate against its predicted probability; a well-calibrated model sits on the 45° line. In their depression example the random forest’s predictions crowd toward 0 and 1, an overconfidence the data did not justify. (Jacobucci et al. 2023) If someone will read the outputs as risks, recalibrate or report rankings rather than probabilities. The appendix shows the one-line correction for reweighting.

The cutoff is a cost decision

Bruce & Bruce make the decisive point bluntly: accuracy and AUC are “a poor man’s way” to choose a classification rule. (Bruce et al. 2020) Usually you can put an estimated cost on a false positive and on a false negative, and those costs should set the cutoff. In their lending example, a defaulted loan loses an expected amount C and a repaid one earns an expected return R. The quantity to compute is not “default or not” but the loan’s expected return, (1-p)R - pC. The predicted probability is just an input to that calculation, and the profit it feeds into is what the business actually plans around. A small loan may be declined in favour of a larger one with a slightly higher default probability. (Bruce et al. 2020) Cost-based thinking reaches the same place as lift by another route: the answer depends on what the errors and the opportunities are worth, not on any property of the model alone.

Jacobucci et al. show how much the “optimal” cutoff depends on what you choose to optimise. (Jacobucci et al. 2023) For the depression model, maximising F1 picked a cutoff of 0.008, the smallest value tested, which is a symptom that the model barely separates the classes. When they changed the outcome to dichotomised neuroticism, where the model separates the classes better, the F1-optimal cutoff moved to a sensible 0.31. Choosing the depression cutoff instead from the ROC point that maximises sensitivity plus specificity gave 0.21. Extreme cutoffs usually cost a lot of performance, but for hard-to-reach populations, such as people with a history of suicide attempts, a low cutoff can be justified. (Jacobucci et al. 2023)

Finally, look at the predictions themselves. Bruce & Bruce fit four classifiers (LDA, logistic regression, a logistic GAM and a tree) to the loan data using just two predictors and draw each one’s decision boundary. LDA and logistic regression are nearly identical. The GAM is a compromise. The tree’s rule is so irregular that in places raising a borrower’s credit score switches the prediction from “paid off” to “default”. (Bruce et al. 2020) Two models with similar AUCs can encode very different rules, so, in their words, exploratory analysis of the predicted values is “always warranted”.

Common pitfalls

WarningWhere this goes wrong

  • Comparing accuracy with 50% instead of the base rate. A model is only useful if it beats labelling everyone as the majority class (the no-information rate). In the depression example that bar was 77%, and the model reached 80%. (Jacobucci et al. 2023)

  • Celebrating a high AUC on a rare outcome. The ROC curve ignores prevalence, so an AUC of 0.90 can sit alongside a model whose flagged cases are mostly false alarms. Report the PR curve, AUPRC or lift, and compare AUPRC with its chance level P/(P+N), not with 0.5. (Jacobucci et al. 2023; Bruce et al. 2020)

  • Rebalancing the evaluation data. Up- or downsampling is a way to train. Precision, lift and calibration all depend on prevalence, so they have to be measured on data with the real-world base rate, or they describe a population that does not exist (the middle panel above).

  • Reading rebalanced outputs as probabilities. A model trained on balanced data overstates risk: in Bruce & Bruce’s reweighted loan model, 58% of loans are flagged when the true default rate is 19%. Recalibrate before anyone reads the outputs as risks. (Bruce et al. 2020; Jacobucci et al. 2023)

  • Reaching for SMOTE by reflex. In simulations built to resemble social-science data it did no better than random oversampling, and resampling in general can harm calibration. (Jacobucci et al. 2023)

  • Leaving the cutoff at 0.5. With a rare outcome the 0.5 default flags almost nobody. Set the cutoff from the costs of the two errors or from the size of the budget, and choose it inside cross-validation. (Bruce et al. 2020; Jacobucci et al. 2023)

Appendix · the mathematics

Rare-class metrics. From the confusion-matrix counts, with \text{Sens}=\tfrac{TP}{TP+FN}, \text{Spec}=\tfrac{TN}{TN+FP}, \text{Prec}=\tfrac{TP}{TP+FP}: \text{Balanced accuracy}=\tfrac12(\text{Sens}+\text{Spec}),\qquad F_1=\frac{2\,\text{Prec}\cdot\text{Sens}}{\text{Prec}+\text{Sens}},\qquad \kappa=\frac{p_o-p_e}{1-p_e}, where p_o is observed accuracy and p_e is the accuracy expected by chance from the row and column totals. PABAK sets p_e=\tfrac12, which for two classes gives \text{PABAK}=2p_o-1.

Why the ROC curve ignores prevalence and the PR curve does not. Let \pi=\Pr(Y=1). At threshold t, \text{TPR}(t)=\Pr(s>t\mid Y=1) and \text{FPR}(t)=\Pr(s>t\mid Y=0). Neither involves \pi, so the ROC curve is the same at every base rate. Precision does involve it: \text{Prec}(t)=\frac{\pi\,\text{TPR}(t)}{\pi\,\text{TPR}(t)+(1-\pi)\,\text{FPR}(t)} . For a model with no skill, \text{TPR}=\text{FPR} at every t, and precision collapses to \pi. That is why the chance level of the PR curve, and of AUPRC, is the prevalence P/(P+N).

Lift. If you flag the top fraction q of records, the share of all positives you capture is \text{TPR}, and q=\pi\,\text{TPR}+(1-\pi)\,\text{FPR}. The lift at depth q is \text{TPR}/q, which is also the response rate among those flagged divided by \pi. Random targeting has lift 1.

The cost-optimal cutoff. Predict 1 when the expected cost of saying 0 exceeds the expected cost of saying 1: p\,C_{FN} > (1-p)\,C_{FP}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad p > p^\ast=\frac{C_{FP}}{C_{FP}+C_{FN}} . Equal costs give the familiar 0.5. If a missed positive costs nine times a false alarm, p^\ast=0.1. Bruce & Bruce’s lending rule is the same calculation in profit terms: approve when (1-p)R-pC>0, that is when p<R/(R+C).

What reweighting does to the probabilities. Give every positive weight w and every negative weight 1. In the reweighted population the conditional odds become \frac{\Pr_w(Y=1\mid x)}{\Pr_w(Y=0\mid x)} = w\cdot\frac{\Pr(Y=1\mid x)}{\Pr(Y=0\mid x)} , so a correctly specified logistic model with an intercept shifts its logit by exactly \ln w and leaves the slopes unchanged. Rankings are preserved and probabilities are inflated. The correction is to subtract \ln w from the fitted logit, or equivalently divide the fitted odds by w. In Bruce & Bruce’s loan example w=1/0.19\approx5.3, so every fitted odds is multiplied by about 5.3. That is why so many more loans cross the 0.5 line after reweighting. Oversampling the positives w-fold has the same effect in expectation, and undersampling the negatives at rate 1/w does too.

::: {.lead-kernel}
The outcomes we most want to predict, like fraud, default, a suicide attempt or a purchase, are usually the
**rare** ones. Rarity breaks the default classifier twice. It **trains** badly, because the common class
swamps the rare one and the model learns to predict "no". It is also **scored** badly, because "no" for
everyone is highly accurate. The remedies come in three layers. Use **metrics that look at the rare class**
(precision–recall, lift). Change the **training diet** (undersample, oversample, reweight, synthesise).
Then pick the cutoff from the **costs of the two errors**, not from the number 0.5.
:::

## Why it matters

[Classification](classification-and-knn.qmd) introduced the confusion matrix and warned that accuracy
misleads when one class is rare. This entry is about what to *do* about it, because in applied work
imbalance is the normal case. Bruce & Bruce's examples are legitimate versus fraudulent insurance
claims and browsers versus buyers at a web store. In each the rare class is the one of interest, and
missing it costs more than a false alarm: a caught fraudulent claim may save thousands of dollars, while a
wrongly flagged honest one only costs a careful second look. [@bruce_practical] In social and behavioural
research the picture is the same and often worse. Clinical outcomes such as a history of suicide attempts
or a cancer diagnosis often have **fewer than 10% positive cases**, and at that level of imbalance
Jacobucci, Grimm & Zhang warn that "a host of problems" appear in training, validation and assessment
alike. [@jacobucci_ml]

## How rarity breaks the default machinery

**The scoreboard lies.** If only 0.1% of a web store's visitors ever buy, a model that predicts "nobody
buys" is 99.9% accurate and useless. Bruce & Bruce would rather have a model that is *less* accurate
overall but good at picking out the buyers, even if it wrongly flags some browsers along the way.
[@bruce_practical] The honest benchmark is therefore not 50% but the **base rate**. Jacobucci et al. call
it the **no-information rate**: the accuracy you would get by labelling every case as the majority class.
[@jacobucci_ml] Their worked example predicts a depression diagnosis from Big Five personality scores. The
prevalence is 0.229, so classifying everyone as not depressed is already **77%** accurate. The logistic
model reaches **80%**, which sounds respectable, but its **sensitivity is 0.26**: it catches about one
depressed person in four. [@jacobucci_ml] Accuracy hid almost the whole failure.

**Training goes wrong too.** Bruce & Bruce's loan data has a true default rate of about **19%**. Fit a
logistic regression to the full, imbalanced training set and only **0.39%** of loans are predicted to
default, less than a twelfth of the real number. [@bruce_practical] Their explanation: with so many good
loans in the data, and the usual noise in the predictors, even a loan that will default is likely to
resemble some good loans by chance. The common class outvotes it. The same model trained on a *balanced*
sample predicts default for roughly half the loans.

**A predicted positive may be mostly negatives.** When 1s are rare, even a decent classifier's flagged
cases can be mostly 0s. That is the screening-test puzzle, and Bruce & Bruce point to mammograms: because
the condition is rare, a positive result usually does not mean cancer. [@bruce_practical] It is
[Bayes' theorem](../01-foundations/bayes-theorem.qmd) in its most practical form. Precision depends on the
base rate, and sensitivity and specificity alone cannot tell you what a positive result means.

## Metrics that look at the rare class

Once the base rate is in view, several metrics correct for it, each in its own way: [@jacobucci_ml]

- **Balanced accuracy** is the plain average of sensitivity and specificity, so each class counts equally.
  Ordinary accuracy weights the classes by how common they are, which is the problem.
- **Kappa** compares observed accuracy with the accuracy expected by chance given the table's marginal
  totals. It is the same statistic used for
  [inter-rater agreement](../00-overture/concepts-and-measurement.qmd). Jacobucci et al. cite Kuhn &
  Johnson's rule of thumb that 0.30–0.50 is reasonable agreement. Kappa itself depends on prevalence, so
  they recommend reporting the **prevalence- and bias-adjusted kappa (PABAK)** alongside it. In the
  depression example PABAK is 0.59, and the gap between the two shows how much the imbalance attenuates
  kappa.
- **F1** is the harmonic mean of precision and recall: one number for how well the positive class is
  handled.
- The **precision–recall (PR) curve** and the **area under it (AUPRC)**. These ignore true negatives
  altogether and track only how the positives are found. Bruce & Bruce single out PR curves as
  "especially useful" for highly unbalanced outcomes. [@bruce_practical]

The difference between the ROC curve and the PR curve is the main idea of this entry. The ROC curve plots
the true-positive rate against the false-positive rate. Each rate is computed *within* its own class, so
**the ROC curve does not change when the class balance changes**. That is a strength when you want to
compare models. It is also why the ROC curve can look excellent when the classifier would be useless in
practice. Jacobucci et al. show this with a simulation. They take a dataset with 200 positives and 400
negatives (AUC 0.64) and add 1,600 extra negatives that are trivially easy to rule out. The AUC climbs to
**0.84** even though nothing about finding positives improved, while the AUPRC stays at about **0.29**.
[@jacobucci_ml] Their own conclusion is measured: that example is extreme, and in realistic imbalance the
AUC has not proved problematic (Jacobucci & Li, 2022). The ROC still weights both classes equally,
though, and with a rare outcome you usually care far more about the positives. **Chance level also
differs.** A useless model scores 0.5 on AUC whatever the prevalence. On AUPRC it scores the
**prevalence** $P/(P+N)$, so an AUPRC is only meaningful next to that baseline. [@jacobucci_ml] For the
depression model the two numbers are AUC 0.78 and AUPRC 0.57. The second is harder to interpret on its
own but more useful for comparing models.

## Lift: rank first, choose the cutoff later

A rare outcome usually forces the cutoff well **below** 0.5. Otherwise nearly every record is labelled 0,
and in effect you choose to over-identify 1s because they matter more. That raises the question of where
the cutoff should go. **Lift** lets you postpone the answer. [@bruce_practical] Sort the records by
predicted probability and ask how much better the model does in the top slice than picking blindly. If
the top decile responds at 0.3% when the overall rate is 0.1%, the model has a **lift of 3** in that
decile. The **cumulative gains chart** plots the share of all positives captured (recall) against the
share of records contacted. The **lift curve** is the ratio of that curve to the diagonal of random
selection.

Bruce & Bruce note that decile gains charts are among the oldest tools in predictive modelling, and that
they come from **direct mail**: post is expensive, so you send it only to the likeliest responders.
[@bruce_practical] The logic carries over to any problem with a **budget**. Their example is a tax
authority that can afford only so many audits and uses a lift chart to decide where to draw the line
between returns that are audited and returns that are left alone. This approach needs only a **ranking**,
so even a method whose probabilities are biased is usable here, as long as it puts the cases in the right
order. [@bruce_practical]

::: {.callout-note title="Uplift is a different thing"}
"Uplift" is sometimes just a synonym for lift. Its more specific meaning comes from an A/B test where the
treatment is fed into a predictive model as a predictor. You score each person once with the treatment
set to A and once set to B, and the difference is that person's predicted **uplift**. Marketers and
political campaigns use it to decide which message to send to whom. [@bruce_practical] That is a
causal question about individual effects, and it belongs with
[heterogeneous treatment effects](../05-causal-inference/heterogeneous-effects-in-experiments.qmd) and
[A/B testing](../02-inference/ab-testing-and-bandits.qmd), not with lift charts.
:::

```{python}

#| fig-cap: "One classifier, three base rates. The score distributions are fixed (positives sit 1.8 SD above negatives), and every curve is computed exactly from them. Left: the ROC curve is the same at 50%, 10% and 1% prevalence, AUC ≈ 0.90 each time, because both of its axes are rates within a class. Middle: the precision–recall curve collapses as the outcome gets rarer. At 1% prevalence (orange) precision stays low across most of the recall range, and each curve's chance line (dashed) is its own prevalence, not 0.5. Right: the cumulative gains chart for the 1% case. Contacting the top-scored 10% of records captures most of the positives (the dot), a large top-decile lift over random targeting (diagonal), even though most of those contacted are still negatives."
#| code-fold: true

import math
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

Phi = np.vectorize(lambda z: 0.5 * (1 + math.erf(z / math.sqrt(2))))

mu = 1.8                                   # positives ~ N(mu, 1), negatives ~ N(0, 1)
t = np.linspace(8, -8, 2001)               # thresholds, high -> low
tpr = 1 - Phi(t - mu)                      # recall / sensitivity
fpr = 1 - Phi(t)                           # 1 - specificity
auc = Phi(mu / math.sqrt(2))               # exact AUC for two unit-variance normals

cases = [(0.50, "50%", "#161613"), (0.10, "10%", "#8a8a7e"), (0.01, "1%", "#e8531d")]

fig, (axA, axB, axC) = plt.subplots(1, 3, figsize=(10.2, 3.6))

# Left: ROC -- one curve, whatever the base rate.
for pi, lab, col in cases:
    axA.plot(fpr, tpr, color=col, lw=2.2 if pi == 0.50 else 1.4,
             ls="-" if pi == 0.50 else (0, (4, 3)) if pi == 0.10 else (0, (1, 2)),
             label=f"prevalence {lab}")
axA.plot([0, 1], [0, 1], color="#b7b6a8", lw=1)
axA.text(0.38, 0.45, f"AUC = {auc:.2f}\nat every base rate", fontsize=8.5, color="#161613")
axA.set_xlabel("false-positive rate"); axA.set_ylabel("true-positive rate (recall)")
axA.set_title("ROC: blind to the base rate", fontsize=10)
axA.set_xlim(0, 1); axA.set_ylim(0, 1.02)
axA.legend(fontsize=7.5, frameon=False, loc="lower right")

# Middle: precision-recall -- the base rate is everything.
label_xy = {0.50: (0.40, 0.84), 0.10: (0.60, 0.63), 0.01: (0.40, 0.25)}   # hand-placed between curves
for pi, lab, col in cases:
    prec = pi * tpr / (pi * tpr + (1 - pi) * fpr)
    axB.plot(tpr, prec, color=col, lw=2.0)
    axB.axhline(pi, color=col, lw=0.9, ls="--", alpha=0.8)
    ap = np.sum(np.diff(tpr) * prec[1:])   # area under the PR curve
    axB.text(*label_xy[pi], f"{lab}: AUPRC {ap:.2f}", fontsize=8, color=col)
axB.set_xlabel("recall"); axB.set_ylabel("precision")
axB.set_title("Precision–recall: the base rate bites", fontsize=10)
axB.set_xlim(0, 1); axB.set_ylim(0, 1.02)

# Right: cumulative gains for the 1% case.
pi = 0.01
share = pi * tpr + (1 - pi) * fpr          # share of all records flagged
axC.plot(share, tpr, color="#e8531d", lw=2.2, label="model")
axC.plot([0, 1], [0, 1], color="#b7b6a8", lw=1, label="random targeting")
k = np.searchsorted(share, 0.10)
axC.plot(share[k], tpr[k], "o", color="#e8531d", ms=6)
axC.annotate(f"top 10% of records\ncatch {tpr[k]:.0%} of positives\n(lift ≈ {tpr[k] / share[k]:.1f})",
             xy=(share[k], tpr[k]), xytext=(0.30, 0.42), fontsize=8, color="#161613",
             arrowprops=dict(arrowstyle="-", color="#5e5e54", lw=0.8))
axC.set_xlabel("share of records contacted"); axC.set_ylabel("share of positives captured")
axC.set_title("Gains chart at 1% prevalence", fontsize=10)
axC.set_xlim(0, 1); axC.set_ylim(0, 1.02)
axC.legend(fontsize=7.5, frameon=False, loc="lower right")

fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

## Changing the training diet

The second family of fixes changes **what the model is trained on**, so that the rare class is not
outvoted. [@bruce_practical; @jacobucci_ml]

- **Undersample (downsample) the common class.** If there is plenty of data, discard some of the
  majority cases. Bruce & Bruce argue that the majority class contains many **redundant** records, and a
  smaller balanced set is quicker to prepare, explore and pilot. As a rough guide, tens of thousands of
  minority-class records are usually enough, and fewer are needed the more easily the classes separate.
  [@bruce_practical]
- **Oversample (upsample) the rare class.** The objection to undersampling is that it **throws away
  information**. When the rare class has only a few hundred or a few thousand records, draw extra copies
  of them with replacement (a [bootstrap](../02-inference/resampling-and-nonparametric-tests.qmd))
  instead. [@bruce_practical]
- **Reweight.** Most fitting routines accept case weights. Give each default the weight $1/p$, where
  $p\approx 0.19$ is the default rate, and give each repaid loan weight 1. The two classes then carry
  roughly equal total weight. In Bruce & Bruce's loan example, the share of loans predicted to default
  jumps from 0.39% to about **58%**. [@bruce_practical] Weighting can stand in for both up- and
  downsampling. It is also the practical way to **change the loss function**: the literature proposes
  rare-class losses, but modifying a complex algorithm is hard, whereas weights simply count errors on
  heavy records more and errors on light records less.
- **Generate synthetic cases (SMOTE).** Instead of exact copies, create new minority records that are
  *similar but not identical* to real ones, so the algorithm has a richer set of examples to learn rules
  from. Bruce & Bruce compare the spirit of this to bagging and boosting. **SMOTE** (Synthetic Minority
  Oversampling Technique; Chawla et al., 2002) finds a
  [nearest neighbour](classification-and-knn.qmd) of a minority record and creates a synthetic record
  that is a randomly weighted average of the two, with a separate weight for each predictor.
  [@bruce_practical; @jacobucci_ml]

**Does any of it work?** Jacobucci et al.'s reading of the evidence is cautious, and worth quoting in
substance. Among the simple methods, random oversampling (ROS) generally beats random undersampling
(RUS). On computer-science benchmark datasets, any form of oversampling tends to beat none, and recent
SMOTE variants do best. Those benchmarks, however, look little like social and behavioural data. Two
2022 simulation studies designed for that setting found much less. Goorbergh et al. found that resampling
**damaged calibration** and did not improve AUC. Jacobucci & Li found only **slight** gains in AUC and
AUPRC, with ROS doing as well as or better than SMOTE, and ROSE (another oversampling variant) performing
poorly in some conditions. Their recommendation is to use plain **random oversampling** if you rebalance
at all. [@jacobucci_ml]

They also suggest a cheaper safeguard: **stratified k-fold cross-validation**. With a 10% outcome,
ordinary random folds can end up with far fewer positives than that by chance. Stratification arranges
each fold to carry roughly the full-sample share of positives. The evidence on it is mixed: it helped in
classification (Kohavi, 1995) but not in regression (Breiman & Spector, 1992). Oversampling removes the
same concern. [@jacobucci_ml]

**The price of rebalancing is calibration.** A model trained on a 50/50 world predicts as if the world
were 50/50. Bruce & Bruce's reweighted loan model flags 58% of loans when the true rate is 19%. The
*ranking* may be fine, but the probabilities are no longer probabilities of anything real. Jacobucci et
al.'s check is the **calibration plot**. Bin the predictions by decile and plot each bin's observed event
rate against its predicted probability; a well-calibrated model sits on the 45° line. In their depression
example the random forest's predictions crowd toward 0 and 1, an overconfidence the data did not
justify. [@jacobucci_ml] If someone will read the outputs as risks, recalibrate or report rankings rather
than probabilities. The appendix shows the one-line correction for reweighting.

## The cutoff is a cost decision

Bruce & Bruce make the decisive point bluntly: accuracy and AUC are "a poor man's way" to choose a
classification rule. [@bruce_practical] Usually you can put an estimated cost on a false positive and on
a false negative, and those costs should set the cutoff. In their lending example, a defaulted loan
loses an expected amount $C$ and a repaid one earns an expected return $R$. The quantity to compute is
not "default or not" but the loan's **expected return**, $(1-p)R - pC$. The predicted probability is
just an input to that calculation, and the profit it feeds into is what the business actually plans
around. A small loan may be declined in favour of a larger one with a slightly higher default
probability. [@bruce_practical] Cost-based thinking reaches the same place as lift by another route: the
answer depends on what the errors and the opportunities are worth, not on any property of the model
alone.

Jacobucci et al. show how much the "optimal" cutoff depends on what you choose to optimise.
[@jacobucci_ml] For the depression model, maximising F1 picked a cutoff of **0.008**, the smallest value
tested, which is a symptom that the model barely separates the classes. When they changed the outcome to
dichotomised neuroticism, where the model separates the classes better, the F1-optimal cutoff moved to a
sensible **0.31**. Choosing the depression cutoff instead from the ROC point that maximises sensitivity
plus specificity gave **0.21**. Extreme cutoffs usually cost a lot of performance, but for hard-to-reach
populations, such as people with a history of suicide attempts, a low cutoff can be justified.
[@jacobucci_ml]

Finally, look at the predictions themselves. Bruce & Bruce fit four classifiers (LDA, logistic
regression, a logistic GAM and a tree) to the loan data using just two predictors and draw each one's
decision boundary. LDA and logistic regression are nearly identical. The GAM is a compromise. The tree's
rule is so irregular that in places *raising* a borrower's credit score switches the prediction from
"paid off" to "default". [@bruce_practical] Two models with similar AUCs can encode very different
rules, so, in their words, exploratory analysis of the predicted values is "always warranted".

## Common pitfalls

::: {.callout-warning title="Where this goes wrong"}

- **Comparing accuracy with 50% instead of the base rate.** A model is only useful if it beats labelling
  everyone as the majority class (the no-information rate). In the depression example that bar was 77%,
  and the model reached 80%. [@jacobucci_ml]

- **Celebrating a high AUC on a rare outcome.** The ROC curve ignores prevalence, so an AUC of 0.90 can
  sit alongside a model whose flagged cases are mostly false alarms. Report the PR curve, AUPRC or lift,
  and compare AUPRC with its chance level $P/(P+N)$, not with 0.5. [@jacobucci_ml; @bruce_practical]

- **Rebalancing the evaluation data.** Up- or downsampling is a way to *train*. Precision, lift and
  calibration all depend on prevalence, so they have to be measured on data with the real-world base
  rate, or they describe a population that does not exist (the middle panel above).

- **Reading rebalanced outputs as probabilities.** A model trained on balanced data overstates risk: in
  Bruce & Bruce's reweighted loan model, 58% of loans are flagged when the true default rate is 19%.
  Recalibrate before anyone reads the outputs as risks. [@bruce_practical; @jacobucci_ml]

- **Reaching for SMOTE by reflex.** In simulations built to resemble social-science data it did no better
  than random oversampling, and resampling in general can harm calibration. [@jacobucci_ml]

- **Leaving the cutoff at 0.5.** With a rare outcome the 0.5 default flags almost nobody. Set the cutoff
  from the costs of the two errors or from the size of the budget, and choose it inside
  [cross-validation](cross-validation.qmd). [@bruce_practical; @jacobucci_ml]

:::

## Appendix · the mathematics {.unnumbered}

::: {.math-appendix}

**Rare-class metrics.** From the confusion-matrix counts, with $\text{Sens}=\tfrac{TP}{TP+FN}$,
$\text{Spec}=\tfrac{TN}{TN+FP}$, $\text{Prec}=\tfrac{TP}{TP+FP}$:
$$
\text{Balanced accuracy}=\tfrac12(\text{Sens}+\text{Spec}),\qquad
F_1=\frac{2\,\text{Prec}\cdot\text{Sens}}{\text{Prec}+\text{Sens}},\qquad
\kappa=\frac{p_o-p_e}{1-p_e},
$$
where $p_o$ is observed accuracy and $p_e$ is the accuracy expected by chance from the row and column
totals. PABAK sets $p_e=\tfrac12$, which for two classes gives $\text{PABAK}=2p_o-1$.

**Why the ROC curve ignores prevalence and the PR curve does not.** Let $\pi=\Pr(Y=1)$. At threshold
$t$, $\text{TPR}(t)=\Pr(s>t\mid Y=1)$ and $\text{FPR}(t)=\Pr(s>t\mid Y=0)$. Neither involves $\pi$, so
the ROC curve is the same at every base rate. Precision does involve it:
$$
\text{Prec}(t)=\frac{\pi\,\text{TPR}(t)}{\pi\,\text{TPR}(t)+(1-\pi)\,\text{FPR}(t)} .
$$
For a model with no skill, $\text{TPR}=\text{FPR}$ at every $t$, and precision collapses to $\pi$. That
is why the chance level of the PR curve, and of AUPRC, is the prevalence $P/(P+N)$.

**Lift.** If you flag the top fraction $q$ of records, the share of all positives you capture is
$\text{TPR}$, and $q=\pi\,\text{TPR}+(1-\pi)\,\text{FPR}$. The lift at depth $q$ is
$\text{TPR}/q$, which is also the response rate among those flagged divided by $\pi$. Random targeting
has lift 1.

**The cost-optimal cutoff.** Predict 1 when the expected cost of saying 0 exceeds the expected cost of
saying 1:
$$
p\,C_{FN} > (1-p)\,C_{FP}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad p > p^\ast=\frac{C_{FP}}{C_{FP}+C_{FN}} .
$$
Equal costs give the familiar 0.5. If a missed positive costs nine times a false alarm, $p^\ast=0.1$.
Bruce & Bruce's lending rule is the same calculation in profit terms: approve when
$(1-p)R-pC>0$, that is when $p<R/(R+C)$.

**What reweighting does to the probabilities.** Give every positive weight $w$ and every negative weight
1. In the reweighted population the conditional odds become
$$
\frac{\Pr_w(Y=1\mid x)}{\Pr_w(Y=0\mid x)} = w\cdot\frac{\Pr(Y=1\mid x)}{\Pr(Y=0\mid x)} ,
$$
so a correctly specified logistic model with an intercept shifts its logit by exactly $\ln w$ and leaves
the slopes unchanged. Rankings are preserved and probabilities are inflated. The correction is to
subtract $\ln w$ from the fitted logit, or equivalently divide the fitted odds by $w$. In Bruce &
Bruce's loan example $w=1/0.19\approx5.3$, so every fitted odds is multiplied by about 5.3. That is why so
many more loans cross the 0.5 line after reweighting. Oversampling the
positives $w$-fold has the same effect in expectation, and undersampling the negatives at rate $1/w$ does
too.

:::
