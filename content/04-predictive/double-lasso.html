Double Lasso: Honest Inference After Choosing Controls

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source

Picking controls by how well they predict the outcome quietly drops the confounders that matter. Select twice: once for the outcome, once for the treatment.

When you have more candidate controls than you can sensibly use, the Lasso can choose them. But if you choose only the ones that predict the outcome, you throw away controls that are weak predictors of the outcome and strong predictors of the regressor you care about, and those are the confounders. Double Lasso runs two prediction problems, one for Y and one for D, partials both out, and then regresses residual on residual. That estimator is built so that small mistakes in the two prediction steps do not move the answer.

Why it matters

Regularization was built for prediction: it trades a little bias for a lot less variance, and it is judged on how well it forecasts new data. Much of social science needs something else, namely a single coefficient with an honest confidence interval. What happens to growth when initial income is higher, holding institutions and schooling fixed? How big is the gender wage gap once hundreds of job characteristics are held fixed?

Chernozhukov and coauthors put the problem in one line: with high-dimensional controls, “good prediction is not enough”, and you need procedures designed for inference (Chernozhukov et al. 2024). Their running example is the growth-convergence question. The Barro–Lee sample has 90 countries and about 60 controls, so p/n is far from small. Ordinary least squares with every control is legal but nearly useless there. Its estimate is noisy, and the book notes that conventional robust standard errors are not even consistent when p/n is not small (Chernozhukov et al. 2024). You need to throw most controls away, but the way you throw them away decides whether the resulting interval can be trusted.

This entry is the bridge between the Lasso as a forecasting tool and double/debiased machine learning, which is the same idea with any ML learner in the prediction steps.

Start from something you already know: partialling out

The Frisch–Waugh–Lovell theorem says that in Y = \alpha D + \beta' W + \varepsilon, the coefficient \alpha can be found in two steps:

  1. Remove from Y everything that W can linearly explain, leaving \tilde Y. Do the same for D, leaving \tilde D.
  2. Regress \tilde Y on \tilde D. The slope is \alpha.

In words: \alpha is how the unexplained part of the outcome moves with the unexplained part of the regressor (Chernozhukov et al. 2024). Step 1 is two prediction problems, and prediction is exactly what the Lasso is good at when W is large. So the Double Lasso recipe is (Chernozhukov et al. 2024):

  1. Lasso Y on W; keep the residuals \check Y.
  2. Lasso D on W; keep the residuals \check D.
  3. Regress \check Y on \check D by least squares, and use an ordinary heteroskedasticity-robust standard error, as if the residuals had been observed.

That last step looks too good to be true. You estimated two whole prediction rules and then ignore that uncertainty? The book’s theorem says yes: under approximate sparsity and sensible tuning, the estimation error in \check Y and \check D has no first-order effect on \hat\alpha, and the estimator behaves like least squares with the true residuals (Chernozhukov et al. 2024). The reason has a name.

Why it works: Neyman orthogonality

Think of \hat\alpha as a function of the two prediction rules it was built from. Those rules are regularized, so they are slightly wrong: the Lasso shrinks, and it misses small coefficients. The question is how much a slightly wrong prediction rule moves \alpha.

For the partialled-out equation the answer is: not at all, to first order. Nudge either prediction rule a little away from the truth and the derivative of \alpha with respect to that nudge is zero. The book calls this local insensitivity Neyman orthogonality and describes it as the property that ensures the regularization bias in the nuisance parts “does not transmit to the estimation of the target parameter, at least to the first order” (Chernozhukov et al. 2024). An error of size \delta in the prediction step shows up in \hat\alpha only as something like \delta^2, which shrinks fast enough to ignore.

Two remarks from the book are worth keeping (Chernozhukov et al. 2024):

  • In a pure randomized experiment, D is unrelated to W, so orthogonality holds automatically. The problem this entry solves is an observational-data problem.
  • The requirement on sparsity is stronger than for prediction. The sorted coefficients must fall off faster than 1/j, because the prediction errors must be small enough that their products vanish at the \sqrt n rate. Good prediction alone is not the target.

Why the obvious shortcut fails: single selection

The tempting alternative is single selection: run one Lasso of Y on (D, W), keep whichever controls survive, refit by least squares, and report the usual interval. Chernozhukov and coauthors say this is “a fine approach if the goal is solely the prediction of the outcome”, but it “can result in very misleading conclusions” about \alpha (Chernozhukov et al. 2024).

The mechanism is omitted-variable bias with a machine-learning accent. The single Lasso keeps controls that help predict Y given D is already in the model. A control that affects Y weakly but drives D strongly looks useless for predicting Y, since D already carries most of its information, and it is dropped. In the book’s words, “weak predictors of the outcome could still be strong predictors of D”, and dropping them “results in a strong omitted variable bias” (Chernozhukov et al. 2024). Double Lasso instead finds controls that matter for either equation, so the residuals are approximately “de-confounded”.

The book demonstrates this with a simulation (its Example 4.3.1): p = n = 100, the controls’ effects on both Y and D fall off as 1/j^2, and the true \alpha = 1. The naive estimator is “heavily biased”, while Double Lasso is “approximately unbiased and Gaussian” (Chernozhukov et al. 2024). The figure below rebuilds that experiment by hand.

Show the figure code 
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from math import erf, sqrt, log

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n = p = 100
coef = 1 / np.arange(1, p + 1) ** 2          # effect of control j on Y and on D

def norm_ppf(q):                              # normal quantile by bisection
    lo, hi = 0.0, 10.0
    for _ in range(80):
        m = (lo + hi) / 2
        lo, hi = (m, hi) if 0.5 * (1 + erf(m / sqrt(2))) < q else (lo, m)
    return lo

def lasso_selected(X, y, lam):                # coordinate descent, standardised columns
    Xs = (X - X.mean(0)) / X.std(0)
    b, r = np.zeros(X.shape[1]), y - y.mean()
    for _ in range(300):
        biggest = 0
        for j in range(X.shape[1]):
            old = b[j]
            rho = Xs[:, j] @ r / len(y) + old
            b[j] = np.sign(rho) * max(abs(rho) - lam, 0)
            if b[j] != old:
                r -= Xs[:, j] * (b[j] - old)
                biggest = max(biggest, abs(b[j] - old))
        if biggest < 1e-7:
            break
    return b != 0

def resid(v, X):                              # least-squares residuals on selected controls
    Z = np.column_stack([np.ones(len(v)), X])
    return v - Z @ np.linalg.lstsq(Z, v, rcond=None)[0]

def plugin_lasso(X, y):                       # plug-in penalty, noise level re-estimated
    N, k = X.shape
    c = 1.1 * norm_ppf(1 - 0.1 / log(N) / (2 * k)) / np.sqrt(N)
    s = y.std()
    for _ in range(5):
        sel = lasso_selected(X, y, c * s)
        s = resid(y, X[:, sel]).std()
    return sel

single, double = [], []
for rep in range(300):
    W = rng.normal(size=(n, p))
    D = W @ coef + rng.normal(size=n) / 4
    Y = 1.0 * D + W @ coef + rng.normal(size=n)
    keep = plugin_lasso(np.column_stack([D, W]), Y)[1:]      # single selection
    rd = resid(D, W[:, keep])
    single.append(rd @ resid(Y, W[:, keep]) / (rd @ rd))
    ry = resid(Y, W[:, plugin_lasso(W, Y)])                   # Double Lasso
    rd = resid(D, W[:, plugin_lasso(W, D)])
    double.append(rd @ ry / (rd @ rd))

single, double = np.array(single), np.array(double)
bins = np.linspace(-0.4, 2.8, 41)
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(8.8, 4.4))
ax.hist(single, bins=bins, color="#b7b6a8", alpha=0.85, edgecolor="#f4f3ec",
        label=f"single selection (mean {single.mean():.2f})")
ax.hist(double, bins=bins, color="#e8531d", alpha=0.65, edgecolor="#f4f3ec",
        label=f"Double Lasso (mean {double.mean():.2f})")
ax.axvline(1, color="#161613", lw=1.6, ls="--")
ax.text(1.03, ax.get_ylim()[1] * 0.93, "true α = 1", color="#161613", fontsize=9)
ax.set_xlabel(r"estimate $\hat\alpha$ across 300 simulated datasets")
ax.set_ylabel("count")
ax.legend(frameon=False, fontsize=9, loc="upper left")
fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()

The simulation is also a warning about expectations. Double Lasso is not magic. It removes the first-order damage from imperfect selection, and at small n with many weak confounders some damage is left. What it fixes is the systematic failure of single selection, which does not go away however careful you are, because that estimator is aimed at the wrong target.

Worked example 1: do poor countries grow faster?

The Solow model predicts conditional convergence: holding institutions, schooling and the like fixed, poorer countries should grow faster. The coefficient \alpha on initial GDP per capita should be negative. Chernozhukov et al. estimate it on the Barro–Lee data (90 countries, about 60 controls) (Chernozhukov et al. 2024):

Method Estimate Std. error 95% CI
OLS, all controls −0.009 0.032 [−0.073, 0.054]
Double Lasso −0.045 0.018 [−0.080, −0.010]

OLS cannot tell convergence from divergence: its interval straddles zero comfortably. Double Lasso gives an annual convergence rate of about −4.5%, with a 95% interval from −8% to −1%, which is “consistent with the conditional convergence hypothesis” (Chernozhukov et al. 2024). The data did not change. What changed is that the estimator stopped spending its precision on 60 nuisance coefficients.

Worked example 2: many coefficients at once

Often you care about a family of coefficients: a main effect plus how it varies across groups. The book returns to CPS 2015 wage data and asks whether the gender wage gap differs by education, region and experience. That gives 12 target coefficients: sex and 11 interactions. The interactions are estimated one by one with Double Lasso, each against 990 controls (pairwise interactions of the modifiers plus occupation and industry dummies) (Chernozhukov et al. 2024).

Taken one at a time, some interactions look interesting. The gap at average characteristics is −0.07 (SE 0.01). The Midwest interaction is −0.12 (SE 0.04, pointwise p = 0.01), and the some-high-school interaction is −0.32 (SE 0.19, p = 0.08). But there are twelve of them. With 12 independent 95% intervals, the chance that at least one misses is 1 - 0.95^{12} \approx 0.46 (Chernozhukov et al. 2024). The book therefore builds a simultaneous 95% confidence band that covers all twelve together. Once multiplicity is accounted for, every interaction interval includes zero. The main gap survives, with a band of [−0.11, −0.03], but the apparent heterogeneity does not (Chernozhukov et al. 2024).

This is the same lesson as multiple testing, met in a high-dimensional setting. Double Lasso makes each estimate honest, and the simultaneous band makes the collection honest.

Relatives with the same property

The book lists two other procedures that are approximately equivalent to Double Lasso and share its orthogonality (Chernozhukov et al. 2024):

  • Double selection. Take the controls the Lasso picks for Y, and the controls it picks for D. Regress Y on D and the union of both sets, then use standard inference. This is “more conservative compared to single selection”, because it makes sure strong confounders of D were not dropped. It is the version most applied papers report.
  • Debiased (desparsified) Lasso. Fit a Lasso of Y on (D, W), then correct its coefficient on D using the residualized \tilde D from a Lasso of D on W. Technically the residualized D “instruments” for D, and the book notes that estimators of this form are called instrumental-variable estimators in econometrics.

All of these generalize, in Chapter 9 of the same book, to double/debiased machine learning: swap the Lasso for forests, boosting or neural nets, and add cross-fitting to stop the flexible learners overfitting their own residuals (Chernozhukov et al. 2024). See causal ML.

Common pitfalls

WarningWhere this goes wrong
  • Single selection. Reporting post-Lasso OLS standard errors after selecting controls from the outcome equation alone. It predicts well and infers badly. (Chernozhukov et al. 2024)
  • Cross-validated penalties in modest samples. Cross-validation is the default for prediction, but the authors have found that in the partialling-out steps it “frequently overfits in moderately sized samples”, giving poor practical performance. Use the theory-driven plug-in penalty, or cross-fitting as in DML. (Chernozhukov et al. 2024)
  • Forgetting that “predictive effect” is not “causal effect”. Double Lasso delivers honest inference on a regression coefficient. Whether that coefficient is causal depends on the identification argument (conditional ignorability, a DAG, a design), which the book treats separately. (Chernozhukov et al. 2024)
  • Treating sparsity as free. The method assumes a few controls (or a fast-decaying set) do most of the work. If confounding is spread thinly over hundreds of variables, no selection rule will capture it, and the simulation above shows the leftover bias.
  • Reading twelve marginal intervals as twelve discoveries. With many targets, report a simultaneous band, or control the false discovery rate. (Chernozhukov et al. 2024)

Appendix · the mathematics

Model and target. Y = \alpha D + \beta'W + \varepsilon. Let \gamma_{YW} and \gamma_{DW} be the best-linear-predictor coefficients of Y and D on W, and \tilde Y = Y - \gamma_{YW}'W, \tilde D = D - \gamma_{DW}'W. Then \alpha solves the partialled-out moment M(a, \eta) = \mathbb E\big[(\tilde Y(\eta) - a\tilde D(\eta))\,\tilde D(\eta)\big] = 0, \qquad \alpha = \big(\mathbb E[\tilde D^2]\big)^{-1}\mathbb E[\tilde D\tilde Y], with nuisance \eta = (\eta_1, \eta_2) entering as \tilde Y(\eta) = Y - \eta_1'W and \tilde D(\eta) = D - \eta_2'W, true value \eta_o = (\gamma_{YW}, \gamma_{DW}).

Orthogonality. By the implicit function theorem, \partial_\eta\alpha(\eta_o) = -\partial_a M(\alpha,\eta_o)^{-1}\,\partial_\eta M(\alpha,\eta_o). Now \partial_{\eta_1} M = \mathbb E[W\tilde D] = 0,\qquad \partial_{\eta_2} M = -\mathbb E[W\tilde Y] + 2\alpha\,\mathbb E[W\tilde D] = 0, both zero because least-squares residuals are orthogonal to the regressors. So \partial_\eta \alpha(\eta_o) = 0.

Why single selection is not orthogonal. Its moment is M(a, b) = \mathbb E[(Y - aD - b'W)D], with \partial_b M(\alpha,\beta) = -\mathbb E[DW] \neq 0 unless D \perp W. The slow \sqrt{s\log(p\vee n)/n} error in \hat\beta therefore passes straight into \hat\alpha.

Distribution. Under approximate sparsity |\gamma|_{(j)} \le A j^{-a} with a > 1 and plug-in tuning, \sqrt n(\hat\alpha - \alpha) \approx \frac{\sqrt n\,\mathbb E_n[\tilde D\varepsilon]}{\mathbb E_n[\tilde D^2]} \;\overset{a}{\sim}\; N(0, V),\qquad V = \big(\mathbb E[\tilde D^2]\big)^{-1}\mathbb E[\tilde D^2\varepsilon^2]\big(\mathbb E[\tilde D^2]\big)^{-1}.

Plug-in penalty (as in the figure). In the scaling \tfrac1{2n}\lVert y - Xb\rVert^2 + \lambda\lVert b\rVert_1 on standardized regressors, \lambda = 1.1\,\hat\sigma\,\Phi^{-1}\!\big(1 - \tfrac{0.1/\log n}{2p}\big)/\sqrt n, with \hat\sigma re-estimated from post-Lasso residuals a few times.

Simultaneous bands. For targets \alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_{p_1} with estimated covariance \hat V, the band \hat\alpha_\ell \pm c\,\sqrt{\hat V_{\ell\ell}/n} uses c = the (1-a)-quantile of \lVert N(0, D^{-1/2}\hat V D^{-1/2})\rVert_\infty, D = \mathrm{diag}(\hat V), found by simulation. It is never narrower than the pointwise band.

Debiased Lasso. \hat\alpha = \big(\mathbb E_n[D\tilde D(\hat\gamma)]\big)^{-1}\mathbb E_n\big[(Y - \hat\beta'W)\tilde D(\hat\gamma)\big], an IV estimator with the residualized \tilde D(\hat\gamma) as instrument.

Connections

  • Builds on: regularization (the Lasso itself, now used for a new purpose), multiple regression (FWL partialling-out and omitted-variable bias), and OLS in matrix form.
  • Leads to: causal ML / double machine learning (the same orthogonal score with any learner, plus cross-fitting), and multiple testing (simultaneous bands are family-wise control).
  • Contrasts with: cross-validation, which tunes for prediction error, the wrong criterion when the goal is a confidence interval for one coefficient.

title: "Double Lasso: Honest Inference After Choosing Controls"
subtitle: "Picking controls by how well they predict the outcome quietly drops the confounders that matter. Select twice: once for the outcome, once for the treatment."
---

::: {.lead-kernel}
When you have more candidate controls than you can sensibly use, the Lasso can choose them. But if you
choose only the ones that predict the **outcome**, you throw away controls that are weak predictors of
the outcome and strong predictors of the **regressor you care about**, and those are the confounders.
**Double Lasso** runs two prediction problems, one for $Y$ and one for $D$, partials both out, and then
regresses residual on residual. That estimator is built so that small mistakes in the two prediction
steps do not move the answer.
:::

## Why it matters

[Regularization](regularization.qmd) was built for *prediction*: it trades a little bias for a lot
less variance, and it is judged on how well it forecasts new data. Much of social science needs
something else, namely a **single coefficient with an honest confidence interval**. What happens to
growth when initial income is higher, holding institutions and schooling fixed? How big is the gender
wage gap once hundreds of job characteristics are held fixed?

Chernozhukov and coauthors put the problem in one line: with high-dimensional controls, "good
prediction is not enough", and you need procedures designed for *inference* [@chernozhukov_causalml].
Their running example is the growth-convergence question. The Barro–Lee sample has **90 countries and
about 60 controls**, so $p/n$ is far from small. Ordinary least squares with every control is legal but
nearly useless there. Its estimate is noisy, and the book notes that conventional robust standard errors
are not even consistent when $p/n$ is not small [@chernozhukov_causalml]. You need to throw most
controls away, but the way you throw them away decides whether the resulting interval can be trusted.

This entry is the bridge between the Lasso as a forecasting tool and
[double/debiased machine learning](../05-causal-inference/causal-machine-learning.qmd), which is the
same idea with any ML learner in the prediction steps.

## Start from something you already know: partialling out

The [Frisch–Waugh–Lovell theorem](../03-regression/multiple-regression.qmd) says that in
$Y = \alpha D + \beta' W + \varepsilon$, the coefficient $\alpha$ can be found in two steps:

1. Remove from $Y$ everything that $W$ can linearly explain, leaving $\tilde Y$. Do the same for $D$,
   leaving $\tilde D$.
2. Regress $\tilde Y$ on $\tilde D$. The slope is $\alpha$.

In words: $\alpha$ is how the *unexplained part* of the outcome moves with the *unexplained part* of
the regressor [@chernozhukov_causalml]. Step 1 is two **prediction** problems, and prediction is
exactly what the Lasso is good at when $W$ is large. So the **Double Lasso** recipe is
[@chernozhukov_causalml]:

1. Lasso $Y$ on $W$; keep the residuals $\check Y$.
2. Lasso $D$ on $W$; keep the residuals $\check D$.
3. Regress $\check Y$ on $\check D$ by least squares, and use an ordinary heteroskedasticity-robust
   standard error, *as if the residuals had been observed*.

That last step looks too good to be true. You estimated two whole prediction rules and then ignore that
uncertainty? The book's theorem says yes: under approximate sparsity and sensible tuning, the estimation
error in $\check Y$ and $\check D$ has **no first-order effect** on $\hat\alpha$, and the estimator
behaves like least squares with the true residuals [@chernozhukov_causalml]. The reason has a name.

## Why it works: Neyman orthogonality

Think of $\hat\alpha$ as a function of the two prediction rules it was built from. Those rules are
regularized, so they are *slightly wrong*: the Lasso shrinks, and it misses small coefficients. The
question is how much a slightly wrong prediction rule moves $\alpha$.

For the partialled-out equation the answer is: **not at all, to first order**. Nudge either prediction
rule a little away from the truth and the derivative of $\alpha$ with respect to that nudge is zero.
The book calls this local insensitivity **Neyman orthogonality** and describes it as the property that
ensures the regularization bias in the nuisance parts "does not transmit to the estimation of the
target parameter, at least to the first order" [@chernozhukov_causalml]. An error of size $\delta$ in
the prediction step shows up in $\hat\alpha$ only as something like $\delta^2$, which shrinks fast
enough to ignore.

Two remarks from the book are worth keeping [@chernozhukov_causalml]:

- In a pure randomized experiment, $D$ is unrelated to $W$, so orthogonality holds automatically. The
  problem this entry solves is an *observational-data* problem.
- The requirement on sparsity is stronger than for prediction. The sorted coefficients must fall off
  faster than $1/j$, because the prediction errors must be small enough that their products vanish
  at the $\sqrt n$ rate. Good prediction alone is not the target.

## Why the obvious shortcut fails: single selection

The tempting alternative is **single selection**: run one Lasso of $Y$ on $(D, W)$, keep whichever
controls survive, refit by least squares, and report the usual interval. Chernozhukov and coauthors
say this is "a fine approach if the goal is solely the prediction of the outcome", but it "can
result in very misleading conclusions" about $\alpha$ [@chernozhukov_causalml].

The mechanism is omitted-variable bias with a machine-learning accent. The single Lasso keeps controls
that help predict $Y$ *given $D$ is already in the model*. A control that affects $Y$ weakly but drives
$D$ strongly looks useless for predicting $Y$, since $D$ already carries most of its information, and
it is dropped. In the book's words, "weak predictors of the outcome could still be strong predictors
of $D$", and dropping them "results in a strong omitted variable bias" [@chernozhukov_causalml]. Double
Lasso instead finds controls that matter for *either* equation, so the residuals are approximately
"de-confounded".

The book demonstrates this with a simulation (its Example 4.3.1): $p = n = 100$, the controls'
effects on both $Y$ and $D$ fall off as $1/j^2$, and the true $\alpha = 1$. The naive estimator is
"heavily biased", while Double Lasso is "approximately unbiased and Gaussian"
[@chernozhukov_causalml]. The figure below rebuilds that experiment by hand.

```{python}
#| label: fig-double-lasso
#| fig-cap: "A by-hand rebuild of Chernozhukov et al.'s Example 4.3.1 (p = n = 100; each control's effect on Y and on D is 1/j²; true α = 1), 300 simulated datasets, Lasso by coordinate descent with a plug-in penalty. Single selection (grey) keeps only controls that predict Y and centres near 1.57, about two of its own standard deviations off the truth. Double Lasso (orange) selects controls for Y and for D separately and centres near 1.15. It is much closer, but not exactly on 1: at n = 100 the weakest confounders (effects of 1/9, 1/16, …) sometimes fall below the penalty in both equations, and what they leave behind is a small residual bias."
#| code-fold: true
#| code-summary: "Show the figure code"

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from math import erf, sqrt, log

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n = p = 100
coef = 1 / np.arange(1, p + 1) ** 2          # effect of control j on Y and on D

def norm_ppf(q):                              # normal quantile by bisection
    lo, hi = 0.0, 10.0
    for _ in range(80):
        m = (lo + hi) / 2
        lo, hi = (m, hi) if 0.5 * (1 + erf(m / sqrt(2))) < q else (lo, m)
    return lo

def lasso_selected(X, y, lam):                # coordinate descent, standardised columns
    Xs = (X - X.mean(0)) / X.std(0)
    b, r = np.zeros(X.shape[1]), y - y.mean()
    for _ in range(300):
        biggest = 0
        for j in range(X.shape[1]):
            old = b[j]
            rho = Xs[:, j] @ r / len(y) + old
            b[j] = np.sign(rho) * max(abs(rho) - lam, 0)
            if b[j] != old:
                r -= Xs[:, j] * (b[j] - old)
                biggest = max(biggest, abs(b[j] - old))
        if biggest < 1e-7:
            break
    return b != 0

def resid(v, X):                              # least-squares residuals on selected controls
    Z = np.column_stack([np.ones(len(v)), X])
    return v - Z @ np.linalg.lstsq(Z, v, rcond=None)[0]

def plugin_lasso(X, y):                       # plug-in penalty, noise level re-estimated
    N, k = X.shape
    c = 1.1 * norm_ppf(1 - 0.1 / log(N) / (2 * k)) / np.sqrt(N)
    s = y.std()
    for _ in range(5):
        sel = lasso_selected(X, y, c * s)
        s = resid(y, X[:, sel]).std()
    return sel

single, double = [], []
for rep in range(300):
    W = rng.normal(size=(n, p))
    D = W @ coef + rng.normal(size=n) / 4
    Y = 1.0 * D + W @ coef + rng.normal(size=n)
    keep = plugin_lasso(np.column_stack([D, W]), Y)[1:]      # single selection
    rd = resid(D, W[:, keep])
    single.append(rd @ resid(Y, W[:, keep]) / (rd @ rd))
    ry = resid(Y, W[:, plugin_lasso(W, Y)])                   # Double Lasso
    rd = resid(D, W[:, plugin_lasso(W, D)])
    double.append(rd @ ry / (rd @ rd))

single, double = np.array(single), np.array(double)
bins = np.linspace(-0.4, 2.8, 41)
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(8.8, 4.4))
ax.hist(single, bins=bins, color="#b7b6a8", alpha=0.85, edgecolor="#f4f3ec",
        label=f"single selection (mean {single.mean():.2f})")
ax.hist(double, bins=bins, color="#e8531d", alpha=0.65, edgecolor="#f4f3ec",
        label=f"Double Lasso (mean {double.mean():.2f})")
ax.axvline(1, color="#161613", lw=1.6, ls="--")
ax.text(1.03, ax.get_ylim()[1] * 0.93, "true α = 1", color="#161613", fontsize=9)
ax.set_xlabel(r"estimate $\hat\alpha$ across 300 simulated datasets")
ax.set_ylabel("count")
ax.legend(frameon=False, fontsize=9, loc="upper left")
fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

The simulation is also a warning about expectations. Double Lasso is not magic. It removes the
*first-order* damage from imperfect selection, and at small $n$ with many weak confounders some damage
is left. What it fixes is the systematic failure of single selection, which does not go away however
careful you are, because that estimator is aimed at the wrong target.

## Worked example 1: do poor countries grow faster?

The Solow model predicts **conditional convergence**: holding institutions, schooling and the like
fixed, poorer countries should grow faster. The coefficient $\alpha$ on initial GDP per capita should be
negative. Chernozhukov et al. estimate it on the Barro–Lee data (90 countries, about 60 controls)
[@chernozhukov_causalml]:

| Method | Estimate | Std. error | 95% CI |
|---|---|---|---|
| OLS, all controls | −0.009 | 0.032 | [−0.073, 0.054] |
| Double Lasso | −0.045 | 0.018 | [−0.080, −0.010] |

OLS cannot tell convergence from divergence: its interval straddles zero comfortably. Double Lasso gives
an annual convergence rate of about −4.5%, with a 95% interval from −8% to −1%, which is "consistent
with the conditional convergence hypothesis" [@chernozhukov_causalml]. The data did not change. What
changed is that the estimator stopped spending its precision on 60 nuisance coefficients.

## Worked example 2: many coefficients at once

Often you care about a *family* of coefficients: a main effect plus how it varies across groups. The
book returns to CPS 2015 wage data and asks whether the **gender wage gap** differs by education, region
and experience. That gives 12 target coefficients: `sex` and 11 interactions. The interactions are
estimated one by one with Double Lasso, each against **990 controls** (pairwise interactions of the
modifiers plus occupation and industry dummies) [@chernozhukov_causalml].

Taken one at a time, some interactions look interesting. The gap at average characteristics is −0.07
(SE 0.01). The Midwest interaction is −0.12 (SE 0.04, pointwise $p = 0.01$), and the some-high-school
interaction is −0.32 (SE 0.19, $p = 0.08$). But there are twelve of them. With 12 independent 95%
intervals, the chance that at least one misses is $1 - 0.95^{12} \approx 0.46$
[@chernozhukov_causalml]. The book therefore builds a **simultaneous 95% confidence band** that covers
all twelve together. Once multiplicity is accounted for, **every interaction interval includes zero**.
The main gap survives, with a band of [−0.11, −0.03], but the apparent heterogeneity does not
[@chernozhukov_causalml].

This is the same lesson as [multiple testing](../02-inference/multiple-testing.qmd), met in a
high-dimensional setting. Double Lasso makes each estimate honest, and the simultaneous band makes the
*collection* honest.

## Relatives with the same property

The book lists two other procedures that are approximately equivalent to Double Lasso and share
its orthogonality [@chernozhukov_causalml]:

- **Double selection.** Take the controls the Lasso picks for $Y$, and the controls it picks for
  $D$. Regress $Y$ on $D$ and the **union** of both sets, then use standard inference. This is "more
  conservative compared to single selection", because it makes sure strong confounders of $D$ were not
  dropped. It is the version most applied papers report.
- **Debiased (desparsified) Lasso.** Fit a Lasso of $Y$ on $(D, W)$, then correct its coefficient on
  $D$ using the residualized $\tilde D$ from a Lasso of $D$ on $W$. Technically the residualized $D$
  "instruments" for $D$, and the book notes that estimators of this form are called
  [instrumental-variable](../05-causal-inference/instrumental-variables.qmd) estimators in econometrics.

All of these generalize, in Chapter 9 of the same book, to **double/debiased machine learning**: swap
the Lasso for forests, boosting or neural nets, and add **cross-fitting** to stop the flexible learners
overfitting their own residuals [@chernozhukov_causalml]. See
[causal ML](../05-causal-inference/causal-machine-learning.qmd).

## Common pitfalls

::: {.callout-warning title="Where this goes wrong"}
- **Single selection.** Reporting post-Lasso OLS standard errors after selecting controls from the
  outcome equation alone. It predicts well and infers badly. [@chernozhukov_causalml]
- **Cross-validated penalties in modest samples.** Cross-validation is the default for prediction, but
  the authors have found that in the partialling-out steps it "frequently overfits in moderately sized
  samples", giving poor practical performance. Use the theory-driven plug-in penalty, or cross-fitting
  as in DML. [@chernozhukov_causalml]
- **Forgetting that "predictive effect" is not "causal effect".** Double Lasso delivers honest inference
  on a *regression coefficient*. Whether that coefficient is causal depends on the identification
  argument (conditional ignorability, a [DAG](../05-causal-inference/directed-acyclic-graphs.qmd), a
  design), which the book treats separately. [@chernozhukov_causalml]
- **Treating sparsity as free.** The method assumes a few controls (or a fast-decaying set) do most
  of the work. If confounding is spread thinly over hundreds of variables, no selection rule will
  capture it, and the simulation above shows the leftover bias.
- **Reading twelve marginal intervals as twelve discoveries.** With many targets, report a simultaneous
  band, or control the false discovery rate. [@chernozhukov_causalml]
:::

## Appendix · the mathematics {.unnumbered}

::: {.math-appendix}

**Model and target.** $Y = \alpha D + \beta'W + \varepsilon$. Let $\gamma_{YW}$ and $\gamma_{DW}$ be the
best-linear-predictor coefficients of $Y$ and $D$ on $W$, and $\tilde Y = Y - \gamma_{YW}'W$,
$\tilde D = D - \gamma_{DW}'W$. Then $\alpha$ solves the partialled-out moment
$$
M(a, \eta) = \mathbb E\big[(\tilde Y(\eta) - a\tilde D(\eta))\,\tilde D(\eta)\big] = 0,
\qquad \alpha = \big(\mathbb E[\tilde D^2]\big)^{-1}\mathbb E[\tilde D\tilde Y],
$$
with nuisance $\eta = (\eta_1, \eta_2)$ entering as $\tilde Y(\eta) = Y - \eta_1'W$ and
$\tilde D(\eta) = D - \eta_2'W$, true value $\eta_o = (\gamma_{YW}, \gamma_{DW})$.

**Orthogonality.** By the implicit function theorem,
$\partial_\eta\alpha(\eta_o) = -\partial_a M(\alpha,\eta_o)^{-1}\,\partial_\eta M(\alpha,\eta_o)$. Now
$$
\partial_{\eta_1} M = \mathbb E[W\tilde D] = 0,\qquad
\partial_{\eta_2} M = -\mathbb E[W\tilde Y] + 2\alpha\,\mathbb E[W\tilde D] = 0,
$$
both zero because least-squares residuals are orthogonal to the regressors. So
$\partial_\eta \alpha(\eta_o) = 0$.

**Why single selection is not orthogonal.** Its moment is
$M(a, b) = \mathbb E[(Y - aD - b'W)D]$, with $\partial_b M(\alpha,\beta) = -\mathbb E[DW] \neq 0$ unless
$D \perp W$. The slow $\sqrt{s\log(p\vee n)/n}$ error in $\hat\beta$ therefore passes straight into
$\hat\alpha$.

**Distribution.** Under approximate sparsity $|\gamma|_{(j)} \le A j^{-a}$ with $a > 1$ and plug-in
tuning,
$$
\sqrt n(\hat\alpha - \alpha) \approx \frac{\sqrt n\,\mathbb E_n[\tilde D\varepsilon]}{\mathbb E_n[\tilde D^2]}
\;\overset{a}{\sim}\; N(0, V),\qquad
V = \big(\mathbb E[\tilde D^2]\big)^{-1}\mathbb E[\tilde D^2\varepsilon^2]\big(\mathbb E[\tilde D^2]\big)^{-1}.
$$

**Plug-in penalty (as in the figure).** In the scaling $\tfrac1{2n}\lVert y - Xb\rVert^2 + \lambda\lVert b\rVert_1$
on standardized regressors, $\lambda = 1.1\,\hat\sigma\,\Phi^{-1}\!\big(1 - \tfrac{0.1/\log n}{2p}\big)/\sqrt n$,
with $\hat\sigma$ re-estimated from post-Lasso residuals a few times.

**Simultaneous bands.** For targets $\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_{p_1}$ with estimated covariance $\hat V$, the
band $\hat\alpha_\ell \pm c\,\sqrt{\hat V_{\ell\ell}/n}$ uses $c$ = the $(1-a)$-quantile of
$\lVert N(0, D^{-1/2}\hat V D^{-1/2})\rVert_\infty$, $D = \mathrm{diag}(\hat V)$, found by simulation. It is
never narrower than the pointwise band.

**Debiased Lasso.** $\hat\alpha = \big(\mathbb E_n[D\tilde D(\hat\gamma)]\big)^{-1}\mathbb E_n\big[(Y - \hat\beta'W)\tilde D(\hat\gamma)\big]$,
an IV estimator with the residualized $\tilde D(\hat\gamma)$ as instrument.

:::
