Text as Data: Tokens, Sentiment & Topic Models

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source

Before a computer can read a sentence it has to count it. Every text method is a decision about what to count and what to throw away.

Text mining turns a pile of documents into a matrix of counts: one row per document, one column per word. Everything after that is ordinary quantitative work. Sentiment analysis gives each word a score from a dictionary. Topic models treat each document as a mixture of hidden themes. Neither one understands the text. They find patterns in what was counted, so how you prepare and count the text matters at least as much as the model.

Why it matters

Social scientists have a lot of text. There are open-ended survey answers, diaries, interview transcripts, and social media posts by the million. Jacobucci, Grimm and Zhang mention a study of 1.6 million tweets about suicide risk and work that predicts personality from Facebook posts. They suggest text may be even more common than numerical data, yet researchers know far less about how to analyse it (Jacobucci et al. 2023). Usually the open-ended item gets read by hand, quoted selectively, or dropped.

This entry covers the counting approach: preprocessing, tokens, the document-term matrix, sentiment dictionaries and LDA topic models. It follows one worked example from start to finish. It does not cover modern language models. The book leaves them out too, for a reason that matters to most social scientists (see what this leaves out).

From words to a matrix

The vocabulary

The book uses a few terms throughout (Jacobucci et al. 2023):

  • A document is one observation’s text. It can be a single sentence or a whole book.
  • A corpus is the collection of documents.
  • Tokenization splits documents into units. The units can be characters, words or sentences, depending on the purpose. Words are the most common choice.
  • Stop words are words like a, and, the that carry little meaning and are usually removed.
  • Stemming reduces word forms to a common root, so loves, loving and loved all become love.

The stem need not be a real word. Easy becomes easi and lecture becomes lectur. The book’s verdict is that “this does not matter”. What matters is that every form of the word gets counted in the same column.

Preprocessing

Getting from raw text to tokens takes several steps (Jacobucci et al. 2023):

  1. Extract the text from its container (HTML, JSON).
  2. Remove names, emails and other identifiers.
  3. Deal with special characters. Emoji can be removed, but if you want sentiment, they may be better recoded as meaningful words.
  4. Expand abbreviations (“HW” means homework).
  5. Correct spelling with a spell-checker.
  6. Remove stop words and stem.

The authors’ summary is that preprocessing “is much more critical than in quantitative data cleaning”. In practice you repeat it alongside tokenization until the counts look sensible.

n-grams

Single words lose context. “Not easy at all” contains easy, and counted word by word it reads as praise. n-grams count runs of words instead, which keeps some of the context: bigrams are two-word runs (“not easy”) and trigrams are three-word runs (“not very helpful”).

The document-term matrix

The output of all this is the document-term matrix (DTM). Rows are documents, columns are tokens, and each cell holds a statistic, usually a count. The matrix is almost always sparse. Any one comment uses a few dozen words out of a vocabulary of tens of thousands, so nearly every cell is zero.

In the worked example below, the DTM has 27,939 rows and 18,216 columns, and fewer than 0.2% of the cells are non-zero. Stored as a full matrix it takes about 3.8 GB. Stored as a sparse matrix, which records only the non-zero cells and where they are, it takes about 18 MB (Jacobucci et al. 2023). The sparse format is not a minor optimisation. Without it, many corpora are too big to analyse at all.

The worked example: 27,939 professor ratings

The chapter uses one dataset throughout: 27,939 student evaluations of 999 professors scraped from a ratings website (Jacobucci et al. 2023). Each evaluation has three parts:

  • a rating from 1 (“Profs get F’s too”) to 5 (“A real gem”), with a mean of 3.85;
  • a difficulty score from 1 to 5, with a mean of 2.88;
  • a free-text comment, with names and universities removed.

The comments contain 1,223,454 words, of which 18,216 are distinct. The average comment is 43.8 words long.

Counting

The most frequent words are useless: the appears 41,864 times, followed by and, to, a. The authors built a stop list by combining three standard lists, SMART, snowball and onix, which together hold 1,149 words. Then they checked the combined list by hand. Some “stop words” carry meaning in teaching evaluations. Least matters in “the least organised professor”, and best matters too, so both were kept. They also added domain words such as professor, teacher and Dr. The final list had 619 words (Jacobucci et al. 2023). The general rule is that no stop list is universal. Any word can be a stop word for a particular purpose.

With stop words removed, the top words are easy, great, good, tests, hard, work, best, help. Once stemmed, help, test and easi lead. The most common bigrams are great teacher, very helpful and extra credit, each with over 1,000 occurrences. Bigrams also pick up not hard, not helpful, and the gradations very helpful and extremely helpful, which single words would have merged (Jacobucci et al. 2023).

A first measurement: who is “he” and who is “she”?

The website does not record the professor’s gender. The authors inferred it by counting gendered words in each professor’s comments. The male list was he, his, him, he’s, mr, and so on, and the female list was she, her, she’s, miss, mrs, and so on. Each professor was assigned to whichever list was more frequent across all their comments. Counts per professor were usually lopsided, such as 153 to 0 or 0 to 118. Still, 27.8% of professors had at least some words from both lists. Pooling many comments made the label reliable, even though any single comment might use the wrong pronoun. The result was 415 female professors (42%) and 584 male (58%) (Jacobucci et al. 2023).

The authors then compared the two groups:

  • Rating: male professors averaged 3.92 and female professors 3.76. The difference is 0.16, Cohen’s d = 0.11, statistically significant but small.
  • Difficulty: 2.877 against 2.886, which is no difference (Jacobucci et al. 2023).

This is a small model of the whole field. A construct (gender) was measured by counting words, and the measurement can be wrong. The resulting variable then went into an ordinary comparison of means.

Sentiment: scoring words with a dictionary

Sentiment analysis, also called opinion mining, scores text by its emotional valence. The common approach uses a dictionary, also called a lexicon: a list of words that people have labelled as positive or negative. A text’s score is the sum or average of the scores of its words. The book compares four lexicons (Jacobucci et al. 2023):

Sentiment lexicons in Jacobucci et al. Ch 11.
Lexicon Size Scoring Note
AFINN 2,477 words and phrases integers from −5 to +5 masterpiece scores +4, robber −2
nrc 6,468 words positive or negative, plus 8 emotions (anger, anticipation, disgust, fear, joy, sadness, surprise, trust) for 4,463 of them labelled by crowd-workers
bing 6,874 words and phrases mostly +1 or −1
syuzhet 10,738 words 16 values between −1 and +1 built from novels, so it “may work best for such text”

The lexicons disagree. One glowing comment in the book scores 6 with bing and 5.35 with syuzhet. Neither number means anything by itself. As the authors put it, the size of a sentiment score “cannot be determined simply by the score without defining a norm first”. The advice is to pick a lexicon that suits the purpose and read through its word list, adding and removing words for your domain (Jacobucci et al. 2023).

Does it measure anything? Check it against the rating

The useful step is validation. Across the corpus, bing scores average 1.68 (SD 2.37), so on the whole the comments are positive. The most common positive words are easy, great, good, interesting. The most common negative words are hard, difficult, boring, horrible. Positive comments reuse the same few words, while negative ones use a wider vocabulary. The sentiment scores correlate with the students’ own numerical ratings (Jacobucci et al. 2023):

Sentiment scores against the numerical ratings (Jacobucci et al. Table 11.10).
Rating Difficulty bing
bing sentiment 0.576 −0.323
syuzhet sentiment 0.561 −0.303 0.852

The authors read this as evidence of criterion validity, “if one trusts the numerical ratings”. This is the same move as validating any new scale against an established one, as in concepts and measurement. A sentiment score is a measurement instrument, and it needs a validity check like any other.

Valence shifters

Dictionaries score words one at a time, so they miss words that change the meaning of their neighbours. These are called valence shifters:

  • negators (isn’t) flip the sign;
  • amplifiers (very, extremely) strengthen it;
  • de-amplifiers (barely) weaken it;
  • adversative conjunctions (but) shift the weight to the clause that follows.

The book’s example has all of them at work. A chemistry course is “not an easy course” and “frustrating”, but with this professor it is “actually interesting, and fun”. Scoring the words one at a time gets this badly wrong. Shifters are common: negators appeared alongside sentiment words about 20% of the time.

Applying shifter weights changed the scores only moderately: the two versions correlate at about 0.86. But the correlation with the rating rose from 0.576 to 0.655 (Jacobucci et al. 2023). The adjusted score is a better measure of the same construct.

Topic models: documents as mixtures of themes

Sentiment asks how positive a comment is. A topic model asks what it is about. The book explains LDA in terms of how a student writes an evaluation. First they decide which aspects to comment on, such as how the lectures were organised or how hard the tests were. Then they choose words that fit each aspect (Jacobucci et al. 2023).

Latent Dirichlet allocation (LDA) runs that process backwards. It assumes:

  • each topic is a probability distribution over the whole vocabulary. A “teaching effectiveness” topic puts weight on prepared, clarity, organised. A “personality” topic puts weight on nice, open, warm.
  • each document is a mixture of a small number of topics, with its own proportions \theta. A 100-word comment with 70 effectiveness words and 30 personality words has \theta = (0.7, 0.3).
  • each word is produced by first picking a topic using the document’s \theta, then picking a word using that topic’s distribution.

Only the words are observed. Estimation recovers the topics and every document’s \theta from the counts alone, which the book calls “a reverse-engineering process”. The original paper, Blei, Ng and Jordan (2003), used a variational EM algorithm (Jacobucci et al. 2023).

The figure fits LDA to a corpus whose true topics we know, because we generated it.

Show the figure code 
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(11)
vocab = ["class", "lectur", "clear", "organ", "prepar", "exam",
         "nice", "funni", "warm", "open", "care", "help"]
V, K, M, N = len(vocab), 2, 300, 40
beta_true = np.array([
    [.16, .16, .14, .14, .12, .14, .01, .01, .01, .01, .02, .08],   # teaching effectiveness
    [.16, .01, .02, .01, .01, .01, .16, .14, .14, .12, .12, .10],   # personality
])
beta_true /= beta_true.sum(1, keepdims=True)
theta_true = rng.dirichlet([0.6, 0.6], M)                 # each document's topic mix
z = (rng.random((M, N)) > theta_true[:, [0]]).astype(int)  # topic of each word slot
w = np.array([[rng.choice(V, p=beta_true[k]) for k in row] for row in z])

# Fit LDA by Gibbs sampling: alternate topic labels | (theta, beta) and (theta, beta) | labels
alpha, delta, iters, burn = 0.5, 0.1, 400, 200
theta = rng.dirichlet([alpha] * K, M)
beta = rng.dirichlet([delta * 10] * V, K)
th_sum, be_sum = np.zeros_like(theta), np.zeros_like(beta)
docs, wf = np.repeat(np.arange(M), N), w.ravel()
for it in range(iters):
    p = theta[docs] * beta[:, wf].T
    p /= p.sum(1, keepdims=True)
    zk = (rng.random(len(wf)) > p[:, 0]).astype(int)
    ndk = np.zeros((M, K)); np.add.at(ndk, (docs, zk), 1)
    nkw = np.zeros((K, V)); np.add.at(nkw, (zk, wf), 1)
    theta = np.array([rng.dirichlet(alpha + r) for r in ndk])
    beta = np.array([rng.dirichlet(delta + r) for r in nkw])
    if it >= burn:
        th_sum += theta; be_sum += beta
theta_hat, beta_hat = th_sum / (iters - burn), be_sum / (iters - burn)
if beta_hat[0, 1] < beta_hat[1, 1]:                        # topics are unlabelled: align them
    theta_hat, beta_hat = theta_hat[:, ::-1], beta_hat[::-1]
r = np.corrcoef(theta_true[:, 0], theta_hat[:, 0])[0, 1]
j = np.argmin(np.abs(theta_true[:, 0] - 0.7))

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(10, 4.2), gridspec_kw={"width_ratios": [1.6, 1]})
ax = axes[0]
x = np.arange(V)
for k, (off, name) in enumerate([(-0.2, "teaching"), (0.2, "personality")]):
    sign = 1 if k == 0 else -1                             # teaching up, personality down
    ax.bar(x + off, sign * beta_true[k], width=0.38, color="#b7b6a8",
           label="true" if k == 0 else None)
    ax.bar(x + off, sign * beta_hat[k], width=0.18, color="#161613",
           label="LDA estimate" if k == 0 else None)
ax.axhline(0, color="#161613", lw=0.8)
ax.set_xticks(x); ax.set_xticklabels(vocab, rotation=45, ha="right", fontsize=9)
ax.set_ylabel("P(word | topic)\n↑ teaching   ↓ personality")
yt = ax.get_yticks(); ax.set_yticks(yt); ax.set_yticklabels([f"{abs(t):.2f}" for t in yt])
ax.legend(frameon=False, fontsize=9, loc="upper center", bbox_to_anchor=(0.66, 0.98))
ax.set_title("Topics: what each theme talks about", fontsize=11)

ax = axes[1]
ax.plot([0, 1], [0, 1], color="#161613", lw=1, ls="--")
ax.scatter(theta_true[:, 0], theta_hat[:, 0], s=14, color="#5e5e54", alpha=0.6)
ax.scatter(theta_true[j, 0], theta_hat[j, 0], s=70, color="#e8531d", zorder=3)
ax.annotate(f"true {theta_true[j, 0]:.2f}\nestimated {theta_hat[j, 0]:.2f}",
            (theta_true[j, 0], theta_hat[j, 0]), xytext=(0.62, 0.12),
            fontsize=9, color="#e8531d", arrowprops=dict(arrowstyle="-", color="#e8531d"))
ax.text(0.03, 0.92, f"r = {r:.2f}", fontsize=10)
ax.set_xlabel("true share of 'teaching' words, θ")
ax.set_ylabel("LDA estimate of θ")
ax.set_xlim(0, 1); ax.set_ylim(0, 1)
ax.set_title("Documents: what mix each one is", fontsize=11)
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

The topics come back almost exactly, including the shared word class. The model learns that class belongs to both topics rather than labelling it as one. Each document’s mixture is recovered well on average. For any single 40-word document, though, the estimate is noisy, so per-document topic shares come with real uncertainty.

LDA on the professor data

For the real analysis, each professor’s comments were pooled into one document, giving 999 documents. After removing stop words, stemming, and dropping words seen fewer than five times, 499,836 words remained, 3,064 of them distinct (Jacobucci et al. 2023). The analysis then had three steps.

1. Choose the number of topics. The authors fitted models with 2 to 9 topics, using 5-fold cross-validation and perplexity, a measure of how well the model predicts held-out words. Lower is better. Perplexity kept falling as topics were added. The authors chose six topics because the improvement slowed down between six and seven (Jacobucci et al. 2023). This is an elbow judgement, the same kind as a scree plot. It is not a test, and the cross-validated criterion does not settle the choice by itself. See cross-validation.

2. Read the topics. A topic is just a ranked list of words with probabilities. To judge whether a word stands out, compare it with the uniform baseline. If every word were equally likely, each would have probability 1/3,064, about 0.03%. The stem lectur has 3.29% in topic 3, more than 100 times the baseline. Meanwhile class is the top word in five of the six topics (Jacobucci et al. 2023), so a frequent word can lead a topic’s list without telling you what the topic is about.

3. Name the topics. This step is human work. The authors named only two:

  • topic 3, about lecturing, from lectur, test, exam, materi;
  • topic 5, about how students perceived the teaching, since great, best, interest and funni appear only there.

They leave the other four as an exercise, and say that meaningful names require “a thorough understanding of the problem under investigation” (Jacobucci et al. 2023).

4. Use the output. Each professor now has a vector of topic proportions. For example, Professor 1’s comments are 55% topic 2. These proportions are new variables. They can be used to group professors by teaching style or entered into a regression (Jacobucci et al. 2023).

How LDA relates to older methods. The book compares LDA with latent semantic analysis (LSA), an older method. LSA factorises the DTM, like principal components. LDA is a generative mixture model, like latent class analysis. The difference “can be boiled down to PCA versus LCA” (Jacobucci et al. 2023). LDA has also been extended in several directions:

  • correlated topic models;
  • supervised topic models, in which an outcome helps shape the topics;
  • supervised models that also take covariates.

The mixture-models entry explains the EM machinery behind these models.

What counting leaves out

Everything above treats a document as a bag of words, a set of counts with the word order thrown away. n-grams and valence shifters only patch the problem locally. Neural language models handle word order properly. The book deliberately leaves them out, because many social-science studies “do not have the amount of text necessary” to train them (Jacobucci et al. 2023).

The authors also make a practical point about small samples and short responses. In that setting, a validated dictionary such as LIWC, with about 6,400 words and stems scored on 90 components, will probably predict better than more complex algorithms (Jacobucci et al. 2023). This fits the theme of the bias–variance entry. A fixed dictionary has no parameters to overfit, and with little data that matters more than flexibility.

Common pitfalls

WarningWhere this goes wrong
  • Using a stop list without reading it. Standard lists remove words that carry meaning in your domain, such as least and best in teaching evaluations, and not almost everywhere. Read the list before you use it.
  • Treating a sentiment score as meaningful on its own. “This comment scores 6” means nothing without a norm. Scores from different lexicons are not comparable. Validate against an outcome you trust, as the authors did with the ratings.
  • Scoring words without context. Negators, amplifiers and “but” clauses are common. Ignoring them weakens the measure: the correlation with the rating was 0.576 without shifters and 0.655 with them.
  • Using a lexicon built for other text. syuzhet was built from contemporary novels and “may work best for such text”. Check that a lexicon’s source resembles your documents.
  • Treating the number of topics as discovered. Perplexity usually keeps improving as topics are added. The final number is a judgement. Report the curve and show that your conclusions hold with neighbouring choices.
  • Overreading topic labels. A topic is a word distribution. Its name is the researcher’s interpretation, and frequent words such as class can top several topics. Name topics from the distinctive words, and say which topics you could not name.
  • Forgetting that text-derived variables are measured with error. Inferred gender, sentiment scores and topic shares are all estimates. When they enter a regression, the errors-in-variables problems in measurement & ML apply. A gap in ratings by inferred gender is a description, not a causal finding; see regression & causality.

Appendix · the mathematics

LDA’s generative model (in the book’s notation). There are K topics, M documents and V distinct words. For each document m and each word position n: \theta_m \sim \text{Dirichlet}(\alpha), \qquad \beta_k \sim \text{Dirichlet}(\delta), z_{mn} \sim \text{Multinomial}(\theta_m), \qquad w_{mn} \mid z_{mn}=k \sim \text{Multinomial}(\beta_k). Here \theta_m=(\theta_{m1},\dots,\theta_{mK}) sums to one; it is the document’s topic mix. \beta_k=(\beta_{k1},\dots,\beta_{kV}) is topic k’s distribution over words. The Dirichlet is a distribution over probability vectors, the multi-category version of the beta distribution. Small \alpha favours documents dominated by one or two topics. Small \delta favours topics concentrated on a few words. Summing out the topic labels, each document’s word probabilities are a mixture: P(w_{mn}=v \mid \theta_m, \beta) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \theta_{mk}\,\beta_{kv}. This is the low-rank structure that LDA shares with LSA’s matrix factorisation: the M \times V matrix of expected word shares equals \Theta B, where \Theta is M\times K and B is K \times V.

The sampler in the figure (our illustration). Given \theta and \beta, each word’s topic is drawn with probability P(z_{mn}=k \mid w_{mn}=v) = \frac{\theta_{mk}\,\beta_{kv}}{\sum_j \theta_{mj}\,\beta_{jv}} . Then, by Dirichlet–multinomial conjugacy, \theta_m \mid z \sim \text{Dirichlet}(\alpha + n_{m\cdot}) and \beta_k \mid z, w \sim \text{Dirichlet}(\delta + n_{k\cdot}). Here n_{m\cdot} counts document m’s words assigned to each topic, and n_{k\cdot} counts each word’s assignments to topic k. Alternating these steps is a Gibbs sampler; see MCMC in depth. The topics come out unlabelled, so “topic 1” in one run can be “topic 2” in the next. That is why the code aligns them at the end.

Perplexity. For held-out documents with N words in total and log-likelihood \log p(\mathbf{w}), \text{perplexity} = \exp\!\Big(-\tfrac{1}{N}\log p(\mathbf{w})\Big), the inverse geometric mean of the per-word likelihood. A model that guessed uniformly over V words would have perplexity V. The book’s cross-validated curve sits at roughly 2,000 to 2,150 (read from its Figure 11.23), below the uniform baseline of V = 3{,}064.

Why sparse storage matters. 27{,}939 \times 18{,}216 \approx 5.09\times10^{8} cells at 8 bytes each is about 4.07 GB (3.8 GiB), the book’s figure. At under 0.2% density, storing only the non-zero values with their row indices and column pointers takes tens of megabytes.

::: {.lead-kernel}
Text mining turns a pile of documents into a **matrix of counts**: one row per document, one column
per word. Everything after that is ordinary quantitative work. **Sentiment analysis** gives each
word a score from a dictionary. **Topic models** treat each document as a mixture of hidden
themes. Neither one understands the text. They find patterns in what was counted, so how you
prepare and count the text matters at least as much as the model.
:::

## Why it matters

Social scientists have a lot of text. There are open-ended survey answers, diaries, interview
transcripts, and social media posts by the million. Jacobucci, Grimm and Zhang mention a study of
1.6 million tweets about suicide risk and work that predicts personality from Facebook posts.
They suggest text may be **even more common than numerical data**, yet researchers know far less
about how to analyse it [@jacobucci_ml]. Usually the open-ended item gets read by hand, quoted
selectively, or dropped.

This entry covers the counting approach: preprocessing, tokens, the document-term matrix, sentiment
dictionaries and LDA topic models. It follows one worked example from start to finish. It does not
cover modern language models. The book leaves them out too, for a reason that matters to most
social scientists (see [what this leaves out](#what-counting-leaves-out)).

## From words to a matrix

### The vocabulary

The book uses a few terms throughout [@jacobucci_ml]:

- A **document** is one observation's text. It can be a single sentence or a whole book.
- A **corpus** is the collection of documents.
- **Tokenization** splits documents into units. The units can be characters, words or sentences,
  depending on the purpose. Words are the most common choice.
- **Stop words** are words like *a*, *and*, *the* that carry little meaning and are usually
  removed.
- **Stemming** reduces word forms to a common root, so *loves*, *loving* and *loved* all become
  *love*.

The stem need not be a real word. *Easy* becomes *easi* and *lecture* becomes *lectur*. The book's
verdict is that "this does not matter". What matters is that every form of the word gets counted in
the same column.

### Preprocessing

Getting from raw text to tokens takes several steps [@jacobucci_ml]:

1. Extract the text from its container (HTML, JSON).
2. Remove names, emails and other identifiers.
3. Deal with special characters. Emoji can be removed, but if you want sentiment, they may be
   better recoded as meaningful words.
4. Expand abbreviations ("HW" means homework).
5. Correct spelling with a spell-checker.
6. Remove stop words and stem.

The authors' summary is that preprocessing "is much more critical than in quantitative data
cleaning". In practice you repeat it alongside tokenization until the counts look sensible.

### n-grams

Single words lose context. "Not easy at all" contains *easy*, and counted word by word it reads as
praise. **n-grams** count runs of words instead, which keeps some of the context: bigrams are
two-word runs ("not easy") and trigrams are three-word runs ("not very helpful").

### The document-term matrix

The output of all this is the **document-term matrix (DTM)**. Rows are documents, columns are
tokens, and each cell holds a statistic, usually a count. The matrix is almost always **sparse**.
Any one comment uses a few dozen words out of a vocabulary of tens of thousands, so nearly every
cell is zero.

In the worked example below, the DTM has 27,939 rows and 18,216 columns, and fewer than 0.2% of the
cells are non-zero. Stored as a full matrix it takes about 3.8 GB. Stored as a sparse matrix,
which records only the non-zero cells and where they are, it takes about 18 MB [@jacobucci_ml].
The sparse format is not a minor optimisation. Without it, many corpora are too big to analyse at
all.

## The worked example: 27,939 professor ratings

The chapter uses one dataset throughout: **27,939 student evaluations of 999 professors** scraped
from a ratings website [@jacobucci_ml]. Each evaluation has three parts:

- a **rating** from 1 ("Profs get F's too") to 5 ("A real gem"), with a mean of 3.85;
- a **difficulty** score from 1 to 5, with a mean of 2.88;
- a **free-text comment**, with names and universities removed.

The comments contain 1,223,454 words, of which 18,216 are distinct. The average comment is 43.8
words long.

### Counting

The most frequent words are useless: *the* appears 41,864 times, followed by *and*, *to*, *a*. The
authors built a stop list by combining three standard lists, SMART, snowball and onix, which
together hold 1,149 words. Then they checked the combined list **by hand**. Some "stop words" carry
meaning in teaching evaluations. *Least* matters in "the least organised professor", and *best*
matters too, so both were kept. They also added domain words such as *professor*, *teacher* and
*Dr*. The final list had 619 words [@jacobucci_ml]. The general rule is that no stop list is
universal. Any word can be a stop word for a particular purpose.

With stop words removed, the top words are *easy, great, good, tests, hard, work, best, help*. Once
stemmed, *help*, *test* and *easi* lead. The most common bigrams are *great teacher*, *very
helpful* and *extra credit*, each with over 1,000 occurrences. Bigrams also pick up *not hard*,
*not helpful*, and the gradations *very helpful* and *extremely helpful*, which single words would
have merged [@jacobucci_ml].

### A first measurement: who is "he" and who is "she"?

The website does not record the professor's gender. The authors inferred it by counting gendered
words in each professor's comments. The male list was *he, his, him, he's, mr*, and so on, and the
female list was *she, her, she's, miss, mrs*, and so on. Each professor was assigned to whichever
list was more frequent across all their comments. Counts per professor were usually lopsided, such
as 153 to 0 or 0 to 118. Still, 27.8% of professors had at least some words from both lists.
Pooling many comments made the label reliable, even though any single comment might use the wrong
pronoun. The result was 415 female professors (42%) and 584 male (58%) [@jacobucci_ml].

The authors then compared the two groups:

- **Rating:** male professors averaged 3.92 and female professors 3.76. The difference is 0.16,
  Cohen's *d* = 0.11, statistically significant but small.
- **Difficulty:** 2.877 against 2.886, which is no difference [@jacobucci_ml].

This is a small model of the whole field. A **construct** (gender) was **measured** by counting
words, and the measurement can be wrong. The resulting variable then went into an ordinary
comparison of means.

## Sentiment: scoring words with a dictionary

**Sentiment analysis**, also called opinion mining, scores text by its emotional valence. The
common approach uses a **dictionary**, also called a **lexicon**: a list of words that people have
labelled as positive or negative. A text's score is the sum or average of the scores of its words.
The book compares four lexicons [@jacobucci_ml]:

| Lexicon | Size | Scoring | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| **AFINN** | 2,477 words and phrases | integers from −5 to +5 | *masterpiece* scores +4, *robber* −2 |
| **nrc** | 6,468 words | positive or negative, plus 8 emotions (anger, anticipation, disgust, fear, joy, sadness, surprise, trust) for 4,463 of them | labelled by crowd-workers |
| **bing** | 6,874 words and phrases | mostly +1 or −1 | |
| **syuzhet** | 10,738 words | 16 values between −1 and +1 | built from novels, so it "may work best for such text" |

: Sentiment lexicons in Jacobucci et al. Ch 11. {.striped}

The lexicons disagree. One glowing comment in the book scores **6** with bing and **5.35** with
syuzhet. Neither number means anything by itself. As the authors put it, the size of a sentiment
score "cannot be determined simply by the score without defining a norm first". The advice is to
pick a lexicon that suits the purpose and **read through its word list**, adding and removing words
for your domain [@jacobucci_ml].

### Does it measure anything? Check it against the rating

The useful step is **validation**. Across the corpus, bing scores average 1.68 (SD 2.37), so on the
whole the comments are positive. The most common positive words are *easy, great, good,
interesting*. The most common negative words are *hard, difficult, boring, horrible*. Positive
comments reuse the same few words, while negative ones use a wider vocabulary. The sentiment
scores correlate with the students' own numerical ratings [@jacobucci_ml]:

| | Rating | Difficulty | bing |
|---|---|---|---|
| bing sentiment | **0.576** | −0.323 | |
| syuzhet sentiment | 0.561 | −0.303 | 0.852 |

: Sentiment scores against the numerical ratings (Jacobucci et al. Table 11.10). {.striped}

The authors read this as evidence of **criterion validity**, "if one trusts the numerical ratings".
This is the same move as validating any new scale against an established one, as in
[concepts and measurement](../00-overture/concepts-and-measurement.qmd). A sentiment score is a
measurement instrument, and it needs a validity check like any other.

### Valence shifters

Dictionaries score words one at a time, so they miss words that change the meaning of their
neighbours. These are called **valence shifters**:

- **negators** (*isn't*) flip the sign;
- **amplifiers** (*very*, *extremely*) strengthen it;
- **de-amplifiers** (*barely*) weaken it;
- **adversative conjunctions** (*but*) shift the weight to the clause that follows.

The book's example has all of them at work. A chemistry course is "not an easy course" and
"frustrating", *but* with this professor it is "actually interesting, and fun". Scoring the words
one at a time gets this badly wrong. Shifters are common: negators appeared alongside sentiment
words about 20% of the time.

Applying shifter weights changed the scores only moderately: the two versions correlate at about
0.86. But the correlation with the rating **rose from 0.576 to 0.655** [@jacobucci_ml]. The
adjusted score is a better measure of the same construct.

## Topic models: documents as mixtures of themes

Sentiment asks how positive a comment is. A **topic model** asks what it is about. The book
explains LDA in terms of how a student writes an evaluation. First they decide which aspects to
comment on, such as how the lectures were organised or how hard the tests were. Then they choose
words that fit each aspect [@jacobucci_ml].

**Latent Dirichlet allocation (LDA)** runs that process backwards. It assumes:

- each **topic** is a probability distribution over the whole vocabulary. A "teaching
  effectiveness" topic puts weight on *prepared*, *clarity*, *organised*. A "personality" topic
  puts weight on *nice*, *open*, *warm*.
- each **document** is a mixture of a small number of topics, with its own proportions $\theta$.
  A 100-word comment with 70 effectiveness words and 30 personality words has
  $\theta = (0.7, 0.3)$.
- each word is produced by first picking a topic using the document's $\theta$, then picking a word
  using that topic's distribution.

Only the words are observed. Estimation recovers the topics and every document's $\theta$ from the
counts alone, which the book calls "a reverse-engineering process". The original paper, Blei, Ng
and Jordan (2003), used a variational EM algorithm [@jacobucci_ml].

The figure fits LDA to a corpus whose true topics we know, because we generated it.

```{python}
#| label: fig-lda
#| fig-cap: "LDA recovering hidden topics (our simulation, not data from the sources). We generated 300 'evaluations' of 40 words each from two known topics: teaching effectiveness and personality. The word 'class' is common in both, as it is in the book's data. Each document's topic mix was drawn at random. Left: the two true topic-word distributions (light bars) and LDA's estimates (dark bars), recovered from the word counts alone. Right: each document's true share of 'teaching' words against LDA's estimate (correlation ≈ 0.97). The orange point is the document closest to the book's example mix of 0.7/0.3. Its estimate is noticeably off: 40 words carry limited information about a mixture."
#| code-fold: true
#| code-summary: "Show the figure code"
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(11)
vocab = ["class", "lectur", "clear", "organ", "prepar", "exam",
         "nice", "funni", "warm", "open", "care", "help"]
V, K, M, N = len(vocab), 2, 300, 40
beta_true = np.array([
    [.16, .16, .14, .14, .12, .14, .01, .01, .01, .01, .02, .08],   # teaching effectiveness
    [.16, .01, .02, .01, .01, .01, .16, .14, .14, .12, .12, .10],   # personality
])
beta_true /= beta_true.sum(1, keepdims=True)
theta_true = rng.dirichlet([0.6, 0.6], M)                 # each document's topic mix
z = (rng.random((M, N)) > theta_true[:, [0]]).astype(int)  # topic of each word slot
w = np.array([[rng.choice(V, p=beta_true[k]) for k in row] for row in z])

# Fit LDA by Gibbs sampling: alternate topic labels | (theta, beta) and (theta, beta) | labels
alpha, delta, iters, burn = 0.5, 0.1, 400, 200
theta = rng.dirichlet([alpha] * K, M)
beta = rng.dirichlet([delta * 10] * V, K)
th_sum, be_sum = np.zeros_like(theta), np.zeros_like(beta)
docs, wf = np.repeat(np.arange(M), N), w.ravel()
for it in range(iters):
    p = theta[docs] * beta[:, wf].T
    p /= p.sum(1, keepdims=True)
    zk = (rng.random(len(wf)) > p[:, 0]).astype(int)
    ndk = np.zeros((M, K)); np.add.at(ndk, (docs, zk), 1)
    nkw = np.zeros((K, V)); np.add.at(nkw, (zk, wf), 1)
    theta = np.array([rng.dirichlet(alpha + r) for r in ndk])
    beta = np.array([rng.dirichlet(delta + r) for r in nkw])
    if it >= burn:
        th_sum += theta; be_sum += beta
theta_hat, beta_hat = th_sum / (iters - burn), be_sum / (iters - burn)
if beta_hat[0, 1] < beta_hat[1, 1]:                        # topics are unlabelled: align them
    theta_hat, beta_hat = theta_hat[:, ::-1], beta_hat[::-1]
r = np.corrcoef(theta_true[:, 0], theta_hat[:, 0])[0, 1]
j = np.argmin(np.abs(theta_true[:, 0] - 0.7))

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(10, 4.2), gridspec_kw={"width_ratios": [1.6, 1]})
ax = axes[0]
x = np.arange(V)
for k, (off, name) in enumerate([(-0.2, "teaching"), (0.2, "personality")]):
    sign = 1 if k == 0 else -1                             # teaching up, personality down
    ax.bar(x + off, sign * beta_true[k], width=0.38, color="#b7b6a8",
           label="true" if k == 0 else None)
    ax.bar(x + off, sign * beta_hat[k], width=0.18, color="#161613",
           label="LDA estimate" if k == 0 else None)
ax.axhline(0, color="#161613", lw=0.8)
ax.set_xticks(x); ax.set_xticklabels(vocab, rotation=45, ha="right", fontsize=9)
ax.set_ylabel("P(word | topic)\n↑ teaching   ↓ personality")
yt = ax.get_yticks(); ax.set_yticks(yt); ax.set_yticklabels([f"{abs(t):.2f}" for t in yt])
ax.legend(frameon=False, fontsize=9, loc="upper center", bbox_to_anchor=(0.66, 0.98))
ax.set_title("Topics: what each theme talks about", fontsize=11)

ax = axes[1]
ax.plot([0, 1], [0, 1], color="#161613", lw=1, ls="--")
ax.scatter(theta_true[:, 0], theta_hat[:, 0], s=14, color="#5e5e54", alpha=0.6)
ax.scatter(theta_true[j, 0], theta_hat[j, 0], s=70, color="#e8531d", zorder=3)
ax.annotate(f"true {theta_true[j, 0]:.2f}\nestimated {theta_hat[j, 0]:.2f}",
            (theta_true[j, 0], theta_hat[j, 0]), xytext=(0.62, 0.12),
            fontsize=9, color="#e8531d", arrowprops=dict(arrowstyle="-", color="#e8531d"))
ax.text(0.03, 0.92, f"r = {r:.2f}", fontsize=10)
ax.set_xlabel("true share of 'teaching' words, θ")
ax.set_ylabel("LDA estimate of θ")
ax.set_xlim(0, 1); ax.set_ylim(0, 1)
ax.set_title("Documents: what mix each one is", fontsize=11)
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

The topics come back almost exactly, including the shared word *class*. The model learns that
*class* belongs to both topics rather than labelling it as one. Each document's mixture is
recovered well on average. For any single 40-word document, though, the estimate is
noisy, so per-document topic shares come with real uncertainty.

### LDA on the professor data

For the real analysis, each professor's comments were pooled into one document, giving 999
documents. After removing stop words, stemming, and dropping words seen fewer than five times,
499,836 words remained, 3,064 of them distinct [@jacobucci_ml]. The analysis then had three steps.

**1. Choose the number of topics.** The authors fitted models with 2 to 9 topics, using 5-fold
cross-validation and **perplexity**, a measure of how well the model predicts held-out words. Lower
is better. Perplexity **kept falling** as topics were added. The authors chose **six topics**
because the improvement slowed down between six and seven [@jacobucci_ml]. This is an elbow
judgement, the same kind as a scree plot. It is not a test, and the cross-validated criterion does
not settle the choice by itself. See [cross-validation](cross-validation.qmd).

**2. Read the topics.** A topic is just a ranked list of words with probabilities. To judge whether
a word stands out, compare it with the uniform baseline. If every word were equally likely, each
would have probability 1/3,064, about 0.03%. The stem *lectur* has 3.29% in topic 3, more than 100
times the baseline. Meanwhile *class* is the top word in five of the six topics [@jacobucci_ml],
so a frequent word can lead a topic's list without telling you what the topic is about.

**3. Name the topics.** This step is human work. The authors named only two:

- **topic 3**, about lecturing, from *lectur*, *test*, *exam*, *materi*;
- **topic 5**, about how students perceived the teaching, since *great*, *best*, *interest* and
  *funni* appear only there.

They leave the other four as an exercise, and say that meaningful names require "a thorough
understanding of the problem under investigation" [@jacobucci_ml].

**4. Use the output.** Each professor now has a vector of topic proportions. For example,
Professor 1's comments are 55% topic 2. These proportions are new variables. They can be used to
group professors by teaching style or entered into a regression [@jacobucci_ml].

**How LDA relates to older methods.** The book compares LDA with **latent semantic analysis
(LSA)**, an older method. LSA factorises the DTM, like [principal components](../ml-track/unsupervised-learning.qmd).
LDA is a generative **mixture model**, like latent class analysis. The difference "can be boiled
down to PCA versus LCA" [@jacobucci_ml]. LDA has also been extended in several directions:

- correlated topic models;
- **supervised** topic models, in which an outcome helps shape the topics;
- supervised models that also take covariates.

The [mixture-models](../ml-track/mixture-models-and-em.qmd) entry explains the EM machinery behind
these models.

## What counting leaves out {#what-counting-leaves-out}

Everything above treats a document as a **bag of words**, a set of counts with the word order
thrown away. n-grams and valence shifters only patch the problem locally. Neural language models
handle word order properly. The book deliberately leaves them out, because many social-science
studies "do not have the amount of text necessary" to train them [@jacobucci_ml].

The authors also make a practical point about small samples and short responses. In that setting,
a validated dictionary such as **LIWC**, with about 6,400 words and stems scored on 90 components,
will probably predict better than more complex algorithms [@jacobucci_ml]. This fits the theme of
the [bias–variance](bias-variance-tradeoff.qmd) entry. A fixed dictionary has no parameters to
overfit, and with little data that matters more than flexibility.

## Common pitfalls

::: {.callout-warning title="Where this goes wrong"}
- **Using a stop list without reading it.** Standard lists remove words that carry meaning in your
  domain, such as *least* and *best* in teaching evaluations, and *not* almost everywhere.
  Read the list before you use it.
- **Treating a sentiment score as meaningful on its own.** "This comment scores 6" means nothing
  without a norm. Scores from different lexicons are not comparable. Validate against an outcome
  you trust, as the authors did with the ratings.
- **Scoring words without context.** Negators, amplifiers and "but" clauses are common. Ignoring
  them weakens the measure: the correlation with the rating was 0.576 without shifters and 0.655
  with them.
- **Using a lexicon built for other text.** syuzhet was built from contemporary novels and "may
  work best for such text". Check that a lexicon's source resembles your documents.
- **Treating the number of topics as discovered.** Perplexity usually keeps improving as topics
  are added. The final number is a judgement. Report the curve and show that your conclusions
  hold with neighbouring choices.
- **Overreading topic labels.** A topic is a word distribution. Its name is the researcher's
  interpretation, and frequent words such as *class* can top several topics. Name topics from
  the distinctive words, and say which topics you could not name.
- **Forgetting that text-derived variables are measured with error.** Inferred gender, sentiment
  scores and topic shares are all estimates. When they enter a regression, the errors-in-variables
  problems in [measurement & ML](measurement-and-ml.qmd) apply. A gap in ratings by inferred gender
  is a description, not a causal finding; see [regression & causality](../03-regression/regression-and-causality.qmd).
:::

## Appendix · the mathematics {.unnumbered}

::: {.math-appendix}
**LDA's generative model** (in the book's notation). There are $K$ topics, $M$ documents and $V$
distinct words. For each document $m$ and each word position $n$:
$$
\theta_m \sim \text{Dirichlet}(\alpha), \qquad \beta_k \sim \text{Dirichlet}(\delta),
$$
$$
z_{mn} \sim \text{Multinomial}(\theta_m), \qquad w_{mn} \mid z_{mn}=k \sim \text{Multinomial}(\beta_k).
$$
Here $\theta_m=(\theta_{m1},\dots,\theta_{mK})$ sums to one; it is the document's topic mix.
$\beta_k=(\beta_{k1},\dots,\beta_{kV})$ is topic $k$'s distribution over words. The **Dirichlet** is
a distribution over probability vectors, the multi-category version of the beta distribution.
Small $\alpha$ favours documents dominated by one or two topics. Small $\delta$ favours topics
concentrated on a few words. Summing out the topic labels, each document's word probabilities are a
mixture:
$$
P(w_{mn}=v \mid \theta_m, \beta) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \theta_{mk}\,\beta_{kv}.
$$
This is the low-rank structure that LDA shares with LSA's matrix factorisation: the
$M \times V$ matrix of expected word shares equals $\Theta B$, where $\Theta$ is $M\times K$ and $B$
is $K \times V$.

**The sampler in the figure** (our illustration). Given $\theta$ and $\beta$, each word's topic is
drawn with probability
$$
P(z_{mn}=k \mid w_{mn}=v) = \frac{\theta_{mk}\,\beta_{kv}}{\sum_j \theta_{mj}\,\beta_{jv}} .
$$
Then, by Dirichlet–multinomial conjugacy, $\theta_m \mid z \sim \text{Dirichlet}(\alpha + n_{m\cdot})$
and $\beta_k \mid z, w \sim \text{Dirichlet}(\delta + n_{k\cdot})$. Here $n_{m\cdot}$ counts document
$m$'s words assigned to each topic, and $n_{k\cdot}$ counts each word's assignments to topic $k$.
Alternating these steps is a Gibbs sampler; see [MCMC in depth](../02-inference/mcmc-in-depth.qmd).
The topics come out unlabelled, so "topic 1" in one run can be "topic 2" in the next. That is why
the code aligns them at the end.

**Perplexity.** For held-out documents with $N$ words in total and log-likelihood
$\log p(\mathbf{w})$,
$$
\text{perplexity} = \exp\!\Big(-\tfrac{1}{N}\log p(\mathbf{w})\Big),
$$
the **inverse** geometric mean of the per-word likelihood. A model that guessed uniformly over $V$
words would have perplexity $V$. The book's cross-validated curve sits at roughly 2,000 to 2,150
(read from its Figure 11.23), below the uniform baseline of $V = 3{,}064$.

**Why sparse storage matters.** $27{,}939 \times 18{,}216 \approx 5.09\times10^{8}$ cells at 8 bytes
each is about 4.07 GB (3.8 GiB), the book's figure. At under 0.2% density, storing only the non-zero
values with their row indices and column pointers takes tens of megabytes.
:::
