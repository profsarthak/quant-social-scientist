Selection Stability: Can You Trust What the Model Picked?

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source

The lasso and the decision tree both hand you a shortlist of variables. Redraw the sample and the shortlist changes. Here is how to measure that, and what to do about it.

When an algorithm picks variables for you, the picking is a yes-or-no decision, and yes-or-no decisions are fragile: a slightly different sample, or even a different random split for cross-validation, can produce a different shortlist. The honest move is to run the selection many times on resampled data and report how often each variable is chosen. A single “it was selected” is not evidence.

Why it matters

Social scientists rarely use the lasso or a decision tree only to predict. They use them to choose: which of 24 personality items predict agreeableness, which background variables split a sample into risk groups, which interactions deserve a theory. The chosen variables then go into a results table, and readers treat that table like a list of findings. Jacobucci, Grimm and Zhang put the problem at the centre of their treatment of both methods: in any procedure that selects variables, the stability of the final model is always somewhat in question, and the doubt concerns whether the result would hold in another sample, not whether it fits this one. (Jacobucci et al. 2023) Murphy makes the same point about the lasso: it “can give quite different results if the data is perturbed slightly.” (Murphy 2012)

This entry is the selection counterpart to the cross-validation entry. Cross-validation tells you whether a model predicts well out of sample. It does not tell you whether the list of variables is reproducible, and the two questions can come apart.

Selection is a yes/no decision, and yes/no decisions wobble

Jacobucci et al. show the problem with a classic dataset: the Holzinger–Swineford ability tests (301 children). (Jacobucci et al. 2023) They fit the same model three times: on the first 100 children, on the next 100, and on all 301.

  • Plain regression barely moves. The coefficients shift a little from sample to sample. At p<.05 only one predictor (x_3, in the second hundred) reaches significance, and only in one sample. Read across the three columns, the conclusions mostly agree.
  • Add backward elimination and the story changes. The first 100 keep x_4 and x_5; the next 100 keep x_3, x_4 and x_6; the full sample keeps x_2 and x_4. Only x_4 survives every time.
  • A decision tree is worse still. The three trees split on \{x_5, x_2\}, \{x_2, x_3\} and \{x_4, x_2, x_3\}. Even where the same variable appears, the cutpoint moves: x_2 is cut at 5.875, 6.125 and 7.375. (Jacobucci et al. 2023)

Their conclusion is the key idea of this entry: the instability comes from the selection step and the hard cutpoints, not from binary trees as such. (Jacobucci et al. 2023) An estimate that moves a little becomes a choice that flips once it is pushed through a threshold.

The lasso inherits the same fragility, because cross-validation itself is random. On their bfi example (24 predictors of an agreeableness score), one run of the lasso with 10-fold CV and the one-standard-error rule selected eight variables. With a different random seed for the folds, the selected set changed again and now included a college-age variable. (Jacobucci et al. 2023) Nothing about the data changed. Only the random assignment of rows to folds did.

Why cross-validation does not fix it

It is tempting to think that tuning \lambda by cross-validation picks the “right” variables. Murphy explains why it does not. (Murphy 2012) CV chooses \lambda for predictive accuracy. Because the lasso shrinks the relevant coefficients along with the irrelevant ones, CV avoids a large \lambda that would shrink the real effects too much. The price is a less sparse model that lets in false positives. Murphy cites the formal result (Meinshausen & Bühlmann, 2006): the prediction-optimal \lambda does not recover the true set of variables, even with infinite data. A method that does recover it as N\to\infty is called model-selection consistent, and plain CV-tuned lasso is not one. (Murphy 2012) Prediction and selection are different goals, and one tuning knob cannot serve both.

Stability selection: count how often, not whether

The fix is simple to state. Repeat the selection many times on random subsamples and record how often each variable is picked. This is stability selection (Meinshausen & Bühlmann, 2010). (Jacobucci et al. 2023; Murphy 2012) Instead of the usual regularisation path (each coefficient’s value as \lambda changes), you look at a stability path: each variable’s probability of being selected under resampling. Then you keep the variables whose probability clears a threshold. (Jacobucci et al. 2023)

NoteJacobucci et al.’s bfi example

With 24 predictors of the agreeableness sum score, they ran the lasso on 1,000 subsamples (stabs package), each time stopping once it had selected seven variables. Allowing several picks per run reduces the chance that one of two highly correlated predictors simply suppresses the other. At a threshold of 0.9, five variables survive: C2, C3, E3, E4 and E5. The single CV-tuned lasso had picked those five plus C5, O2 and gender. (Jacobucci et al. 2023)

Two points the authors stress. The threshold is a tuning parameter you choose, on practical and theoretical grounds; 0.9 is not a law. And stability selection usually gives a sparser model, which helps when one lasso run selects too much. (Jacobucci et al. 2023)

Murphy adds two useful readings. (Murphy 2012)

  • It approximates a Bayesian quantity. The share of resamples that select variable j approximates its posterior inclusion probability, p(\gamma_j = 1 \mid \mathcal D): the probability, given the data, that variable j belongs in the model. Murphy calls the Bayesian version “much more robust” than a single lasso fit.
  • Bolasso (Bach, 2008) runs the lasso at a fixed \lambda on bootstrap samples and keeps a variable only if it appears in at least 90% of runs. Taking this near-intersection removes the false positives that the plain lasso lets in, and it is model-selection consistent under a wider range of conditions. Murphy replicates Bach’s experiment (256 datasets, N=1{,}000, 16 variables of which 8 are relevant). The bolasso recovers the right set over a wide range of \lambda where the lasso does not, and about 32 bootstraps is a good compromise between speed and accuracy. (Murphy 2012)

See it

Panel (a) below builds this by hand. Twenty predictors arrive in four correlated blocks of five (like item sets on a questionnaire), and four of them carry real signal. Each bar is a variable’s selection probability across 500 half-samples. Each dot shows how often one CV-tuned lasso kept that variable when only the random fold assignment changed. Panel (b) is about trees and anticipates the section below. It shows what a greedy splitter does when no predictor matters.

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# ---------- (a) stability selection vs one cross-validated lasso ----------
rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
n, p = 200, 20
blk = np.repeat(np.arange(4), 5)                 # four blocks of five correlated "items"
Z = rng.normal(size=(n, 4))
X = 0.6 * Z[:, blk] + 0.8 * rng.normal(size=(n, p))
beta = np.zeros(p); beta[[0, 1, 5, 10]] = [0.45, 0.35, 0.45, 0.35]   # x1, x2, x6, x11 matter
y = X @ beta + rng.normal(0, 1.0, n)

def lasso_path(X, y, lams, iters=200):
    """Coordinate descent for (1/2n)||y - Xb||^2 + lam ||b||_1, warm-started down a lambda grid."""
    n, p = X.shape; b = np.zeros(p); r = y.copy(); out = []
    for lam in lams:
        for _ in range(iters):
            step = 0.0
            for j in range(p):
                old = b[j]; rho = X[:, j] @ r / n + old
                new = np.sign(rho) * max(abs(rho) - lam, 0.0)
                if new != old:
                    r += X[:, j] * (old - new); b[j] = new; step = max(step, abs(new - old))
            if step < 1e-6: break
        out.append(b.copy())
    return np.array(out)

def prep(X, y):
    return (X - X.mean(0)) / X.std(0), y - y.mean()

def grid(Xs, yc):
    return np.max(np.abs(Xs.T @ yc)) / len(yc) * np.logspace(0, -2.5, 60)

# stability selection: 500 half-samples, lasso stopped once it has picked 7 variables
B = 500; picked = np.zeros(p)
for _ in range(B):
    idx = rng.choice(n, n // 2, replace=False)
    Xb, yb = prep(X[idx], y[idx])
    path = lasso_path(Xb, yb, grid(Xb, yb))
    picked += path[np.argmax((path != 0).sum(1) >= 7)] != 0
stab = picked / B

# one lasso, 10-fold CV + one-SE rule, re-run with 40 different fold assignments
Xs, yc = prep(X, y); lams = grid(Xs, yc); full = lasso_path(Xs, yc, lams)
single = np.zeros(p); sets = set()
for seed in range(40):
    folds = np.random.default_rng(seed).permutation(n) % 10
    err = np.zeros((10, len(lams)))
    for f in range(10):
        tr, te = folds != f, folds == f
        mu, sd, ym = X[tr].mean(0), X[tr].std(0), y[tr].mean()
        path = lasso_path((X[tr] - mu) / sd, y[tr] - ym, lams)
        err[f] = ((y[te][:, None] - ym - ((X[te] - mu) / sd) @ path.T) ** 2).mean(0)
    m, se = err.mean(0), err.std(0, ddof=1) / np.sqrt(10)
    k = np.min(np.where(m <= m.min() + se[np.argmin(m)])[0])     # sparsest model within 1 SE
    chosen = full[k] != 0; single += chosen; sets.add(tuple(np.flatnonzero(chosen)))
single /= 40

# ---------- (b) which variable does a greedy split pick when NOTHING matters? ----------
rb = np.random.default_rng(3)
def best_gain(x, y):
    o = np.argsort(x, kind="stable"); xs, cs = x[o], np.cumsum(y[o])
    N = len(y); k = np.arange(1, N); ok = xs[1:] != xs[:-1]
    g = cs[:-1]**2 / k + (cs[-1] - cs[:-1])**2 / (N - k) - cs[-1]**2 / N
    return g[ok].max() if ok.any() else 0.0

levels = [2, 4, 10, 50, None]                    # None = continuous (100 distinct values)
names = ["2 values", "4 values", "10 values", "50 values", "continuous"]
greedy = np.zeros(5); permsel = np.zeros(5); reps, nb, P = 500, 100, 99
for _ in range(reps):
    Xr = [rb.integers(0, L, nb).astype(float) if L else rb.normal(size=nb) for L in levels]
    yr = rb.normal(size=nb)                      # outcome unrelated to every predictor
    g = np.array([best_gain(x, yr) for x in Xr])
    greedy[np.argmax(g)] += 1
    pv = np.array([(1 + sum(best_gain(x, rb.permutation(yr)) >= g[v] for _ in range(P))) / (P + 1)
                   for v, x in enumerate(Xr)])
    permsel[rb.choice(np.flatnonzero(pv == pv.min()))] += 1
greedy /= reps; permsel /= reps

# ---------- draw ----------
fig, (a, b) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(10, 5.2), gridspec_kw={"width_ratios": [1.15, 1]})
order = np.argsort(stab); ypos = np.arange(p)
truth = {0, 1, 5, 10}
cols = ["#e8531d" if stab[j] >= 0.9 else "#b7b6a8" for j in order]
a.barh(ypos, stab[order], color=cols, height=0.7, edgecolor="#f4f3ec")
a.scatter(single[order], ypos, s=26, color="#161613", zorder=3,
          label="share of 40 CV runs\nthat kept it (one lasso)")
a.axvline(0.9, color="#5e5e54", ls="--", lw=1)
a.text(0.885, p - 0.55, "threshold 0.9", fontsize=8, color="#5e5e54", ha="right")
a.set_yticks(ypos)
a.set_yticklabels([f"x{j+1}" + ("  (real)" if j in truth else "") for j in order], fontsize=8)
a.set_xlim(0, 1.02); a.set_xlabel("selection probability across 500 half-samples")
a.set_title("(a) Stability selection vs one cross-validated lasso", fontsize=10)
a.legend(fontsize=8, frameon=False, loc="lower right")

xb = np.arange(5); w = 0.38
b.bar(xb - w/2, greedy, w, color="#161613", label="best split wins (CART-style)")
b.bar(xb + w/2, permsel, w, color="#b7b6a8", label="smallest permutation p wins")
b.axhline(0.2, color="#8a8a7e", ls=":", lw=1.2)
b.text(-0.45, 0.212, "fair share 1/5", fontsize=8, color="#8a8a7e")
b.set_xticks(xb); b.set_xticklabels(names, fontsize=8)
b.set_ylabel("share of 500 null datasets where it is split on first")
b.set_xlabel("pure-noise predictor (number of distinct values)")
b.set_title("(b) With no signal, greedy splits favour many cutpoints", fontsize=10)
b.legend(fontsize=8, frameon=False, loc="upper left")
fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()

How to read panel (a). The single lasso is consistent in one way: it keeps x_1, x_2 and x_6 in every run. It is also consistently wrong in another: it keeps the proxy x_3 in 39 of 40 runs. x_3 has no effect of its own, but it is correlated with two real predictors, and the prediction-tuned \lambda is small enough to let it in. This is Murphy’s false-positive mechanism. (Murphy 2012) Across half-samples, x_3 is selected only 73% of the time, so stability selection drops it. The same run also shows the cost. The real but weaker x_{11} reaches 0.86 and falls just below a 0.9 threshold. Stability selection trades false positives for some risk of missing a real variable, and where you put the threshold decides that trade. This is why Jacobucci et al. call it a tuning choice. (Jacobucci et al. 2023)

What about p-values?

Many reviewers in the social sciences expect p-values, and “the lasso selected it” can sound unconvincing to them. Jacobucci et al. therefore describe the covariance test of Lockhart, Taylor, Tibshirani and Tibshirani (2014). It computes p-values for lasso coefficients while accounting for the fact that the lasso chose them adaptively. (Jacobucci et al. 2023) It is built on least angle regression (LARS), which computes the whole lasso path. LARS resembles stepwise regression: it enters predictors one at a time according to their correlation with the residuals. The difference is that each coefficient grows only gradually, until another predictor becomes equally correlated with the residual. (Jacobucci et al. 2023)

On their ECLS-K example the test confirmed all three variables the one-SE lasso had chosen, and it also gave gender p < .001. Gender would have been selected anyway if they had used the CV-minimum \lambda instead of the one-SE rule. (Jacobucci et al. 2023) The authors’ point is not the p-values themselves. A variable’s inclusion depended on which of two reasonable rules they applied, and that is selection instability again. If what you actually want is a confidence interval for one coefficient after the lasso has chosen the controls, that is a different and better-posed problem. See the double lasso.

Selecting sets, not single variables

Social science predictors often come in natural groups: the five items of a personality facet, or a set of demographic dummies. Sometimes the scientific question concerns the group, not the item.

The theory-led version is hierarchical regression. You enter predictor blocks in a sequence fixed in advance and test each block’s increase in R^2. This is not the same as hierarchical (multilevel) models, which are about random effects. (Jacobucci et al. 2023) Jacobucci et al. ran it on 150 bfi respondents, entering demographics, then conscientiousness, then openness, then neuroticism items:

Hierarchical regression on 150 bfi respondents (outcome: agreeableness). (Jacobucci et al. 2023)
Block added \Delta R^2 p
Demographics (4) .07 .016
Conscientiousness (5) .06 .070
Openness (5) .09 .008
Neuroticism (5) .06 .076

You could call Model 1 or Model 3 “best”, but only through an arbitrary p < .05 cutoff, because the four \Delta R^2 values are about the same size. (Jacobucci et al. 2023) (The same blockwise logic appears in multiple regression as sequential entry.)

The data-led version is the group lasso (Yuan & Lin). It penalises each group’s coefficient vector as a whole, so an entire group is either kept or set to zero together. (Jacobucci et al. 2023; Murphy 2012) Murphy explains why this works. The penalty is the length of each group’s coefficient vector, not the squared length. Squared lengths would add up to an ordinary ridge penalty. With the unsquared length, the only way to make the penalty small is to shrink every element of the group toward zero together. (Murphy 2012) Larger groups usually receive a proportionally larger penalty (scaled by \sqrt{\text{group size}}). Murphy’s examples come straight from everyday modelling: a categorical variable expanded into dummies, where you want all the dummies in or all out, and multinomial models with one coefficient per class. (Murphy 2012)

The group lasso gives no sparsity within a group. To also select items within the kept groups, use a bi-level method such as the sparse group lasso, which adds an ordinary lasso penalty, or the composite MCP. (Jacobucci et al. 2023) On the same 150 respondents the two methods tell different stories:

Group versus bi-level selection on the bfi subset. (Jacobucci et al. 2023)
Set Group lasso keeps? Bi-level (composite MCP) items kept
Demographics Yes 2
Conscientiousness Yes 1
Openness Yes 3

The group lasso keeps conscientiousness as a block. The bi-level method keeps only one of its items, C2 (“continue until everything is perfect”). That item had the highest correlation with agreeableness (.26) and with the other conscientiousness items, and it was the only one significant in the plain regression. Correlation among items had hidden which one carried the signal. (Jacobucci et al. 2023)

Searching for interactions without drowning

With p predictors there are about p^2/2 possible two-way interactions, far too many to type in by hand. Jacobucci et al. describe the hierarchical lasso (Bien, Taylor & Tibshirani, 2013). It penalises main effects and interactions together, under a hierarchy rule: (Jacobucci et al. 2023)

  • Strong hierarchy. An interaction X_jX_k may enter only if both main effects are non-zero.
  • Weak hierarchy. An interaction needs only one of its main effects to be non-zero.

They argue for strong hierarchy in the social sciences. An interaction is a product of two variables, and when either main effect is small, which is the usual case in social science, the interaction is unlikely to be large. (Jacobucci et al. 2023)

NoteECLS-K science scores: select on one half, estimate on the other

With N = 4{,}556, they ran the hierarchical lasso (strong hierarchy, CV, one-SE rule) on half the data. It selected main effects of math, general knowledge, income and gender; squared terms for math, knowledge, income and BMI; and two interactions, math × knowledge and knowledge × income. Weak hierarchy gave the same result. They then fitted an ordinary regression with only those terms on the other half (n = 2{,}278, R^2 = .403). With everything standardised, math is .376 and math × knowledge is −.098. As knowledge rises one SD above its mean, the effects of math and income both shrink. (Jacobucci et al. 2023)

Two lessons come from this. First, selecting on one half and estimating on the other keeps the reported coefficients from being chosen and tested on the same data, the same honesty that a held-out test set brings to prediction. Second, the authors warn that the “interaction” may partly be nonlinearity in disguise. Cognitive scores often have diminishing returns at the high end, which is exactly why they allowed squared terms. Searching for interactions while ignoring curvature is a known trap. (Jacobucci et al. 2023) (See the functional-form entry for how the two get confused.) A practical warning as well: the hierarchical lasso is slow. Fitting even ten penalty values can take hours, and the authors report runs that never finished. (Jacobucci et al. 2023)

The Bayesian route, briefly

Each penalty corresponds to a prior. Ridge corresponds to a normal prior on the coefficients and the lasso to a Laplace prior; the lasso estimate is the mode of the resulting posterior. (Jacobucci et al. 2023; Murphy 2012) Murphy adds a subtlety. Under a Laplace prior the posterior mean, the posterior median and the posterior draws are not sparse. Only the mode is, so a “Bayesian lasso” will not set anything exactly to zero. (Murphy 2012) For selection, Jacobucci et al. recommend sparsity-inducing hierarchical priors such as the horseshoe or spike-and-slab. One model run then learns how much shrinkage to apply, instead of a grid of 100 \lambda values. On 150 ECLS-K cases with 20 added noise variables, only math and knowledge had posteriors clearly away from zero. Selecting by 95% credible intervals was less clear-cut, because some noise coefficients had intervals excluding zero. (Jacobucci et al. 2023) They also caution that selection depends heavily on sample size: larger samples tend to select more variables. (Jacobucci et al. 2023) See hierarchical Bayesian models for the machinery.

Single trees: the same problem, drawn as a flowchart

The main criticism of decision trees is that they are unstable (Breiman, 1996): a small change in the data can produce a very different tree. (Jacobucci et al. 2023) Murphy gives the mechanism. A tree is grown top-down, so an arbitrary choice at the root changes everything below it. In frequentist terms, trees are high-variance estimators. (Murphy 2012) The trees-and-forests entry deals with this by averaging many trees. This entry asks the harder question: what if you need one tree to interpret?

NoteECLS-K: 100 bootstrap trees, 100 different trees

Predicting eighth-grade science from math and general knowledge, Jacobucci et al. compared linear regression with two tree algorithms over 100 bootstrap samples, scoring each by 10-fold CV. (Jacobucci et al. 2023)

Avg. terminal nodes (SD) CV RMSE CV R^2
Linear regression — 11.75 .37
CART (rpart) 15.42 (4.4) 11.51 .39
Conditional inference tree (ctree) 22.67 (9.4) 11.48 .40

Both tree methods predicted slightly better than the line, which is evidence that the relationship really is nonlinear. Yet every one of the bootstrap trees was a different structure, even after the cutpoints were rounded to one decimal place. (Jacobucci et al. 2023)

The tree had found real structure, but it did not find one particular structure. Jacobucci et al. are careful about what this implies. Instability is not a problem for describing the sample at hand. It is a problem for the claim that the same tree would appear in another sample. A single tree should be read as one of several structures that fit about equally well. (Jacobucci et al. 2023) Their practical advice: (Jacobucci et al. 2023)

  1. Round predictors to the precision you actually care about. If a split between 7.345 and 7.346 means nothing in your field, round. Fewer possible cutpoints give more stable trees and faster runs.
  2. Bootstrap the structure, not just the error. Refit on resamples and report which variables and cutpoints keep recurring (the authors’ dtree package has a stable() function for this).
  3. Check against an ensemble. Fit a random forest or boosting alongside the tree, and check whether the single tree’s variables are also the ones the ensemble keeps using.

Conditional inference trees: a test before every split

CART has a second, quieter bias. To split on an ordered predictor with m distinct values it tries m-1 cutpoints. For an unordered category with m levels it tries every two-way grouping, which is 2^{m-1}-1 splits. (Jacobucci et al. 2023) A predictor with more possible cutpoints gets more chances to produce a large fit improvement by luck. Panel (b) of the figure shows the result. When nothing matters, the greedy rule picks the continuous noise variable 44% of the time and the binary one 3%.

Conditional inference trees (ctree; Hothorn, Hornik & Zeileis, 2006) fix this with a permutation test at every node. (Jacobucci et al. 2023) The test asks how large the association between a predictor and the outcome is compared with what it would be if the outcome values were randomly reshuffled. Because each predictor is judged against its own null distribution, the number of cutpoints is accounted for. This is unbiased variable selection, and it gives the flat grey bars in panel (b). The test also tells the tree when to stop: if no predictor reaches p < .05 (the default), the node is not split. This guards against splits on noise while the tree is grown, so no pruning is needed afterwards. (Jacobucci et al. 2023)

In practice ctree tends to grow large trees (see its 22.7 average nodes above). The authors’ CTreePrune grows a deliberately oversized tree with a very lenient criterion. It then works from the bottom up, re-judging each split with a false-discovery-rate correction (Benjamini–Hochberg) from multiple testing. (Jacobucci et al. 2023)

CART’s own remedy is cost-complexity pruning, with the tree size chosen by CV. Jacobucci et al.’s tables show how much judgement is left over. On their suicidal-ideation example, CV error fell from 1.00 to 0.83 with the first split and then stayed between 0.82 and 0.84. On ECLS-K reading scores, the lowest CV error came at three splits, but the error bars of neighbouring sizes overlapped, and the one-SE rule chose a tree with a single split. (Jacobucci et al. 2023; Murphy 2012)

Reading a single tree honestly

Most splits are interactions. In a tree, a pure main effect means the same split appears with the same gain in every branch. Jacobucci et al. show a pure main-effects tree in which gender is split first and then depression is split identically within each gender, adding 4 points each time. With continuous predictors, an exact repeated cutpoint almost never happens in real data, so, following Berk, most splits in a real tree represent interactions. (Jacobucci et al. 2023) This is a strength (trees find interactions you never specified) and also a reason for caution: a tree cannot simply show a main effect.

Different routes, same destination. Trees can show equifinality: different combinations of predictor values leading to similar outcomes. They can also show multifinality: similar values leading to different outcomes. (Jacobucci et al. 2023) In a ctree of ECLS-K science scores, one leaf held children with lower knowledge (< 25.7) but higher math (> 28.63), and another held children with higher knowledge (up to 35.5). The two leaves had very similar science scores, consistent with strong math compensating for weaker knowledge. A third leaf’s scores ranged from about 40 to above 100: similar predictor values, very different outcomes. (Jacobucci et al. 2023) Two cautions apply. The pathways are only as trustworthy as the tree is stable, and a split says nothing about effect size. This four-leaf tree explained 37% of the variance. (Jacobucci et al. 2023)

Missing data: surrogate splits. When a case is missing the variable a node splits on (say, education ≤ 12 years vs more), CART uses a surrogate. It treats the primary split as an outcome to be predicted, finds the other variables that best reproduce it (rpart keeps five), and routes the case with those. (Jacobucci et al. 2023) Murphy’s verdict is mixed. Surrogates amount to learning a local model of how the predictors relate to each other, which is useful, but the method is “entirely ad hoc”. For a categorical predictor, the simpler alternative is to code “missing” as a category of its own. (Murphy 2012) Algorithms without surrogates fall back on multiple imputation. That gives K trees instead of one, but those K trees double as another stability check. (Jacobucci et al. 2023)

Variable importance and the near-duplicate. Collinear predictors compete for the same split, and only one can win it. Jacobucci et al. added a noisy copy of math (r = .95) and refit. The copy never appeared in the tree, but rpart’s importance scores, which also credit a variable for the splits it would have made as a surrogate, were math 39, knowledge 33 and the copy 28. (Jacobucci et al. 2023) So a variable missing from the tree can still be nearly as important as one in it. Treat only the ranking of importances as meaningful, not the exact numbers (Strobl, Malley & Tutz, 2009). (Jacobucci et al. 2023)

Common pitfalls

WarningWhere this goes wrong

  • Reporting one run’s selected set as “the predictors”. A lasso or stepwise shortlist from one sample and one CV seed is a single draw from a distribution of shortlists. Re-run it on resamples and report the selection frequencies. (Jacobucci et al. 2023; Murphy 2012)

  • Assuming CV-tuned \lambda finds the true variables. CV tunes for prediction, and the prediction-optimal lasso lets in correlated proxies. It is not model-selection consistent. (Murphy 2012)

  • Treating the stability threshold as objective. 0.9 is a dial. Higher values give fewer false positives and more missed real effects. State it and justify it. (Jacobucci et al. 2023)

  • Selecting and estimating on the same data. Coefficients refit on the rows that chose them are over-optimistic. Use a held-out half (as in the ECLS-K interaction search) or a post-selection method. (Jacobucci et al. 2023)

  • Calling a tree’s pathway a mechanism. A single tree is one of many near-equivalent structures, and its splits are mostly interactions with no effect sizes attached. Bootstrap it, compare it with an ensemble, and keep the explanatory language modest. (Jacobucci et al. 2023)

  • Trusting CART’s choice of split variable when predictors differ in granularity. Many-valued predictors win splits by chance. Use a conditional inference tree or round the predictors. (Jacobucci et al. 2023)

  • Reading “absent from the tree” as “unimportant”. A collinear twin can lose every split and still carry almost as much information. Check importances and surrogates. (Jacobucci et al. 2023)

Appendix · the mathematics

Stability selection. Let \hat S_\lambda(I) be the set of variables the lasso selects on the subsample I. Draw subsamples I_1,\dots,I_B (typically half the data each) and compute each variable’s selection frequency \hat\Pi_j \;=\; \frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\mathbf 1\!\left\{\,j\in \hat S_\lambda(I_b)\right\}, \qquad \hat S_{\text{stable}} \;=\; \{\,j : \hat\Pi_j \ge \pi_{\text{thr}}\,\}. In Murphy’s reading, \hat\Pi_j approximates the posterior inclusion probability p(\gamma_j=1\mid\mathcal D). The bolasso is the case of bootstrap resamples at a fixed \lambda with \pi_{\text{thr}}=0.9. (Murphy 2012) In the figure we follow Jacobucci et al.’s variant: each run moves down a \lambda grid until a fixed number of variables (7) have entered. (Jacobucci et al. 2023)

Model-selection consistency. An estimator \hat S_N of the true support S^*=\{j:w^*_j\neq0\} is model-selection consistent if P(\hat S_N = S^*)\to 1 as N\to\infty. The CV-optimal lasso is not. (Murphy 2012)

Group lasso. Partition the coefficients into groups g=1,\dots,G of sizes p_g: \hat\beta^{\text{grp}} = \arg\min_\beta \;\sum_{i=1}^N\Big(y_i-\beta_0-\sum_j x_{ij}\beta_j\Big)^2 + \lambda\sum_{g=1}^{G}\sqrt{p_g}\,\lVert\beta_g\rVert_2 . Squaring the group norms gives \sum_g\lVert\beta_g\rVert_2^2=\lVert\beta\rVert_2^2, which is ridge. Leaving them unsquared penalises the radius of each group’s vector, which can be small only if every element is small. The result is all-or-nothing sparsity by group. (Murphy 2012) The sparse group lasso adds \lambda_2\lVert\beta\rVert_1 to obtain sparsity within groups as well. (Jacobucci et al. 2023) In Bayesian terms, the group lasso is the MAP estimate under a prior proportional to \exp\!\big(-\tfrac{\gamma}{\sigma}\sum_g\lVert w_g\rVert_2\big). (Murphy 2012)

Interactions under hierarchy. The all-pairs model is Y=\beta_0+\sum_j\beta_jX_j+\tfrac12\sum_{j,k}\Theta_{jk}X_jX_k+\epsilon , with \Theta symmetric. Strong hierarchy imposes \Theta_{jk}\neq0\Rightarrow\beta_j\neq0 and \beta_k\neq0. Weak hierarchy imposes \Theta_{jk}\neq0\Rightarrow\beta_j\neq0 or \beta_k\neq0. The default in hierNet also lets the diagonal (squared terms) in. (Jacobucci et al. 2023)

Hierarchical regression arithmetic. With nested models M_0\subset\dots\subset M_K, R^2(M_K)=\sum_k\Delta R^2(M_k), where \Delta R^2(M_k)=(\text{RSS}_{k-1}-\text{RSS}_k)/\text{RSS}_0. The F test of block k uses the largest model’s residual mean square, F_k=\big[(\text{RSS}_{k-1}-\text{RSS}_k)/\Delta df_k\big]\big/\big[\text{RSS}_K/df_K\big]. Using Jacobucci et al.’s residual sums of squares (2190.83, 2036.00, 1908.60, 1708.12, 1583.23 on 149, 145, 140, 135, 130 df), this reproduces their F = 3.18, 2.09, 3.29, 2.05 and total R^2 = .277. Dividing by each step’s own residual mean square instead would give 2.76 for the first block. (Jacobucci et al. 2023)

Cutpoint counts and pruning. An ordered predictor with m distinct values offers m-1 binary splits. An unordered one offers 2^{m-1}-1. CART’s cost-complexity criterion is R_\alpha(T)=R(T)+\alpha\,|T|, with |T| the number of leaves. Increasing \alpha traces a nested sequence of subtrees, and CV with the one-SE rule chooses among them. (Jacobucci et al. 2023; Murphy 2012)

The permutation p-value in panel (b). For predictor v, let G_v be its best-split reduction in the sum of squares. Recompute it on P=99 random permutations of y, which gives G^{*}_{v,1},\dots,G^{*}_{v,P}, and set \hat p_v=\big(1+\#\{G^{*}_{v,r}\ge G_v\}\big)/(P+1). Each predictor is compared with its own null distribution, so a predictor with many cutpoints gains nothing from them. This is the idea behind conditional inference trees. (ctree itself uses a family of linear permutation statistics; our max-split version is a simplified stand-in.)

## Why it matters

Social scientists rarely use the [lasso](regularization.qmd) or a [decision tree](trees-and-forests.qmd)
only to predict. They use them to **choose**: which of 24 personality items predict agreeableness,
which background variables split a sample into risk groups, which interactions deserve a theory. The
chosen variables then go into a results table, and readers treat that table like a list of findings.
Jacobucci, Grimm and Zhang put the problem at the centre of their treatment of both methods: in any
procedure that selects variables, **the stability of the final model is always somewhat in
question**, and the doubt concerns whether the result would hold in another sample, not whether it
fits this one. [@jacobucci_ml] Murphy makes the same point about the lasso: it "can give quite
different results if the data is perturbed slightly." [@murphy_ml]

This entry is the **selection** counterpart to the [cross-validation](cross-validation.qmd) entry.
Cross-validation tells you whether a model *predicts* well out of sample. It does not tell you
whether the *list of variables* is reproducible, and the two questions can come apart.

## Selection is a yes/no decision, and yes/no decisions wobble {#sec-wobble}

Jacobucci et al. show the problem with a classic dataset: the Holzinger–Swineford ability tests (301
children). [@jacobucci_ml] They fit the same model three times: on the first 100 children, on the
next 100, and on all 301.

- **Plain regression barely moves.** The coefficients shift a little from sample to sample. At
  $p<.05$ only one predictor ($x_3$, in the second hundred) reaches significance, and only in one
  sample. Read across the three columns, the conclusions mostly agree.
- **Add backward elimination and the story changes.** The first 100 keep $x_4$ and $x_5$; the next 100
  keep $x_3$, $x_4$ and $x_6$; the full sample keeps $x_2$ and $x_4$. Only $x_4$ survives every time.
- **A decision tree is worse still.** The three trees split on $\{x_5, x_2\}$, $\{x_2, x_3\}$ and
  $\{x_4, x_2, x_3\}$. Even where the same variable appears, the cutpoint moves: $x_2$ is cut at
  5.875, 6.125 and 7.375. [@jacobucci_ml]

Their conclusion is the key idea of this entry: **the instability comes from the selection step
and the hard cutpoints, not from binary trees as such.** [@jacobucci_ml] An estimate that moves a
little becomes a choice that flips once it is pushed through a threshold.

The lasso inherits the same fragility, because cross-validation itself is random. On their `bfi`
example (24 predictors of an agreeableness score), one run of the lasso with 10-fold CV and the
[one-standard-error rule](cross-validation.qmd) selected eight variables. With a different random seed
for the folds, the selected set changed again and now included a college-age variable.
[@jacobucci_ml] Nothing about the data changed. Only the random assignment of rows to folds did.

### Why cross-validation does not fix it

It is tempting to think that tuning $\lambda$ by cross-validation picks the "right" variables. Murphy
explains why it does not. [@murphy_ml] CV chooses $\lambda$ for **predictive accuracy**. Because the
lasso shrinks the relevant coefficients along with the irrelevant ones, CV avoids a large $\lambda$
that would shrink the real effects too much. The price is a less sparse model that lets in **false
positives**. Murphy cites the formal result (Meinshausen & Bühlmann, 2006): the prediction-optimal
$\lambda$ does **not** recover the true set of variables, even with infinite data. A method that
does recover it as $N\to\infty$ is called **model-selection consistent**, and plain CV-tuned lasso is
not one. [@murphy_ml] Prediction and selection are different goals, and one tuning knob cannot serve
both.

## Stability selection: count how often, not whether {#sec-stability}

The fix is simple to state. **Repeat the selection many times on random subsamples and record how
often each variable is picked.** This is **stability selection** (Meinshausen & Bühlmann, 2010).
[@jacobucci_ml; @murphy_ml] Instead of the usual *regularisation path* (each coefficient's value
as $\lambda$ changes), you look at a **stability path**: each variable's *probability of being
selected* under resampling. Then you keep the variables whose probability clears a threshold.
[@jacobucci_ml]

::: {.callout-note title="Jacobucci et al.'s bfi example"}
With 24 predictors of the agreeableness sum score, they ran the lasso on **1,000 subsamples**
(`stabs` package), each time stopping once it had selected **seven** variables. Allowing several
picks per run reduces the chance that one of two highly correlated predictors simply suppresses the
other. At a threshold of **0.9**, five variables survive: C2, C3, E3, E4 and E5. The single
CV-tuned lasso had picked those five **plus** C5, O2 and gender. [@jacobucci_ml]
:::

Two points the authors stress. The threshold is a **tuning parameter you choose**, on practical and
theoretical grounds; 0.9 is not a law. And stability selection usually gives a **sparser** model, which
helps when one lasso run selects too much. [@jacobucci_ml]

Murphy adds two useful readings. [@murphy_ml]

- **It approximates a Bayesian quantity.** The share of resamples that select variable $j$
  approximates its **posterior inclusion probability**, $p(\gamma_j = 1 \mid \mathcal D)$: the
  probability, given the data, that variable $j$ belongs in the model. Murphy calls the Bayesian
  version "much more robust" than a single lasso fit.
- **Bolasso** (Bach, 2008) runs the lasso at a fixed $\lambda$ on bootstrap samples and keeps a
  variable only if it appears in at least 90% of runs. Taking this near-intersection removes the
  false positives that the plain lasso lets in, and it is model-selection consistent under a wider
  range of conditions. Murphy replicates Bach's experiment (256 datasets, $N=1{,}000$, 16 variables
  of which 8 are relevant). The bolasso recovers the right set over a wide range of $\lambda$ where
  the lasso does not, and about **32 bootstraps** is a good compromise between speed and accuracy.
  [@murphy_ml]

Picking the predictor with the smallest permutation p-value (grey) gives each about its fair 1/5 share."

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# ---------- (a) stability selection vs one cross-validated lasso ----------
rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
n, p = 200, 20
blk = np.repeat(np.arange(4), 5)                 # four blocks of five correlated "items"
Z = rng.normal(size=(n, 4))
X = 0.6 * Z[:, blk] + 0.8 * rng.normal(size=(n, p))
beta = np.zeros(p); beta[[0, 1, 5, 10]] = [0.45, 0.35, 0.45, 0.35]   # x1, x2, x6, x11 matter
y = X @ beta + rng.normal(0, 1.0, n)

def lasso_path(X, y, lams, iters=200):
    """Coordinate descent for (1/2n)||y - Xb||^2 + lam ||b||_1, warm-started down a lambda grid."""
    n, p = X.shape; b = np.zeros(p); r = y.copy(); out = []
    for lam in lams:
        for _ in range(iters):
            step = 0.0
            for j in range(p):
                old = b[j]; rho = X[:, j] @ r / n + old
                new = np.sign(rho) * max(abs(rho) - lam, 0.0)
                if new != old:
                    r += X[:, j] * (old - new); b[j] = new; step = max(step, abs(new - old))
            if step < 1e-6: break
        out.append(b.copy())
    return np.array(out)

def prep(X, y):
    return (X - X.mean(0)) / X.std(0), y - y.mean()

def grid(Xs, yc):
    return np.max(np.abs(Xs.T @ yc)) / len(yc) * np.logspace(0, -2.5, 60)

# stability selection: 500 half-samples, lasso stopped once it has picked 7 variables
B = 500; picked = np.zeros(p)
for _ in range(B):
    idx = rng.choice(n, n // 2, replace=False)
    Xb, yb = prep(X[idx], y[idx])
    path = lasso_path(Xb, yb, grid(Xb, yb))
    picked += path[np.argmax((path != 0).sum(1) >= 7)] != 0
stab = picked / B

# one lasso, 10-fold CV + one-SE rule, re-run with 40 different fold assignments
Xs, yc = prep(X, y); lams = grid(Xs, yc); full = lasso_path(Xs, yc, lams)
single = np.zeros(p); sets = set()
for seed in range(40):

    err = np.zeros((10, len(lams)))
    for f in range(10):
        tr, te = folds != f, folds == f
        mu, sd, ym = X[tr].mean(0), X[tr].std(0), y[tr].mean()
        path = lasso_path((X[tr] - mu) / sd, y[tr] - ym, lams)
        err[f] = ((y[te][:, None] - ym - ((X[te] - mu) / sd) @ path.T) ** 2).mean(0)
    m, se = err.mean(0), err.std(0, ddof=1) / np.sqrt(10)
    k = np.min(np.where(m <= m.min() + se[np.argmin(m)])[0])     # sparsest model within 1 SE
    chosen = full[k] != 0; single += chosen; sets.add(tuple(np.flatnonzero(chosen)))
single /= 40

# ---------- (b) which variable does a greedy split pick when NOTHING matters? ----------
rb = np.random.default_rng(3)
def best_gain(x, y):
    o = np.argsort(x, kind="stable"); xs, cs = x[o], np.cumsum(y[o])
    N = len(y); k = np.arange(1, N); ok = xs[1:] != xs[:-1]
    g = cs[:-1]**2 / k + (cs[-1] - cs[:-1])**2 / (N - k) - cs[-1]**2 / N
    return g[ok].max() if ok.any() else 0.0

levels = [2, 4, 10, 50, None]                    # None = continuous (100 distinct values)
names = ["2 values", "4 values", "10 values", "50 values", "continuous"]
greedy = np.zeros(5); permsel = np.zeros(5); reps, nb, P = 500, 100, 99
for _ in range(reps):
    Xr = [rb.integers(0, L, nb).astype(float) if L else rb.normal(size=nb) for L in levels]
    yr = rb.normal(size=nb)                      # outcome unrelated to every predictor
    g = np.array([best_gain(x, yr) for x in Xr])
    greedy[np.argmax(g)] += 1
    pv = np.array([(1 + sum(best_gain(x, rb.permutation(yr)) >= g[v] for _ in range(P))) / (P + 1)
                   for v, x in enumerate(Xr)])
    permsel[rb.choice(np.flatnonzero(pv == pv.min()))] += 1

How to read panel (a). The single lasso is consistent in one way: it keeps $x_1$, $x_2$ and $x_6$
in every run. It is also consistently wrong in another: it keeps the **proxy** $x_3$ in 39 of 40 runs.
$x_3$ has no effect of its own, but it is correlated with two real predictors, and the
prediction-tuned $\lambda$ is small enough to let it in. This is Murphy's false-positive mechanism.
[@murphy_ml] Across half-samples, $x_3$ is selected only 73% of the time, so stability selection
drops it. The same run also shows the cost. The real but weaker $x_{11}$ reaches 0.86 and falls
just below a 0.9 threshold. Stability selection trades false positives for some risk of missing a
real variable, and where you put the threshold decides that trade. This is why Jacobucci et al. call
it a tuning choice. [@jacobucci_ml]

## What about p-values? {#sec-lasso-p}

Many reviewers in the social sciences expect p-values, and "the lasso selected it" can sound
unconvincing to them. Jacobucci et al. therefore describe the **covariance test** of Lockhart, Taylor,
Tibshirani and Tibshirani (2014). It computes p-values for lasso coefficients while accounting for the
fact that the lasso chose them adaptively. [@jacobucci_ml] It is built on **least angle regression
(LARS)**, which computes the whole lasso path. LARS resembles stepwise regression: it enters
predictors one at a time according to their correlation with the residuals. The difference is that
each coefficient grows only gradually, until another predictor becomes equally correlated with the
residual. [@jacobucci_ml]

On their ECLS-K example the test confirmed all three variables the one-SE lasso had chosen, and it
also gave gender $p < .001$. Gender would have been selected anyway if they had used the CV-minimum
$\lambda$ instead of the one-SE rule. [@jacobucci_ml] The authors' point is not the p-values
themselves. A variable's inclusion depended on which of two reasonable rules they applied, and that
is selection instability again. If what you actually want is a confidence interval for **one**
coefficient after the lasso has chosen the controls, that is a different and better-posed problem.
See the [double lasso](double-lasso.qmd).

## Selecting sets, not single variables {#sec-groups}

Social science predictors often come in **natural groups**: the five items of a personality facet, or
a set of demographic dummies. Sometimes the scientific question concerns the group, not the item.

**The theory-led version is hierarchical regression.** You enter predictor *blocks* in a sequence fixed
in advance and test each block's increase in $R^2$. This is not the same as hierarchical (multilevel)
*models*, which are about random effects. [@jacobucci_ml] Jacobucci et al. ran it on 150 `bfi`
respondents, entering demographics, then conscientiousness, then openness, then neuroticism items:

| Block added | $\Delta R^2$ | $p$ |
|---|---|---|
| Demographics (4) | .07 | .016 |
| Conscientiousness (5) | .06 | .070 |
| Openness (5) | .09 | .008 |
| Neuroticism (5) | .06 | .076 |

: Hierarchical regression on 150 `bfi` respondents (outcome: agreeableness). [@jacobucci_ml]

You could call Model 1 or Model 3 "best", but only through an arbitrary $p < .05$ cutoff, because the
four $\Delta R^2$ values are about the same size. [@jacobucci_ml] (The same blockwise logic appears in
[multiple regression](../03-regression/multiple-regression.qmd) as sequential entry.)

**The data-led version is the group lasso** (Yuan & Lin). It penalises each group's coefficient vector
as a whole, so an entire group is either kept or set to zero together. [@jacobucci_ml; @murphy_ml]
Murphy explains why this works. The penalty is the **length** of each group's coefficient vector,
not the squared length. Squared lengths would add up to an ordinary ridge penalty. With the
unsquared length, the only way to make the penalty small is to shrink every element of the group
toward zero together. [@murphy_ml] Larger groups usually receive a proportionally larger penalty
(scaled by $\sqrt{\text{group size}}$). Murphy's examples come straight from everyday modelling: a
categorical variable expanded into dummies, where you want all the dummies in or all out, and
multinomial models with one coefficient per class. [@murphy_ml]

The group lasso gives **no sparsity within a group**. To also select items within the kept groups,
use a **bi-level** method such as the sparse group lasso, which adds an ordinary lasso penalty, or
the composite MCP. [@jacobucci_ml] On the same 150 respondents the two methods tell different
stories:

| Set | Group lasso keeps? | Bi-level (composite MCP) items kept |
|---|---|---|
| Demographics | Yes | 2 |
| Conscientiousness | Yes | 1 |
| Openness | Yes | 3 |
| Neuroticism | No | 2 |

: Group versus bi-level selection on the `bfi` subset. [@jacobucci_ml]

The group lasso keeps conscientiousness as a block. The bi-level method keeps only one of its items,
C2 ("continue until everything is perfect"). That item had the highest correlation with
agreeableness (.26) and with the other conscientiousness items, and it was the only one significant
in the plain regression. Correlation among items had hidden which one carried the signal.
[@jacobucci_ml]

## Searching for interactions without drowning {#sec-hierarchy}

With $p$ predictors there are about $p^2/2$ possible two-way interactions, far too many to type in by
hand. Jacobucci et al. describe the **hierarchical lasso** (Bien, Taylor & Tibshirani, 2013). It
penalises main effects and interactions together, under a **hierarchy** rule: [@jacobucci_ml]

- **Strong hierarchy.** An interaction $X_jX_k$ may enter only if *both* main effects are non-zero.
- **Weak hierarchy.** An interaction needs only *one* of its main effects to be non-zero.

They argue for strong hierarchy in the social sciences. An interaction is a product of two variables,
and when either main effect is small, which is the usual case in social science, the interaction is
unlikely to be large. [@jacobucci_ml]

::: {.callout-note title="ECLS-K science scores: select on one half, estimate on the other"}
With $N = 4{,}556$, they ran the hierarchical lasso (strong hierarchy, CV, one-SE rule) on half the
data. It selected main effects of math, general knowledge, income and gender; squared terms for math,
knowledge, income and BMI; and two interactions, math × knowledge and knowledge × income. Weak
hierarchy gave the same result. They then fitted an ordinary regression with only those terms on the
**other** half ($n = 2{,}278$, $R^2 = .403$). With everything standardised, math is .376 and math ×
knowledge is −.098. As knowledge rises one SD above its mean, the effects of math and income both
shrink. [@jacobucci_ml]
:::

Two lessons come from this. First, **selecting on one half and estimating on the other** keeps the
reported coefficients from being chosen and tested on the same data, the same honesty that a
[held-out test set](cross-validation.qmd) brings to prediction. Second, the authors warn that the
"interaction" may partly be **nonlinearity in disguise**. Cognitive scores often have diminishing
returns at the high end, which is exactly why they allowed squared terms. Searching for interactions
while ignoring curvature is a known trap. [@jacobucci_ml] (See the
[functional-form](../03-regression/functional-form.qmd) entry for how the two get confused.) A
practical warning as well: the hierarchical lasso is slow. Fitting even ten penalty values can take
hours, and the authors report runs that never finished. [@jacobucci_ml]

### The Bayesian route, briefly

Each penalty corresponds to a prior. Ridge corresponds to a normal prior on the coefficients and the
lasso to a Laplace prior; the lasso estimate is the **mode** of the resulting posterior.
[@jacobucci_ml; @murphy_ml] Murphy adds a subtlety. Under a Laplace prior the posterior mean, the
posterior median and the posterior draws are **not sparse**. Only the mode is, so a "Bayesian lasso"
will not set anything exactly to zero. [@murphy_ml] For selection, Jacobucci et al. recommend
sparsity-inducing hierarchical priors such as the **horseshoe** or spike-and-slab. One model run
then learns how much shrinkage to apply, instead of a grid of 100 $\lambda$ values. On 150 ECLS-K
cases with 20 added noise variables, only math and knowledge had posteriors clearly away from zero.
Selecting by 95% credible intervals was less clear-cut, because some noise coefficients had
intervals excluding zero. [@jacobucci_ml] They also caution that selection depends heavily on
sample size: larger samples tend to select more variables. [@jacobucci_ml] See
[hierarchical Bayesian models](../02-inference/hierarchical-bayesian-models.qmd) for the machinery.

## Single trees: the same problem, drawn as a flowchart {#sec-tree-stability}

The main criticism of decision trees is that they are **unstable** (Breiman, 1996): a small change in
the data can produce a very different tree. [@jacobucci_ml] Murphy gives the mechanism. A tree is
grown top-down, so an arbitrary choice at the root changes everything below it. In frequentist terms,
trees are **high-variance** estimators. [@murphy_ml] The
[trees-and-forests](trees-and-forests.qmd) entry deals with this by averaging many trees. This entry
asks the harder question: what if you need **one** tree to interpret?

::: {.callout-note title="ECLS-K: 100 bootstrap trees, 100 different trees"}
Predicting eighth-grade science from math and general knowledge, Jacobucci et al. compared linear
regression with two tree algorithms over 100 bootstrap samples, scoring each by 10-fold CV.
[@jacobucci_ml]

| | Avg. terminal nodes (SD) | CV RMSE | CV $R^2$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Linear regression | — | 11.75 | .37 |
| CART (`rpart`) | 15.42 (4.4) | 11.51 | .39 |
| Conditional inference tree (`ctree`) | 22.67 (9.4) | 11.48 | .40 |

Both tree methods predicted slightly better than the line, which is evidence that the relationship
really is nonlinear. Yet **every one of the bootstrap trees was a different structure**, even after
the cutpoints were rounded to one decimal place. [@jacobucci_ml]
:::

The tree had found real structure, but it did not find one *particular* structure. Jacobucci et al.
are careful about what this implies. Instability is not a problem for describing the sample at hand.
It is a problem for the claim that **the same tree would appear in another sample**. A single tree
should be read as one of several structures that fit about equally well. [@jacobucci_ml] Their
practical advice: [@jacobucci_ml]

1. **Round predictors to the precision you actually care about.** If a split between 7.345 and 7.346
   means nothing in your field, round. Fewer possible cutpoints give more stable trees and faster runs.
2. **Bootstrap the structure, not just the error.** Refit on resamples and report which variables and
   cutpoints keep recurring (the authors' `dtree` package has a `stable()` function for this).
3. **Check against an ensemble.** Fit a [random forest or boosting](trees-and-forests.qmd) alongside
   the tree, and check whether the single tree's variables are also the ones the ensemble keeps using.

## Conditional inference trees: a test before every split {#sec-ctree}

CART has a second, quieter bias. To split on an ordered predictor with $m$ distinct values it tries
$m-1$ cutpoints. For an unordered category with $m$ levels it tries every two-way grouping, which is
$2^{m-1}-1$ splits. [@jacobucci_ml] A predictor with more possible cutpoints gets **more chances** to
produce a large fit improvement by luck. Panel (b) of the figure shows the result. When nothing
matters, the greedy rule picks the continuous noise variable 44% of the time and the binary one 3%.

**Conditional inference trees** (`ctree`; Hothorn, Hornik & Zeileis, 2006) fix this with a
**permutation test** at every node. [@jacobucci_ml] The test asks how large the association between
a predictor and the outcome is compared with what it would be if the outcome values were randomly
reshuffled. Because each predictor is judged against its *own* null distribution, the number of
cutpoints is accounted for. This is **unbiased variable selection**, and it gives the flat grey bars
in panel (b). The test also tells the tree when to stop: if no predictor reaches $p < .05$ (the
default), the node is not split. This guards against splits on noise while the tree is grown, so
no pruning is needed afterwards. [@jacobucci_ml]

In practice ctree tends to grow **large** trees (see its 22.7 average nodes above). The authors'
**CTreePrune** grows a deliberately oversized tree with a very lenient criterion. It then works from
the bottom up, re-judging each split with a **false-discovery-rate** correction (Benjamini–Hochberg)
from [multiple testing](../meta-research/multiple-testing.qmd). [@jacobucci_ml]

CART's own remedy is **cost-complexity pruning**, with the tree size chosen by CV. Jacobucci et al.'s
tables show how much judgement is left over. On their suicidal-ideation example, CV error fell from
1.00 to 0.83 with the first split and then stayed between 0.82 and 0.84. On ECLS-K reading scores,
the lowest CV error came at three splits, but the error bars of neighbouring sizes overlapped, and
the one-SE rule chose a tree with a **single** split. [@jacobucci_ml; @murphy_ml]

## Reading a single tree honestly {#sec-reading}

**Most splits are interactions.** In a tree, a pure main effect means the *same* split appears with
the same gain in every branch. Jacobucci et al. show a pure main-effects tree in which gender is split
first and then depression is split identically within each gender, adding 4 points each time. With
continuous predictors, an exact repeated cutpoint almost never happens in real data, so, following
Berk, **most splits in a real tree represent interactions**. [@jacobucci_ml] This is a strength
(trees find interactions you never specified) and also a reason for caution: a tree cannot simply
show a main effect.

**Different routes, same destination.** Trees can show **equifinality**: different combinations of
predictor values leading to similar outcomes. They can also show **multifinality**: similar values
leading to different outcomes. [@jacobucci_ml] In a ctree of ECLS-K science scores, one leaf held
children with lower knowledge (< 25.7) but higher math (> 28.63), and another held children with
higher knowledge (up to 35.5). The two leaves had very similar science scores, consistent with
strong math compensating for weaker knowledge. A third leaf's scores ranged from about 40 to above
100: similar predictor values, very different outcomes. [@jacobucci_ml] Two cautions apply. The
pathways are only as trustworthy as the tree is [stable](#sec-tree-stability), and **a split says
nothing about effect size**. This four-leaf tree explained 37% of the variance. [@jacobucci_ml]

**Missing data: surrogate splits.** When a case is missing the variable a node splits on (say,
education ≤ 12 years vs more), CART uses a **surrogate**. It treats the primary split as an outcome
to be predicted, finds the other variables that best reproduce it (`rpart` keeps five), and routes
the case with those. [@jacobucci_ml] Murphy's verdict is mixed. Surrogates amount to learning a
local model of how the predictors relate to each other, which is useful, but the method is
"entirely ad hoc". For a categorical predictor, the simpler alternative is to code "missing" as a
category of its own. [@murphy_ml] Algorithms without surrogates fall back on
[multiple imputation](../02-inference/multiple-imputation.qmd). That gives $K$ trees instead of one,
but those $K$ trees double as another stability check. [@jacobucci_ml]

**Variable importance and the near-duplicate.** Collinear predictors compete for the same split, and
only one can win it. Jacobucci et al. added a noisy copy of math ($r = .95$) and refit. The copy never
appeared in the tree, but `rpart`'s importance scores, which also credit a variable for the splits
it would have made as a surrogate, were math 39, knowledge 33 and the copy 28. [@jacobucci_ml] So a
variable *missing from the tree* can still be nearly as important as one in it. Treat only the
**ranking** of importances as meaningful, not the exact numbers (Strobl, Malley & Tutz, 2009).
[@jacobucci_ml]

## Common pitfalls

::: {.callout-warning title="Where this goes wrong"}

- **Reporting one run's selected set as "the predictors".** A lasso or stepwise shortlist from one
  sample and one CV seed is a single draw from a distribution of shortlists. Re-run it on resamples
  and report the selection frequencies. [@jacobucci_ml; @murphy_ml]

- **Assuming CV-tuned $\lambda$ finds the true variables.** CV tunes for prediction, and the
  prediction-optimal lasso lets in correlated proxies. It is not model-selection consistent.
  [@murphy_ml]

- **Treating the stability threshold as objective.** 0.9 is a dial. Higher values give fewer false
  positives and more missed real effects. State it and justify it. [@jacobucci_ml]

- **Selecting and estimating on the same data.** Coefficients refit on the rows that chose them are
  over-optimistic. Use a held-out half (as in the ECLS-K interaction search) or a
  [post-selection method](double-lasso.qmd). [@jacobucci_ml]

- **Calling a tree's pathway a mechanism.** A single tree is one of many near-equivalent structures,
  and its splits are mostly interactions with no effect sizes attached. Bootstrap it, compare it with
  an ensemble, and keep the explanatory language modest. [@jacobucci_ml]

- **Trusting CART's choice of split variable when predictors differ in granularity.** Many-valued
  predictors win splits by chance. Use a conditional inference tree or round the predictors.
  [@jacobucci_ml]

- **Reading "absent from the tree" as "unimportant".** A collinear twin can lose every split and still
  carry almost as much information. Check importances and surrogates. [@jacobucci_ml]

:::

## Appendix · the mathematics {.unnumbered}

::: {.math-appendix}

**Stability selection.** Let $\hat S_\lambda(I)$ be the set of variables the lasso selects on the
subsample $I$. Draw subsamples $I_1,\dots,I_B$ (typically half the data each) and compute each
variable's selection frequency
$$
\hat\Pi_j \;=\; \frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\mathbf 1\!\left\{\,j\in \hat S_\lambda(I_b)\right\},
\qquad
\hat S_{\text{stable}} \;=\; \{\,j : \hat\Pi_j \ge \pi_{\text{thr}}\,\}.
$$
In Murphy's reading, $\hat\Pi_j$ approximates the posterior inclusion probability
$p(\gamma_j=1\mid\mathcal D)$. The bolasso is the case of bootstrap resamples at a fixed $\lambda$ with
$\pi_{\text{thr}}=0.9$. [@murphy_ml] In the figure we follow Jacobucci et al.'s variant: each run moves
down a $\lambda$ grid until a fixed number of variables (7) have entered. [@jacobucci_ml]

**Model-selection consistency.** An estimator $\hat S_N$ of the true support $S^*=\{j:w^*_j\neq0\}$ is
model-selection consistent if $P(\hat S_N = S^*)\to 1$ as $N\to\infty$. The CV-optimal lasso is not.
[@murphy_ml]

**Group lasso.** Partition the coefficients into groups $g=1,\dots,G$ of sizes $p_g$:
$$
\hat\beta^{\text{grp}} = \arg\min_\beta \;\sum_{i=1}^N\Big(y_i-\beta_0-\sum_j x_{ij}\beta_j\Big)^2
+ \lambda\sum_{g=1}^{G}\sqrt{p_g}\,\lVert\beta_g\rVert_2 .
$$
Squaring the group norms gives $\sum_g\lVert\beta_g\rVert_2^2=\lVert\beta\rVert_2^2$, which is ridge.
Leaving them unsquared penalises the *radius* of each group's vector, which can be small only if every
element is small. The result is all-or-nothing sparsity by group. [@murphy_ml] The **sparse group
lasso** adds $\lambda_2\lVert\beta\rVert_1$ to obtain sparsity within groups as well. [@jacobucci_ml]
In Bayesian terms, the group lasso is the MAP estimate under a prior proportional to
$\exp\!\big(-\tfrac{\gamma}{\sigma}\sum_g\lVert w_g\rVert_2\big)$. [@murphy_ml]

**Interactions under hierarchy.** The all-pairs model is
$$
Y=\beta_0+\sum_j\beta_jX_j+\tfrac12\sum_{j,k}\Theta_{jk}X_jX_k+\epsilon ,
$$
with $\Theta$ symmetric. Strong hierarchy imposes $\Theta_{jk}\neq0\Rightarrow\beta_j\neq0$ **and**
$\beta_k\neq0$. Weak hierarchy imposes $\Theta_{jk}\neq0\Rightarrow\beta_j\neq0$ **or** $\beta_k\neq0$.
The default in `hierNet` also lets the diagonal (squared terms) in. [@jacobucci_ml]

**Hierarchical regression arithmetic.** With nested models $M_0\subset\dots\subset M_K$,
$R^2(M_K)=\sum_k\Delta R^2(M_k)$, where $\Delta R^2(M_k)=(\text{RSS}_{k-1}-\text{RSS}_k)/\text{RSS}_0$.
The $F$ test of block $k$ uses the **largest** model's residual mean square,
$F_k=\big[(\text{RSS}_{k-1}-\text{RSS}_k)/\Delta df_k\big]\big/\big[\text{RSS}_K/df_K\big]$. Using
Jacobucci et al.'s residual sums of squares (2190.83, 2036.00, 1908.60, 1708.12, 1583.23 on 149, 145,
140, 135, 130 df), this reproduces their $F$ = 3.18, 2.09, 3.29, 2.05 and total $R^2 = .277$. Dividing
by each step's *own* residual mean square instead would give 2.76 for the first block. [@jacobucci_ml]

**Cutpoint counts and pruning.** An ordered predictor with $m$ distinct values offers $m-1$ binary
splits. An unordered one offers $2^{m-1}-1$. CART's cost-complexity criterion is
$R_\alpha(T)=R(T)+\alpha\,|T|$, with $|T|$ the number of leaves. Increasing $\alpha$ traces a nested
sequence of subtrees, and CV with the one-SE rule chooses among them. [@jacobucci_ml; @murphy_ml]

**The permutation p-value in panel (b).** For predictor $v$, let $G_v$ be its best-split reduction in
the sum of squares. Recompute it on $P=99$ random permutations of $y$, which gives $G^{*}_{v,1},\dots,G^{*}_{v,P}$,
and set $\hat p_v=\big(1+\#\{G^{*}_{v,r}\ge G_v\}\big)/(P+1)$. Each predictor is compared with its own
null distribution, so a predictor with many cutpoints gains nothing from them. This is the idea
behind conditional inference trees. (`ctree` itself uses a family of linear permutation statistics;
our max-split version is a simplified stand-in.)
