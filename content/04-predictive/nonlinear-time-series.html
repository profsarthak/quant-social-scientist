Nonlinear Time Series: Thresholds, Smooth Transitions & Regime Switching

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source

Unemployment rises like a rocket and falls like a feather. A linear model cannot say that, so here are the models that can.

Every model in this sub-track so far has been linear: double the shock and you double the response, flip its sign and you flip the path. Many social and economic series do not behave like that. Unemployment rises sharply and falls slowly. The Fed reacts harder when inflation is high. Small price gaps survive while large ones get arbitraged away. Nonlinear time-series models let the dynamics depend on where the series is: above or below a threshold (TAR), sliding between regimes along a smooth transition (STAR), or jumping between hidden states with fixed odds (Markov switching). The cost is honesty about the fit. Thresholds have to be searched for, the usual tests stop working, and a wrong nonlinear model can be worse than none.

Why it matters

Enders opens the chapter with a road atlas (Enders 2014). A flat map is a linear approximation to a curved earth. It is perfect for driving to the next town and disastrous for planning a rocket launch. Linear ARIMA and VAR models are the road atlas of time series. They are usually good enough, but policy makers “could make a serious error” if they ignore the evidence that unemployment increases more sharply than it decreases (Enders 2014). Asymmetry like this is everywhere once you look:

  • Gasoline prices go up faster than they come down (Enders 2014).
  • Transport costs stop anyone arbitraging a small gap between cotton prices in Alabama and Mississippi, while large gaps disappear almost at once (Enders 2014).
  • Interest-rate spreads may sit below their long-run value for much longer than they sit above it (Enders 2014).

For social science these are substantive claims about behaviour: thresholds of action, reluctance to adjust, and policy that responds differently in good times and bad. A linear model averages the two behaviours into one coefficient and hides exactly the thing you wanted to know.

What “linear” actually means

Take the AR(1) y_t = a_1 y_{t-1} with a_1 = 0.5. Start at y_{t-1}=1 and the expected path is 1, 0.5, 0.25, \dots. Start at 2 and every value doubles. Start at -1 and the path is the mirror image. Linearity means scaling the starting point by \lambda scales the whole path by \lambda (Enders 2014). Sign and size of a deviation never change how the series gets back.

A nonlinear process breaks this. Enders gives two pictures (Enders 2014):

  • A kink. The series follows y_t=a_1y_{t-1} above zero and y_t=a_2y_{t-1} below, with a_2>a_1. Negative deviations then die out more slowly than positive ones.
  • Gravity. An object falling toward the earth speeds up as it gets closer, so halving the distance does not halve the next step.

Once you leave linearity there are endless possible shapes. That is the main warning of the whole chapter: “adopting an incorrect nonlinear specification may be more problematic than simply ignoring the nonlinearity altogether” (Enders 2014). The rest of this entry is about choosing the shape carefully.

The simplest extensions of ARMA

Before thresholds, Enders lists two direct generalisations (Enders 2014):

  • GAR (generalised autoregressive) models add powers and cross-products of lagged values, such as y_{t-1}^2 or y_{t-1}y_{t-2}. They are a Taylor-series approximation to an unknown nonlinear function, estimated by OLS and pared down by AIC or SBC.
  • Bilinear models add products of lagged values and lagged shocks, such as y_{t-1}\varepsilon_{t-1}. The persistence then depends on the latest surprise. They are estimated by maximum likelihood.

Rothman’s (1998) study of detrended US unemployment (1948Q1–1979Q4) fits AR, GAR, bilinear and TAR models to the same data. The nonlinear models cut the residual variance only modestly: the variance ratios relative to the AR are 0.965, 0.936 and 0.942. By AIC the ranking is bilinear, AR, GAR, then TAR (Enders 2014). Nonlinearity is real, but a better fit inside the sample is not a large prize.

Is there anything to model? Testing for nonlinearity

The ordinary autocorrelation function can miss nonlinear dependence completely. The process y_t=\varepsilon^2_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t has zero autocorrelations, even though y_t plainly depends on the past (Enders 2014). Enders’ chaotic example y_t=4y_{t-1}(1-y_{t-1}) is fully deterministic. Its first six autocorrelations are all within ±0.08, yet the correlation of y^2_t with y^2_{t-1} is −0.281 (Enders 2014). So you test the residuals of a good linear model in ways that can see curvature:

Test What it does Strength / weakness
McLeod–Li Ljung–Box Q on the squared residuals, or equivalently TR^2 from regressing \hat e^2_t on its own lags the exact LM test for ARCH errors; also sees other nonlinearity (Enders 2014)
RESET adds powers \hat y^2,\hat y^3,\dots of the fitted values, then an F-test very general alternative, but “little power to detect asymmetric models” such as TAR (Enders 2014)
BDS portmanteau test of independence built from distances between pairs of residuals responds to many kinds of misspecification; bootstrap the critical values in small samples (Enders 2014)
LM tests regress residuals on the derivatives of a specific nonlinear model (GAR, bilinear, STAR), evaluated under linearity aimed at a named alternative, so more power against it (Enders 2014)

The US unemployment rate shows why you run more than one. Enders fits a linear AR to monthly changes (January 1960–June 2013, 642 observations, mean 6.10%) (Enders 2014). The RESET finds nothing (F=1.42, p=0.234). The McLeod–Li regression of e^2_t on two lags gives F=10.95, which is highly significant. Regressing e_t on e^2_{t-1} gives a positive coefficient (0.3298, t=2.30): a large error of either sign last month is followed by a positive error this month (Enders 2014). In a symmetric world that cannot happen.

Threshold autoregression (TAR)

The workhorse is the two-regime TAR. Pick a threshold variable, usually a lag y_{t-d} of the series itself, and let the AR coefficients switch when it crosses a value \tau (Enders 2014): y_t = I_t\Big[\alpha_{10}+\sum_i\alpha_{1i}y_{t-i}\Big] + (1-I_t)\Big[\alpha_{20}+\sum_i\alpha_{2i}y_{t-i}\Big]+\varepsilon_t, \qquad I_t=\mathbf 1\{y_{t-d}>\tau\}.

If you know \tau and the delay d, the model is two OLS regressions on two parts of the sample. Usually you know neither, and three practical questions follow.

Finding the threshold: Chan’s grid search. Chan (1993) showed that the \tau minimising the sum of squared residuals is a super-consistent estimate (Enders 2014). So you sort the candidate values, fit the TAR at each one, and keep the minimum. To make sure each regime has enough data, “the highest and lowest 15% of the values are excluded from the search” (10% in very large samples). With 200 observations that leaves about 141 candidate thresholds (Enders 2014). Plotting SSR(\tau) is the diagnostic. One clear trough suggests one threshold; several troughs suggest several regimes.

Choosing the delay. Repeat the search for each candidate d and keep the lowest SSR, or compare by AIC or SBC (Enders 2014).

Testing it: the Davies problem. This is the subtle part. Under the null of linearity the threshold does not exist, so \tau is not identified under the null (Enders 2014). The usual F- or likelihood-ratio statistic then does not have its textbook distribution: you searched over \tau for the best fit, so a linear series will always produce some threshold that looks good. Hansen’s (1997) fix is a bootstrap. Generate many fake samples under the null, rerun the whole threshold search on each, and compare your F with the 95th percentile of the bootstrapped F^* values (Enders 2014).

Worked example: asymmetric unemployment

Back to monthly US unemployment. With d=1 the SSR is minimised at \tau=0.070: the regime switches when last month’s change exceeds 0.07 percentage points (Enders 2014). Delays of 1, 2 and 3 give SSRs of 14.296, 14.319 and 14.385, so d=1 wins. The naive F for linearity is [(14.883-14.296)/6]/[14.296/617]=4.22, but because \tau was searched for it cannot be read from an F table. Hansen’s bootstrap rejects linearity at the 0.0025 level (Enders 2014).

The two criteria disagree on the full threshold model: AIC prefers it, SBC prefers the linear one, and many threshold coefficients are small. Enders pares it down and finds that persistence is far higher in the rising regime (lag coefficients 0.387, 0.376 and 0.130) than in the falling one (0.155 and 0.188). Increases in unemployment are far more persistent than decreases (Enders 2014). That is the “rocket and feather” in numbers.

Worked example: an asymmetric Taylor rule

The Taylor rule has the Fed setting the federal funds rate i_t from inflation \pi_t, the output gap y_t and last quarter’s rate. Bunzel and Enders (2010) estimate it for 1979Q4–2007Q3. The linear fit is i_t=-0.269+0.464\pi_t+0.345y_t+0.810i_{t-1}, a long-run inflation response of 0.464/(1-0.810)=2.44 (Enders 2014). They then let it be a threshold regression, trying four threshold variables with 15% trimming. All four fit better than the linear model and all are highly significant by bootstrap. Inflation two quarters back, at \tau=3.527\%, fits best (Enders 2014):

Regime Constant Inflation \pi_t Output gap y_t Smoothing i_{t-1}
High inflation (\pi_{t-2}\ge3.527) 1.383 1.055 0.472 0.374
Low inflation (\pi_{t-2}<3.527) −0.440 0.227 0.305 0.967
Linear (one rule for all) −0.269 0.464 0.345 0.810

When inflation is high the Fed reacts hard and smooths little; when it is low the Fed reacts weakly and mostly holds its rate. The linear rule “seems to ‘average’ the responses” across the two regimes (Enders 2014). It describes a Fed that never existed.

Variations

  • Band-TAR (Balke and Fomby 1997). Three regimes: a random walk inside a band \bar s\pm c, and mean reversion outside it. This is the shape of a transaction-cost story, where small gaps are not worth closing (Enders 2014).
  • Threshold regression. The threshold switches the slope on some other regressor x_t, as in the Taylor rule (Enders 2014).
  • Multiple thresholds. Boetel, Hoffman and Liu (2007) find three regimes in how pork producers adjust their breeding stock, with thresholds estimated one after the other (Enders 2014).

Smooth transitions: LSTAR and ESTAR

A TAR switches regimes like a light switch. Often a dimmer is more realistic: behaviour shifts gradually as the state variable moves. The smooth transition autoregression (STAR) blends two AR models with a weight \theta\in[0,1] that depends on y_{t-1} (Enders 2014):

  • LSTAR (logistic). \theta=[1+\exp(-\gamma(y_{t-1}-c))]^{-1} is an S-curve. Low values of the series get one set of dynamics and high values the other. As the smoothness parameter \gamma grows, the S sharpens until the model is a TAR. As \gamma\to0 it collapses back to a linear AR (Enders 2014).
  • ESTAR (exponential). \theta=1-\exp[-\gamma(y_{t-1}-c)^2] is a U-shape, symmetric about c. Only the size of the deviation matters, not its sign (Enders 2014).

ESTAR is the natural model for purchasing power parity with transaction costs. Michael, Nobay and Peel (1997) argue that small deviations from PPP are not worth arbitraging, while large ones are corrected quickly (Enders 2014). Their estimate for the monthly US–UK real exchange rate has \gamma=532.4 and c=0.038. Near 0.038 the speed-of-adjustment coefficient is zero, so there is no pull back at all. Far from it the coefficient on y_{t-1} approaches -1, so adjustment is fast. “The adjustment of the real exchange rate is consistent with the presence of transaction costs” (Enders 2014). A linear model fitted to such a series sees a lot of time spent drifting and may call the whole thing a random walk.

Choosing between them (Teräsvirta 1994). You cannot test for STAR directly, because \gamma and c are unidentified under linearity: the Davies problem again. Instead, replace \theta by a Taylor expansion and run an auxiliary regression of the residuals on the regressors multiplied by y_{t-d}, y^2_{t-d} and y^3_{t-d} (Enders 2014). If the cubic terms matter, choose LSTAR; otherwise choose ESTAR, whose expansion is quadratic. On Enders’ simulated LSTAR series (250 observations) the AR(1) residuals look like white noise (Q p-values 0.900, 0.347, 0.471), yet the RESET gives F=95.60 against a 1% critical value of 3.86, and the cubic terms are what matter, so the test correctly selects LSTAR (Enders 2014). Nonlinear least squares then recovers parameters close to the truth, such as a threshold of 5.01 against the true 5 (Enders 2014).

Markov switching: regimes you cannot see

In TAR and STAR models the regime is set by an observable variable. Hamilton’s (1989) Markov switching model “posits that regime switches are exogenous” (Enders 2014). A hidden state follows a two-state Markov chain. The chain stays in regime 1 with probability p_{11} and in regime 2 with probability p_{22}, and each regime has its own AR process. “No attempt is made to explain the reason that regime changes occur”, only to estimate the transition probabilities along with everything else (Enders 2014). The long-run share of time in regime 1 is p_1=(1-p_{22})/(2-p_{11}-p_{22}). With p_{11}=0.75 and p_{22}=0.5 the system spends two-thirds of its time in regime 1 (Enders 2014). In practice it is “very difficult” to tell a Markov-switching model from a STAR model statistically (Enders 2014).

Worked example: crisis volatility. Edwards and Susmel (2000) study weekly interest rates in emerging markets. For Brazil (April 1994–April 1999) a standard GARCH(1,1) gives variance coefficients summing to more than one (1.321+0.395), so it predicts explosive volatility (Enders 2014). Their SWARCH alternative puts an ARCH model inside each of three hidden volatility states. Relative to the calm state, the middle state is 4.851 times as volatile and the crisis state 128.51 times. The chance of leaving the crisis state is high, so extreme volatility is short-lived (Enders 2014). GARCH read occasional storms as permanent persistence; the switching model reads them as rare, brief visits to a different world. This links directly to ARCH/GARCH and to the hidden-state logic of state-space models.

Enders also covers artificial neural networks in this chapter. They are a sum of logistic “nodes” that can approximate any nonlinear function (Kuan and White 1994), but their parameters are not globally identified once there is more than one node (Enders 2014). They sit at the predictive end of the spectrum and are covered in the ML track’s neural networks entry.

Shocks that depend on history

The figure below shows the most important practical consequence. In a linear model an impulse response is a fixed object: a one-unit shock has effect 1,\rho,\rho^2,\dots, a two-unit shock has twice that, and a negative shock is the mirror image. In a nonlinear model impulse responses are history-dependent (Enders 2014). Their sign matters, their size matters (a big shock is more likely to push the series across the threshold), and so do the shocks that arrive later. You have to say which history and which shock, then average over future shocks by simulation. That is the generalised impulse response of Koop, Pesaran and Potter (1996) (Enders 2014).

Potter’s (1995) model of US GNP growth (1947Q1–1990Q4) shows what is at stake. With a threshold at zero growth two quarters back, the contractionary regime has only 37 of the 170 observations, larger shocks (variance 1.50 vs 0.763), and an AR(2) coefficient of −0.946, which produces sharp reversals out of recessions (Enders 2014). One-step-ahead forecasts from 1960Q1 correlate 0.35 with actual growth for the TAR against 0.23 for the linear AR (Enders 2014). From the boom of 1983–84 even a −2% shock leaves growth positive, so the responses look linear. From the mild downturn of 1970 negative shocks push growth across the threshold, and the responses become asymmetric and no longer proportional to the size of the shock (Enders 2014).

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)

# --- Left: Chan (1993) grid search on a simulated TAR -------------------------
T = 200
y = np.zeros(T + 1)
eps = rng.normal(0, 1, T + 1)
for t in range(1, T + 1):
    a = 0.3 if y[t-1] > 0 else 0.7
    y[t] = a * y[t-1] + eps[t]
y = y[1:]
ylag, ycur = y[:-1], y[1:]

cands = np.sort(ylag)
lo, hi = int(0.15 * cands.size), int(0.85 * cands.size)
taus = cands[lo:hi]
ssr = []
for tau in taus:
    I = ylag > tau
    X = np.column_stack([I * ylag, (~I) * ylag])
    b, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(X, ycur, rcond=None)
    ssr.append(np.sum((ycur - X @ b) ** 2))
ssr = np.array(ssr)
tau_hat = taus[ssr.argmin()]

# --- Right: generalised impulse responses (Koop–Pesaran–Potter) -----------------
H, R = 12, 2000
def path(y0, shock, future):
    out = np.empty(H + 1)
    prev = y0
    for h in range(H + 1):
        a = 0.9 if prev > 0 else 0.1
        out[h] = a * prev + (shock if h == 0 else 0.0) + future[h]
        prev = out[h]
    return out

girf_pos = np.zeros(H + 1); girf_neg = np.zeros(H + 1)
for _ in range(R):
    future = rng.normal(0, 1, H + 1)
    base = path(0.0, 0.0, future)
    girf_pos += path(0.0, 1.0, future) - base

girf_pos /= R; girf_neg /= R

Unit roots when adjustment is asymmetric

The Dickey–Fuller test assumes linear adjustment toward the long-run value. If the true adjustment is asymmetric, the test is misspecified and has low power, so it may fail to detect a perfectly good long-run attractor (Enders 2014). Enders and Granger (1998) generalise it. Let the speed of adjustment differ above and below the attractor \tau: \Delta y_t = I_t\rho_1(y_{t-1}-\tau)+(1-I_t)\rho_2(y_{t-1}-\tau)+\varepsilon_t . If I_t depends on the level y_{t-1}\ge\tau, this is a TAR. If it depends on the direction \Delta y_{t-1}>0, it is a momentum TAR (M-TAR), where the series adjusts differently when rising than when falling (Enders 2014). The test of \rho_1=\rho_2=0 (no attractor) needs special critical values, as Dickey–Fuller does. Only once you reject it can you test symmetry, \rho_1=\rho_2 (Enders 2014).

On the spread between the 10-year Treasury rate and the federal funds rate (1958Q1–1994Q1), the RESET flags neglected nonlinearity (p=0.0016 and 0.00009). Both AIC and SBC prefer the M-TAR. Its attractor test statistic of 11.44 rejects “no attractor”, and symmetry is rejected (F=12.24, p=0.0006) (Enders 2014). The point estimates put the equilibrium spread at 1.64. When the spread is rising it adjusts quickly (−0.299); when it is falling, adjustment is “almost nonexistent” (−0.007). The linear model reports one speed for both. Its intercept is insignificant (t=1.52), which suggests an equilibrium spread of zero (Enders 2014). This extends naturally to a nonlinear error-correction model for cointegrated series.

Thresholds and structural breaks are cousins

A structural break is a threshold in time: the regime switches when t passes some date rather than when y_{t-d} passes some value. Enders makes the connection explicit: “in a model with structural breaks, time is the threshold variable” (Enders 2014). His simulated break series (Y_BREAKS) can be estimated as a threshold model in t. The search finds the true break at t=100, and the linearity F of 29.57 dwarfs the bootstrap 95% value of about 3.15 (Enders 2014). Carrasco (2002) shows that dummy-variable break tests have little power against threshold processes, so she recommends the threshold model as “a general test for parameter instability” (Enders 2014).

When the date is unknown, the same Davies problem applies. Andrews–Quandt takes the supremum of the break statistic over trimmed candidate dates. Bai–Perron extends this to several breaks and selects their number by SBC: “The AIC is not recommended as it selects too many breaks” (Enders 2014). For transnational terrorism, SBC picks four breaks in the mean (1975Q3, 1992Q3, 1994Q3 and 1997Q4), each with a confidence interval for its date (Enders 2014). A break can also be smooth: a logistic function of time, the STAR idea applied to the calendar (Enders 2014). For the unit-root side of breaks, see the Perron critique.

Common pitfalls

WarningWhere this goes wrong

  • Reading a searched-for threshold off an F table. Under linearity the threshold is not identified, so the usual distribution does not apply. Bootstrap the whole search (Hansen 1997). Unemployment’s “F=4.22” means nothing until it is bootstrapped. (Enders 2014)

  • Trusting the RESET alone. It has little power against asymmetric (threshold) models. It missed the unemployment nonlinearity that McLeod–Li caught. Use several tests. (Enders 2014)

  • Fitting a nonlinear model because you can. A wrong nonlinear specification can do more harm than ignoring nonlinearity. Rothman’s in-sample gains were a few percent of residual variance. Ask whether the shape has a story (transaction costs, policy asymmetry) and whether it forecasts better out of sample, as Potter’s TAR did. (Enders 2014)

  • Reporting one impulse response. In a nonlinear model the response depends on the history, the sign and the size of the shock. State them, and use generalised impulse responses. (Enders 2014)

  • A thin regime. If one regime rarely occurs, its coefficients are poorly estimated. That is why the search is trimmed at 15%, and it is a warning about any regime with few observations, such as Potter’s 37 recession quarters. (Enders 2014)

  • Concluding “unit root” from a linear test on an asymmetric series. Dickey–Fuller has low power against asymmetric adjustment. Try the Enders–Granger TAR or M-TAR test. (Enders 2014)

  • Using AIC to count structural breaks. It selects too many. Use SBC or the sequential Bai–Perron procedure. (Enders 2014)

Appendix · the mathematics

Chan’s estimator. For a fixed \tau the TAR is linear in its coefficients, so SSR(\tau)=\min_{\alpha}\sum_t(y_t-x_t(\tau)'\alpha)^2 comes from one OLS fit. SSR(\tau) is a step function that changes only when \tau passes an observed value of y_{t-d}. So the search over the real line reduces to the sorted sample values between the 15th and 85th percentiles, and \hat\tau=\arg\min SSR(\tau). Chan (1993) shows that \hat\tau converges at rate T rather than \sqrt T (super-consistency), which is why the slope coefficients can then be treated as if \tau were known.

Why the F-test breaks (Davies). Under H_0:\alpha_{1i}=\alpha_{2i} the likelihood does not depend on \tau at all. The test statistic is therefore a whole process F(\tau), and the reported statistic is \sup_\tau F(\tau). The supremum of many correlated F statistics is stochastically larger than any one of them, so textbook critical values over-reject. Hansen’s bootstrap simulates \sup_\tau F^*(\tau) under the null and uses its quantiles instead.

The STAR pretest. Write h=\gamma(y_{t-d}-c) and \theta(h)=(1+e^{-h})^{-1}. At h=0: \theta=\tfrac12, \theta'=\tfrac14, \theta''=0, \theta'''=-\tfrac18. A third-order expansion gives \theta-\tfrac12\approx h/4-h^3/48. Substituting into the STAR model turns the unidentified (\gamma,c) into ordinary regression coefficients on y_{t-i}y_{t-d}, y_{t-i}y^2_{t-d} and y_{t-i}y^3_{t-d}, which can be tested with an F-test. ESTAR’s \theta=1-e^{-h^2} is even in h, so its expansion has no cubic term. That asymmetry is what lets the cubic terms discriminate LSTAR from ESTAR.

Markov-switching long-run shares. The stationary distribution (p_1,p_2) of the chain solves p_1=p_{11}p_1+(1-p_{22})p_2 with p_1+p_2=1. Hence p_1(1-p_{11})=(1-p_{22})(1-p_1), which gives p_1=(1-p_{22})/(2-p_{11}-p_{22}). The expected stay in regime j is 1/(1-p_{jj}) periods. That is why a crisis state with a high exit probability is extreme but brief.

Generalised impulse response. For history \omega_{t-1} and shock \delta, GI(h,\delta,\omega_{t-1})=\mathbb E[y_{t+h}\mid\varepsilon_t=\delta,\omega_{t-1}]-\mathbb E[y_{t+h}\mid\omega_{t-1}], both expectations taken over future shocks \varepsilon_{t+1},\dots,\varepsilon_{t+h} and computed by simulation with common random numbers, as in the figure. For a linear model GI is \delta\rho^h, independent of \omega_{t-1}, linear in \delta and odd in \delta. Each of these three properties can fail for a TAR.

Unemployment rises sharply and falls slowly. The Fed reacts harder when inflation is high. Small price

## Why it matters

Enders opens the chapter with a road atlas [@enders_time_series]. A flat map is a linear approximation
to a curved earth. It is perfect for driving to the next town and disastrous for planning a rocket
launch. Linear [ARIMA](arima-forecasting.qmd) and [VAR](var-and-impulse-responses.qmd) models are the
road atlas of time series. They are usually good enough, but policy makers "could make a serious error"
if they ignore the evidence that **unemployment increases more sharply than it decreases**
[@enders_time_series]. Asymmetry like this is everywhere once you look:

- Gasoline prices go up faster than they come down [@enders_time_series].
- **Transport costs** stop anyone arbitraging a small gap between cotton prices in Alabama and
  Mississippi, while large gaps disappear almost at once [@enders_time_series].
- Interest-rate spreads may sit below their long-run value for much longer than they sit above it
  [@enders_time_series].

For social science these are substantive claims about behaviour: thresholds of action, reluctance to
adjust, and policy that responds differently in good times and bad. A linear model averages the two
behaviours into one coefficient and hides exactly the thing you wanted to know.

## What "linear" actually means

Take the AR(1) $y_t = a_1 y_{t-1}$ with $a_1 = 0.5$. Start at $y_{t-1}=1$ and the expected path is
$1, 0.5, 0.25, \dots$. Start at 2 and every value doubles. Start at $-1$ and the path is the mirror image.
**Linearity means scaling the starting point by $\lambda$ scales the whole path by $\lambda$**
[@enders_time_series]. Sign and size of a deviation never change *how* the series gets back.

A nonlinear process breaks this. Enders gives two pictures [@enders_time_series]:

- **A kink.** The series follows $y_t=a_1y_{t-1}$ above zero and $y_t=a_2y_{t-1}$ below, with
  $a_2>a_1$. Negative deviations then die out more slowly than positive ones.
- **Gravity.** An object falling toward the earth speeds up as it gets closer, so halving the distance
  does not halve the next step.

Once you leave linearity there are endless possible shapes. That is the main warning of the whole
chapter: **"adopting an incorrect nonlinear specification may be more problematic than simply ignoring
the nonlinearity altogether"** [@enders_time_series]. The rest of this entry is about choosing the shape
carefully.

## The simplest extensions of ARMA

Before thresholds, Enders lists two direct generalisations [@enders_time_series]:

- **GAR (generalised autoregressive)** models add powers and cross-products of lagged values, such as
  $y_{t-1}^2$ or $y_{t-1}y_{t-2}$. They are a Taylor-series approximation to an unknown nonlinear
  function, estimated by OLS and pared down by AIC or SBC.
- **Bilinear** models add products of lagged values and lagged *shocks*, such as $y_{t-1}\varepsilon_{t-1}$.
  The persistence then depends on the latest surprise. They are estimated by maximum likelihood.

Rothman's (1998) study of detrended US unemployment (1948Q1–1979Q4) fits AR, GAR, bilinear and TAR
models to the same data. The nonlinear models cut the residual variance only modestly: the variance
ratios relative to the AR are 0.965, 0.936 and 0.942. By AIC the ranking is bilinear, AR, GAR, then TAR
[@enders_time_series]. Nonlinearity is real, but a better fit inside the sample is not a large prize.

## Is there anything to model? Testing for nonlinearity

The ordinary autocorrelation function can miss nonlinear dependence completely. The process
$y_t=\varepsilon^2_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t$ has **zero autocorrelations**, even though $y_t$ plainly depends
on the past [@enders_time_series]. Enders' chaotic example $y_t=4y_{t-1}(1-y_{t-1})$ is fully
deterministic. Its first six autocorrelations are all within ±0.08, yet the correlation of $y^2_t$ with
$y^2_{t-1}$ is −0.281 [@enders_time_series]. So you test the *residuals of a good linear model* in ways
that can see curvature:

| Test | What it does | Strength / weakness |
|---|---|---|
| **McLeod–Li** | Ljung–Box $Q$ on the **squared** residuals, or equivalently $TR^2$ from regressing $\hat e^2_t$ on its own lags | the exact LM test for ARCH errors; also sees other nonlinearity [@enders_time_series] |
| **RESET** | adds powers $\hat y^2,\hat y^3,\dots$ of the fitted values, then an $F$-test | very general alternative, but "little power to detect asymmetric models" such as TAR [@enders_time_series] |
| **BDS** | portmanteau test of independence built from distances between pairs of residuals | responds to many kinds of misspecification; bootstrap the critical values in small samples [@enders_time_series] |
| **LM tests** | regress residuals on the derivatives of a specific nonlinear model (GAR, bilinear, STAR), evaluated under linearity | aimed at a named alternative, so more power against it [@enders_time_series] |

The US unemployment rate shows why you run more than one. Enders fits a linear AR to monthly changes
(January 1960–June 2013, 642 observations, mean 6.10%) [@enders_time_series]. The RESET finds nothing
($F=1.42$, $p=0.234$). The McLeod–Li regression of $e^2_t$ on two lags gives $F=10.95$, which is highly
significant. Regressing $e_t$ on $e^2_{t-1}$ gives a positive coefficient (0.3298, $t=2.30$): a large
error of *either* sign last month is followed by a positive error this month [@enders_time_series]. In a
symmetric world that cannot happen.

## Threshold autoregression (TAR)

The workhorse is the **two-regime TAR**. Pick a threshold variable, usually a lag $y_{t-d}$ of the series
itself, and let the AR coefficients switch when it crosses a value $\tau$ [@enders_time_series]:
$$
y_t = I_t\Big[\alpha_{10}+\sum_i\alpha_{1i}y_{t-i}\Big] + (1-I_t)\Big[\alpha_{20}+\sum_i\alpha_{2i}y_{t-i}\Big]+\varepsilon_t,
\qquad I_t=\mathbf 1\{y_{t-d}>\tau\}.
$$

If you know $\tau$ and the **delay** $d$, the model is two OLS regressions on two parts of the sample.
Usually you know neither, and three practical questions follow.

**Finding the threshold: Chan's grid search.** Chan (1993) showed that the $\tau$ minimising the sum of
squared residuals is a **super-consistent** estimate [@enders_time_series]. So you sort the candidate
values, fit the TAR at each one, and keep the minimum. To make sure each regime has enough data,
"the highest and lowest 15% of the values are excluded from the search" (10% in very large samples). With
200 observations that leaves about 141 candidate thresholds [@enders_time_series]. Plotting $SSR(\tau)$
is the diagnostic. One clear trough suggests one threshold; several troughs suggest several regimes.

**Choosing the delay.** Repeat the search for each candidate $d$ and keep the lowest SSR, or compare by
AIC or SBC [@enders_time_series].

**Testing it: the Davies problem.** This is the subtle part. Under the null of linearity the threshold
does not exist, so $\tau$ is **not identified under the null** [@enders_time_series]. The usual $F$- or
likelihood-ratio statistic then does not have its textbook distribution: you searched over $\tau$ for the
best fit, so a linear series will always produce some threshold that looks good. Hansen's (1997) fix is
a bootstrap. Generate many fake samples under the null, rerun the *whole* threshold search on each, and
compare your $F$ with the 95th percentile of the bootstrapped $F^*$ values [@enders_time_series].

### Worked example: asymmetric unemployment

Back to monthly US unemployment. With $d=1$ the SSR is minimised at $\tau=0.070$: the regime switches when
last month's *change* exceeds 0.07 percentage points [@enders_time_series]. Delays of 1, 2 and 3 give SSRs
of 14.296, 14.319 and 14.385, so $d=1$ wins. The naive $F$ for linearity is
$[(14.883-14.296)/6]/[14.296/617]=4.22$, but because $\tau$ was searched for it cannot be read from an $F$
table. Hansen's bootstrap rejects linearity at the 0.0025 level [@enders_time_series].

The two criteria disagree on the full threshold model: AIC prefers it, SBC prefers the linear one, and
many threshold coefficients are small. Enders pares it down and finds that **persistence is far higher in
the rising regime** (lag coefficients 0.387, 0.376 and 0.130) than in the falling one (0.155 and 0.188).
Increases in unemployment are far more persistent than decreases [@enders_time_series]. That is the
"rocket and feather" in numbers.

### Worked example: an asymmetric Taylor rule

The Taylor rule has the Fed setting the federal funds rate $i_t$ from inflation $\pi_t$, the output gap
$y_t$ and last quarter's rate. Bunzel and Enders (2010) estimate it for 1979Q4–2007Q3. The linear fit is
$i_t=-0.269+0.464\pi_t+0.345y_t+0.810i_{t-1}$, a long-run inflation response of
$0.464/(1-0.810)=2.44$ [@enders_time_series]. They then let it be a **threshold regression**, trying four
threshold variables with 15% trimming. All four fit better than the linear model and all are highly
significant by bootstrap. Inflation two quarters back, at $\tau=3.527\%$, fits best [@enders_time_series]:

| Regime | Constant | Inflation $\pi_t$ | Output gap $y_t$ | Smoothing $i_{t-1}$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| High inflation ($\pi_{t-2}\ge3.527$) | 1.383 | **1.055** | 0.472 | 0.374 |
| Low inflation ($\pi_{t-2}<3.527$) | −0.440 | 0.227 | 0.305 | **0.967** |
| Linear (one rule for all) | −0.269 | 0.464 | 0.345 | 0.810 |

When inflation is high the Fed reacts hard and smooths little; when it is low the Fed reacts weakly and
mostly holds its rate. The linear rule "seems to 'average' the responses" across the two regimes
[@enders_time_series]. It describes a Fed that never existed.

### Variations

- **Band-TAR** (Balke and Fomby 1997). Three regimes: a random walk inside a band $\bar s\pm c$, and mean
  reversion outside it. This is the shape of a transaction-cost story, where small gaps are not worth
  closing [@enders_time_series].
- **Threshold regression.** The threshold switches the slope on some *other* regressor $x_t$, as in the
  Taylor rule [@enders_time_series].
- **Multiple thresholds.** Boetel, Hoffman and Liu (2007) find three regimes in how pork producers adjust
  their breeding stock, with thresholds estimated one after the other [@enders_time_series].

## Smooth transitions: LSTAR and ESTAR

A TAR switches regimes like a light switch. Often a **dimmer** is more realistic: behaviour shifts
gradually as the state variable moves. The **smooth transition autoregression** (STAR) blends two AR
models with a weight $\theta\in[0,1]$ that depends on $y_{t-1}$ [@enders_time_series]:

- **LSTAR (logistic).** $\theta=[1+\exp(-\gamma(y_{t-1}-c))]^{-1}$ is an S-curve. Low values of the series
  get one set of dynamics and high values the other. As the smoothness parameter $\gamma$ grows, the S
  sharpens until the model *is* a TAR. As $\gamma\to0$ it collapses back to a linear AR
  [@enders_time_series].
- **ESTAR (exponential).** $\theta=1-\exp[-\gamma(y_{t-1}-c)^2]$ is a U-shape, symmetric about $c$. Only
  the *size* of the deviation matters, not its sign [@enders_time_series].

ESTAR is the natural model for **purchasing power parity with transaction costs**. Michael, Nobay and Peel
(1997) argue that small deviations from PPP are not worth arbitraging, while large ones are corrected
quickly [@enders_time_series]. Their estimate for the monthly US–UK real exchange rate has
$\gamma=532.4$ and $c=0.038$. Near 0.038 the speed-of-adjustment coefficient is zero, so there is no pull
back at all. Far from it the coefficient on $y_{t-1}$ approaches $-1$, so adjustment is fast. "The
adjustment of the real exchange rate is consistent with the presence of transaction costs"
[@enders_time_series]. A linear model fitted to such a series sees a lot of time spent drifting and may
call the whole thing a random walk.

**Choosing between them** (Teräsvirta 1994). You cannot test for STAR directly, because $\gamma$ and $c$
are unidentified under linearity: the Davies problem again. Instead, replace $\theta$ by a Taylor
expansion and run an auxiliary regression of the residuals on the regressors multiplied by $y_{t-d}$,
$y^2_{t-d}$ and $y^3_{t-d}$ [@enders_time_series]. If the cubic terms matter, choose LSTAR; otherwise
choose ESTAR, whose expansion is quadratic. On Enders' simulated LSTAR series (250 observations) the AR(1)
residuals look like white noise ($Q$ p-values 0.900, 0.347, 0.471), yet the RESET gives $F=95.60$ against
a 1% critical value of 3.86, and the cubic terms are what matter, so the test correctly selects LSTAR
[@enders_time_series]. Nonlinear least squares then recovers parameters close to the truth, such as a
threshold of 5.01 against the true 5 [@enders_time_series].

## Markov switching: regimes you cannot see

In TAR and STAR models the regime is set by an **observable** variable. Hamilton's (1989) **Markov
switching** model "posits that regime switches are exogenous" [@enders_time_series]. A hidden state
follows a two-state Markov chain. The chain stays in regime 1 with probability $p_{11}$ and in regime 2
with probability $p_{22}$, and each regime has its own AR process. "No attempt is made to explain the
reason that regime changes occur", only to estimate the transition probabilities along with everything
else [@enders_time_series]. The long-run share of time in regime 1 is
$p_1=(1-p_{22})/(2-p_{11}-p_{22})$. With $p_{11}=0.75$ and $p_{22}=0.5$ the system spends two-thirds of
its time in regime 1 [@enders_time_series]. In practice it is "very difficult" to tell a Markov-switching
model from a STAR model statistically [@enders_time_series].

**Worked example: crisis volatility.** Edwards and Susmel (2000) study weekly interest rates in emerging
markets. For Brazil (April 1994–April 1999) a standard GARCH(1,1) gives variance coefficients summing to
more than one ($1.321+0.395$), so it **predicts explosive volatility** [@enders_time_series]. Their
**SWARCH** alternative puts an ARCH model inside each of three hidden volatility states. Relative to the
calm state, the middle state is 4.851 times as volatile and the crisis state **128.51 times**. The chance
of leaving the crisis state is high, so extreme volatility is short-lived [@enders_time_series]. GARCH
read occasional storms as permanent persistence; the switching model reads them as rare, brief visits to
a different world. This links directly to [ARCH/GARCH](volatility-arch-garch.qmd) and to the hidden-state
logic of [state-space models](state-space-and-kalman-filter.qmd).

Enders also covers **artificial neural networks** in this chapter. They are a sum of logistic "nodes" that
can approximate any nonlinear function (Kuan and White 1994), but their parameters are not globally
identified once there is more than one node [@enders_time_series]. They sit at the predictive end of the
spectrum and are covered in the ML track's [neural networks](../ml-track/deep-learning-neural-networks.qmd)
entry.

## Shocks that depend on history

The figure below shows the most important practical consequence. In a linear model an impulse response is
a fixed object: a one-unit shock has effect $1,\rho,\rho^2,\dots$, a two-unit shock has twice that, and a
negative shock is the mirror image. In a nonlinear model **impulse responses are history-dependent**
[@enders_time_series]. Their sign matters, their size matters (a big shock is more likely to push the
series across the threshold), and so do the shocks that arrive later. You have to say *which history* and
*which shock*, then average over future shocks by simulation. That is the **generalised impulse
response** of Koop, Pesaran and Potter (1996) [@enders_time_series].

Potter's (1995) model of US GNP growth (1947Q1–1990Q4) shows what is at stake. With a threshold at zero
growth two quarters back, the contractionary regime has only 37 of the 170 observations, larger shocks
(variance 1.50 vs 0.763), and an AR(2) coefficient of −0.946, which produces sharp reversals out of
recessions [@enders_time_series]. One-step-ahead forecasts from 1960Q1 correlate 0.35 with actual growth
for the TAR against 0.23 for the linear AR [@enders_time_series]. From the boom of 1983–84 even a −2%
shock leaves growth positive, so the responses look linear. From the mild downturn of 1970 negative
shocks push growth across the threshold, and the responses become asymmetric and no longer proportional
to the size of the shock [@enders_time_series].

```{python}
#| label: fig-nonlinear-ts
#| fig-cap: "Two signatures of a threshold process, both rebuilt by hand from Enders' textbook models. Left: Chan's grid search on a simulated two-regime TAR, yₜ = 0.3yₜ₋₁ when yₜ₋₁ > 0 and 0.7yₜ₋₁ otherwise (200 observations, the middle 70% of sorted values searched). The sum of squared residuals falls into a flat trough around the true threshold of zero (dashed line), and its minimum (orange) lands slightly above it. The threshold estimate is consistent, but in a sample of 200 it is not exact. Right: generalised impulse responses for the Koop–Pesaran–Potter model yₜ = 0.9yₜ₋₁ above zero and 0.1yₜ₋₁ below, starting from yₜ₋₁ = 0 and averaged over 2,000 draws of future shocks. A +1 shock (orange) lands in the persistent regime and lingers. A −1 shock (ink, sign-flipped for comparison) lands in the fast-decaying regime and has mostly faded within two periods. It does not vanish immediately, because later random shocks can push the series back across zero. In a linear model the two lines would coincide exactly."
#| code-fold: true
#| code-summary: "Show the figure code"

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)

# --- Left: Chan (1993) grid search on a simulated TAR -------------------------
T = 200
y = np.zeros(T + 1)
eps = rng.normal(0, 1, T + 1)
for t in range(1, T + 1):
    a = 0.3 if y[t-1] > 0 else 0.7
    y[t] = a * y[t-1] + eps[t]
y = y[1:]
ylag, ycur = y[:-1], y[1:]

cands = np.sort(ylag)
lo, hi = int(0.15 * cands.size), int(0.85 * cands.size)
taus = cands[lo:hi]
ssr = []
for tau in taus:
    I = ylag > tau
    X = np.column_stack([I * ylag, (~I) * ylag])
    b, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(X, ycur, rcond=None)
    ssr.append(np.sum((ycur - X @ b) ** 2))
ssr = np.array(ssr)
tau_hat = taus[ssr.argmin()]

# --- Right: generalised impulse responses (Koop–Pesaran–Potter) -----------------
H, R = 12, 2000
def path(y0, shock, future):
    out = np.empty(H + 1)
    prev = y0
    for h in range(H + 1):
        a = 0.9 if prev > 0 else 0.1
        out[h] = a * prev + (shock if h == 0 else 0.0) + future[h]
        prev = out[h]
    return out

girf_pos = np.zeros(H + 1); girf_neg = np.zeros(H + 1)
for _ in range(R):
    future = rng.normal(0, 1, H + 1)
    base = path(0.0, 0.0, future)
    girf_pos += path(0.0, 1.0, future) - base
    girf_neg += path(0.0, -1.0, future) - base
girf_pos /= R; girf_neg /= R

fig, (axL, axR) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(9.0, 3.8))
axL.plot(taus, ssr, color="#161613", lw=1.4)
axL.axvline(0.0, color="#8a8a7e", ls="--", lw=1.0)
axL.text(0.02, ssr.max(), "true τ = 0", fontsize=8, color="#5e5e54", va="top")
axL.plot(tau_hat, ssr.min(), "o", color="#e8531d", ms=7)
axL.annotate(f"SSR minimum\nτ = {tau_hat:.2f}", (tau_hat, ssr.min()),
             xytext=(tau_hat, ssr.min() + 0.3 * (ssr.max() - ssr.min())),
             textcoords="data", fontsize=8, ha="center",
             arrowprops=dict(arrowstyle="->", color="#5e5e54", lw=0.8))
axL.set_xlabel("candidate threshold τ")
axL.set_ylabel("sum of squared residuals")
axL.set_title("Chan's search: SSR(τ) dips at the threshold")

h = np.arange(H + 1)
axR.plot(h, girf_pos, color="#e8531d", lw=2.0, marker="o", ms=4, label="+1 shock")
axR.plot(h, -girf_neg, color="#161613", lw=1.6, marker="s", ms=4,
         label="−1 shock (sign flipped)")
axR.axhline(0, color="#b7b6a8", lw=0.8)
axR.set_xlabel("periods after the shock")
axR.set_ylabel("average response")
axR.set_title("Same size, opposite sign, different story")
axR.legend(fontsize=8, frameon=False)
fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

## Unit roots when adjustment is asymmetric

The [Dickey–Fuller test](unit-roots-and-stationarity.qmd) assumes **linear** adjustment toward the
long-run value. If the true adjustment is asymmetric, the test is misspecified and has low power, so it
may fail to detect a perfectly good long-run attractor [@enders_time_series]. Enders and Granger (1998)
generalise it. Let the speed of adjustment differ above and below the attractor $\tau$:
$$
\Delta y_t = I_t\rho_1(y_{t-1}-\tau)+(1-I_t)\rho_2(y_{t-1}-\tau)+\varepsilon_t .
$$
If $I_t$ depends on the *level* $y_{t-1}\ge\tau$, this is a TAR. If it depends on the *direction*
$\Delta y_{t-1}>0$, it is a **momentum TAR (M-TAR)**, where the series adjusts differently when rising
than when falling [@enders_time_series]. The test of $\rho_1=\rho_2=0$ (no attractor) needs special
critical values, as Dickey–Fuller does. Only once you reject it can you test symmetry, $\rho_1=\rho_2$
[@enders_time_series].

On the spread between the 10-year Treasury rate and the federal funds rate (1958Q1–1994Q1), the RESET
flags neglected nonlinearity ($p=0.0016$ and $0.00009$). Both AIC and SBC prefer the M-TAR. Its attractor
test statistic of 11.44 rejects "no attractor", and symmetry is rejected ($F=12.24$, $p=0.0006$)
[@enders_time_series]. The point estimates put the equilibrium spread at 1.64. When the spread is rising
it adjusts quickly (−0.299); when it is falling, adjustment is "almost nonexistent" (−0.007). The linear
model reports one speed for both. Its intercept is insignificant ($t=1.52$), which suggests an
equilibrium spread of zero [@enders_time_series]. This extends naturally to a **nonlinear error-correction model** for
[cointegrated](cointegration-and-error-correction.qmd) series.

## Thresholds and structural breaks are cousins

A structural break is a threshold in *time*: the regime switches when $t$ passes some date rather than
when $y_{t-d}$ passes some value. Enders makes the connection explicit: "in a model with structural breaks, time is the
threshold variable" [@enders_time_series]. His simulated break series (Y_BREAKS) can be estimated
as a threshold model in $t$. The search finds the
true break at $t=100$, and the linearity $F$ of 29.57 dwarfs the bootstrap 95% value of about 3.15
[@enders_time_series]. Carrasco (2002) shows that dummy-variable break tests have little power against
threshold processes, so she recommends the threshold model as "a general test for parameter instability"
[@enders_time_series].

When the date is unknown, the same Davies problem applies. **Andrews–Quandt** takes the supremum of the
break statistic over trimmed candidate dates. **Bai–Perron** extends this to several breaks and selects
their number by SBC: "The AIC is not recommended as it selects too many breaks" [@enders_time_series].
For transnational terrorism, SBC picks four breaks in the mean (1975Q3, 1992Q3, 1994Q3 and 1997Q4), each
with a confidence interval for its date [@enders_time_series]. A break can also be *smooth*: a
logistic function of time, the STAR idea applied to the calendar [@enders_time_series]. For the unit-root
side of breaks, see [the Perron critique](unit-roots-and-stationarity.qmd).

## Common pitfalls

::: {.callout-warning title="Where this goes wrong"}

- **Reading a searched-for threshold off an $F$ table.** Under linearity the threshold is not identified,
  so the usual distribution does not apply. Bootstrap the whole search (Hansen 1997). Unemployment's
  "$F=4.22$" means nothing until it is bootstrapped. [@enders_time_series]

- **Trusting the RESET alone.** It has little power against asymmetric (threshold) models. It missed the
  unemployment nonlinearity that McLeod–Li caught. Use several tests. [@enders_time_series]

- **Fitting a nonlinear model because you can.** A wrong nonlinear specification can do more harm than
  ignoring nonlinearity. Rothman's in-sample gains were a few percent of residual variance. Ask whether
  the shape has a story (transaction costs, policy asymmetry) and whether it forecasts better out of
  sample, as Potter's TAR did. [@enders_time_series]

- **Reporting one impulse response.** In a nonlinear model the response depends on the history, the sign
  and the size of the shock. State them, and use generalised impulse responses. [@enders_time_series]

- **A thin regime.** If one regime rarely occurs, its coefficients are poorly estimated. That is why the
  search is trimmed at 15%, and it is a warning about any regime with few observations, such as Potter's
  37 recession quarters. [@enders_time_series]

- **Concluding "unit root" from a linear test on an asymmetric series.** Dickey–Fuller has low power
  against asymmetric adjustment. Try the Enders–Granger TAR or M-TAR test. [@enders_time_series]

- **Using AIC to count structural breaks.** It selects too many. Use SBC or the sequential Bai–Perron
  procedure. [@enders_time_series]

:::

## Appendix · the mathematics {.unnumbered}

::: {.math-appendix}

**Chan's estimator.** For a fixed $\tau$ the TAR is linear in its coefficients, so
$SSR(\tau)=\min_{\alpha}\sum_t(y_t-x_t(\tau)'\alpha)^2$ comes from one OLS fit. $SSR(\tau)$ is a step
function that changes only when $\tau$ passes an observed value of $y_{t-d}$. So the search over the
real line reduces to the sorted sample values between the 15th and 85th percentiles, and
$\hat\tau=\arg\min SSR(\tau)$. Chan (1993) shows that $\hat\tau$ converges at rate $T$ rather than
$\sqrt T$ (super-consistency), which is why the slope coefficients can then be treated as if $\tau$ were
known.

**Why the $F$-test breaks (Davies).** Under $H_0:\alpha_{1i}=\alpha_{2i}$ the likelihood does not depend on
$\tau$ at all. The test statistic is therefore a whole *process* $F(\tau)$, and the reported statistic is
$\sup_\tau F(\tau)$. The supremum of many correlated $F$ statistics is stochastically larger than any one
of them, so textbook critical values over-reject. Hansen's bootstrap simulates $\sup_\tau F^*(\tau)$
under the null and uses its quantiles instead.

**The STAR pretest.** Write $h=\gamma(y_{t-d}-c)$ and $\theta(h)=(1+e^{-h})^{-1}$. At $h=0$:
$\theta=\tfrac12$, $\theta'=\tfrac14$, $\theta''=0$, $\theta'''=-\tfrac18$. A third-order expansion gives
$\theta-\tfrac12\approx h/4-h^3/48$. Substituting into the STAR model turns the unidentified
$(\gamma,c)$ into ordinary regression coefficients on $y_{t-i}y_{t-d}$, $y_{t-i}y^2_{t-d}$ and
$y_{t-i}y^3_{t-d}$, which can be tested with an $F$-test. ESTAR's $\theta=1-e^{-h^2}$ is even in $h$, so
its expansion has no cubic term. That asymmetry is what lets the cubic terms discriminate LSTAR from ESTAR.

**Markov-switching long-run shares.** The stationary distribution $(p_1,p_2)$ of the chain solves
$p_1=p_{11}p_1+(1-p_{22})p_2$ with $p_1+p_2=1$. Hence
$p_1(1-p_{11})=(1-p_{22})(1-p_1)$, which gives $p_1=(1-p_{22})/(2-p_{11}-p_{22})$. The expected stay in
regime $j$ is $1/(1-p_{jj})$ periods. That is why a crisis state with a high exit probability is extreme
but brief.

**Generalised impulse response.** For history $\omega_{t-1}$ and shock $\delta$,
$$
GI(h,\delta,\omega_{t-1})=\mathbb E[y_{t+h}\mid\varepsilon_t=\delta,\omega_{t-1}]-\mathbb E[y_{t+h}\mid\omega_{t-1}],
$$
both expectations taken over future shocks $\varepsilon_{t+1},\dots,\varepsilon_{t+h}$ and computed by
simulation with common random numbers, as in the figure. For a linear model $GI$ is $\delta\rho^h$,
independent of $\omega_{t-1}$, linear in $\delta$ and odd in $\delta$. Each of these three properties can
fail for a TAR.
