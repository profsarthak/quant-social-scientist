Using Existing Data: Secondary Analysis, Official Statistics & Big Data

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source

Most of the best data you will ever analyse was collected by someone else, for some other purpose. That is its strength and its catch.

Data you did not collect is usually bigger, better sampled and cheaper than anything you could gather yourself. But every existing data set was produced for a purpose: a survey’s research questions, an agency’s administrative routine, a platform’s business. Before you analyse it, find out how each number was made. That process is part of what the number measures.

Why it matters

Bryman’s teaching experience is that “many students assume any research they conduct has to involve collecting primary data” (Clark et al. 2021). Often it should not. Research funders routinely ask applicants who propose new data collection to show that suitable secondary data do not already exist (Clark et al. 2021). A national panel survey with tens of thousands of respondents, professional fieldwork and documented non-response follow-up will almost always beat a student’s convenience sample. It also leaves more of the project’s time for analysis.

The catch is that you inherit decisions you did not make: who was sampled, what was asked, how categories were coded, and, for official statistics, which events an agency processed at all. A recorded crime rate is not a count of crimes. It is a count of crimes that someone noticed, recognised, reported, and that the police chose to record (Clark et al. 2021). Using existing data well means reconstructing that production process before you trust the numbers.

Four ways to use data that already exist

Bryman distinguishes four routes, alongside content analysis of documents and media, which also works on material that already exists (Clark et al. 2021):

Route What you analyse Typical source
Secondary analysis other researchers’ raw data, for questions they may not have had in mind large continuous surveys, archived studies
Meta-analysis published results (effect sizes, correlations) from many studies journal articles; see meta-analysis
Official statistics records that state agencies keep in the course of their work crime, unemployment, education, health departments
Big Data very large, fast, varied digital traces social media, loyalty cards, wearables

(Clark et al. 2021)

Even the line between primary and secondary analysis is blurry. A researcher who collects a survey, publishes from it, and returns years later to re-analyse it for a new question is doing something that is hard to classify as either (Clark et al. 2021).

Secondary analysis: what you gain

Bryman lists six advantages, drawing on Dale et al. (1988) and Johnston (2017) (Clark et al. 2021):

  • Cost and time. Archived data costs a tiny fraction of what fieldwork would.
  • Quality. Rigorous sampling, national coverage, experienced fieldwork teams, and established procedures for chasing non-respondents.
  • Longitudinal, subgroup and cross-cultural analysis that a single project could not afford.
  • More time for analysis, which students tend to underestimate.
  • New interpretations. A secondary analyst can bring in a variable the original team ignored, a new theory, or a different technique.
  • Wider obligations. Participants gave their time, usually for nothing, and much social research is “chronically under-analysed”. Reuse also reduces research fatigue.

Three of these show up clearly in Bryman’s examples.

NoteLongitudinal: does having a child make you greener?

Thomas et al. (2018) used Understanding Society (USoc), a UK household panel with a core sample of about 40,000 households, to test the “legacy hypothesis”: that becoming a parent makes people care more about the environment because they think about future generations. Of the 40,172 wave-1 individuals, 27,193 (67.7%) stayed in through wave 4, and 1,656 reported a newborn in between. Changes in the three attitude items and 11 behaviours were small, and all were negative: new parents did slightly less. The only positive change was among first-time parents who already had high environmental concern (Clark et al. 2021). A panel of this size and length would have been impossible to collect for one study. On analysing panels, see panel data and fixed effects.

Subgroups. Large samples contain large numbers of small categories: grandparents providing childcare, self-employed workers’ pensions. Sloan (2017), one of the book’s authors, used the 2011 census and the 2015 British Social Attitudes survey (with an extra question added) to profile who uses Twitter. Users were disproportionately male, mainly young (though with more older users than earlier estimates), and strongly patterned by class (Clark et al. 2021). One secondary data set was used to check how representative another kind of data was, a point that returns below under Big Data.

Cross-cultural comparison works only if the questions are comparable. The European Social Survey is designed so that questions are understood the same way across countries and languages. Röder and Mühlau (2014) used rounds 2 and 4 (2004, 2008) for 27 of the 33 nations to study gender egalitarianism (Clark et al. 2021). Beyond Europe, the Demographic and Health Surveys Program runs more than 400 surveys in over 90 countries, and UNICEF’s Multiple Indicator Cluster Surveys provide comparable data on women and children (Clark et al. 2021).

A few of the large UK data sets Bryman lists, to give a sense of scale (Clark et al. 2021):

Data set Design Scale
National Child Development Study cohort: everyone born in Great Britain, 3–9 March 1958 ~17,000; 10 waves to 2020
1970 British Cohort Study cohort: born in one week of 1970 17,200
Millennium Cohort Study cohort: born 2000–02 19,000 babies and families
Understanding Society (formerly BHPS) household panel, annual 40,000 households (BHPS: ~10,000 people in ~5,000 households from 1991)
British Social Attitudes repeated cross-section since 1983 3,000+ adults, multi-stage stratified sample
Crime Survey for England & Wales victimisation survey, annual since 2001 ~50,000 households invited (2018–19)
Labour Force Survey quarterly since 1991 ~40,000 addresses
European Social Survey biennial since 2001 30+ countries

Archives such as the UK Data Archive at Essex, CESSDA, data.gov, the Australian Data Archive and the re3data registry hold far more. A single keyword search of the UK archive for “housing” returned 1,180 studies, each with documentation on sponsors, sampling, data collection and access conditions (Clark et al. 2021). The surveys in this table are complex samples (stratified, clustered, weighted), so analyse them with design-based estimation, not as if they were simple random samples.

Secondary analysis: what you give up

The limitations are the mirror image (Clark et al. 2021):

  • Unfamiliarity. You have to learn the variables, the coding and the file structure. With large, complex data sets this takes a long time.
  • Complexity. Many large surveys are hierarchical: USoc records some variables at the household level (number of cars, children at home) and others at the individual level (income, employment). You must choose a level of analysis and extract the right file. Analysing both levels at once is what mixed-effects models are for.
  • No control over quality. Well-documented surveys can be checked. Others need more caution.
  • Missing key variables. The data were collected for someone else’s questions. A concept that became important after fieldwork may not be measured at all.

Bryman’s advice on the last point is practical: look at the variables before committing to a data set, then either adapt the research question or collect primary data (Clark et al. 2021). In the language of regression and causality, a missing variable is not only a missing topic. If it is a confounder, its absence biases every estimate you make from that data set.

Official statistics: a census of what agencies process

States keep continuous records of their activity. Brought together, those records become official statistics: police-recorded crime, the unemployment claimant count, school examination results, class sizes, health-workforce figures, immigration data (Clark et al. 2021). Their advantages overlap with those of secondary analysis, with two additions (Clark et al. 2021):

  • A potentially complete picture. The records may cover a whole population rather than a sample.
  • Low reactivity. Nobody is answering a researcher’s question, so people’s awareness of being studied does not change what is recorded. Durkheim’s Suicide (1897) was built on comparative official statistics.

That second advantage makes official statistics an unobtrusive method, a term from Webb et al. (1966). Webb and colleagues argued that social research leaned too heavily on reactive methods such as interviews and questionnaires, and called for triangulation of reactive and non-reactive evidence, not replacement (Clark et al. 2021). Their four types, and Lee’s (2000) re-sorting of them:

Webb et al. (1966) Examples Lee (2000)
Physical traces graffiti, rubbish found data
Archive materials official and organisational statistics, diaries, mass media, historical records retrieved data: running records (births, marriages, deaths; personal ads) and personal and episodic records (letters, diaries, press photos, inquiry reports)
Simple observation observer passive and unseen captured data
Contrived observation setting varied unobtrusively, or hidden recording captured data
— — records produced online (email, forums; now blogs and tweets)

(Clark et al. 2021)

Bryman adds an important caveat. Documents that were not produced for research are non-reactive, but “we cannot assume that they are unproblematic”. They were “always produced for a purpose (even if not for research purposes)” (Clark et al. 2021).

How a number gets made

Crime statistics are Bryman’s main case. An offence enters the recorded crime rate only after passing a series of steps, adapted from Beardsworth et al. It must be witnessed or experienced by a member of the public, or seen by the police while on patrol. It must be recognised as a crime and reported. The police must then decide to record it rather than, say, issue a warning (Clark et al. 2021). Each step is a place where an offence can drop out. Police discretion depends on severity, the person’s record and demeanour, and the workload, since every charge brings paperwork. What the police witness depends on where patrols are sent (Clark et al. 2021). The unrecorded remainder is criminology’s “dark figure” (Clark et al. 2021).

This is why the Office for National Statistics describes the Crime Survey for England and Wales, a victimisation survey, as “a better indicator of long-term trends” for the crimes it covers than police records: the survey is “unaffected by changes in levels of reporting to the police or police recording practices”, and its method has stayed comparable since 1981 (Clark et al. 2021). The claimant count has the same problem in another field. It misses unemployed people who do not claim or whose claims are rejected, and it includes claimants who in fact work in the informal economy (Clark et al. 2021).

Over time, three kinds of change move an official series without any change in the underlying behaviour (Clark et al. 2021):

  1. Policy and practice. A crackdown on drugs or drink-driving shifts resources and makes charging more likely, so recorded offences rise.
  2. Definitions. Marital rape became a crime in England and Wales in 1991. Since then, willingness to report sexual assault has also shifted, for example with #MeToo.
  3. Recording rules. Several burglaries in one block of flats on one day might now be logged as a single incident instead of several.

The right-hand panel of the figure below simulates the consequence.

for j in range(J):

    offend = rng.random(m) < 0.02 + 0.08 * depriv[j]     # same for A and B

Associations in official data, and the ecological fallacy

The problems extend from the counts to the associations drawn from them. Suppose recorded crime is higher in areas with a particular ethnic or class composition. Bryman gives two reasons not to conclude that ethnicity or class is related to offending (Clark et al. 2021).

The first is validity. Recorded differences can come from the recording process itself: members of the public reporting differently depending on who the suspect is, patrols concentrated in some areas, discretion exercised unevenly, and less investigation of crimes linked to advantage, such as white-collar crime. Institutional racism has been documented in UK police forces (MacPherson 1999) (Clark et al. 2021).

The second is the ecological fallacy: inferring something about individuals from aggregated data, in which groups of observations have been replaced by summary statistics (Clark et al. 2021). The left panel of the figure shows how. Area offending rises steeply with the share of group A, yet within every area the two groups offend at the same rate. The area-level relationship is real, but it comes from something that varies between areas (here, deprivation) and says nothing about whether individual members of group A behave differently. A Simpson’s-paradox reversal is the same problem in its most extreme form.

Note‘You are where you live’? Low-participation neighbourhoods

UK universities and government use low participation neighbourhoods (LPNs), areas defined from historic aggregate data as places where young people are unlikely to enter higher education, to target disadvantaged applicants. Harrison and McCaig (2015) checked this against a range of official data. More disadvantaged families live outside LPNs than inside them, and LPNs contain a higher-than-expected share of relatively advantaged families. Targeting by area slightly improves the odds of “finding” the right students, but the label lacks “the predictive power that one might assume” (Clark et al. 2021). An area statistic is a weak proxy for an individual’s circumstances.

None of this is a reason to avoid official statistics. Bryman’s position is to use them, state their limitations in the write-up, and take those limitations into account in the claims you make. He also points out that reliability and validity are questions for every data source, not only official ones (Clark et al. 2021).

Big Data: non-reactive, but not representative

“Big Data” is a catch-all for data sets too large to process by conventional means, and sometimes for the predictive analytics run on them: loyalty-card transactions, wearables, mobile transactions, automated cameras (Kitchin 2014). In social research so far it has mostly meant social media (Clark et al. 2021). Sloan (2017) names three properties that create the challenges: the size of the data, the speed at which it is produced, and its variety of forms (text, images, audio, video, hyperlinks) (Clark et al. 2021).

There are two main ways to analyse it (Clark et al. 2021):

  • Content. What the posts say, often on a sample. Examples include Humphreys et al. (2014) on the personal information people reveal on Twitter, Greaves et al. (2014) on tweets to NHS hospitals, and Bharti et al. (2016) on sarcasm. Turning text into variables is covered in text as data.
  • Structure and process. How information flows. Tinati et al. (2014) built a tool to follow 12,831 tweets from 4,737 users about the 2011 #feesprotest against higher English tuition fees (8 October – 21 November). It showed which users were most influential, how content shifted from calls to participate towards complaints about policing, and that some users who wrote little themselves mattered a great deal because they retweeted regularly. This kind of analysis needs more specialist skills, such as network methods.

Scourfield et al. (2019) give a comparative design. They identified every death by suicide or road traffic accident of an 11–18-year-old in England reported in newspapers from February to July 2014, then searched a database of all tweets worldwide for the names of the deceased. Suicides drew more tweets than newspaper reports, Twitter and newspaper coverage were more closely correlated for suicides than for road deaths, and bullying-related suicides were especially newsworthy (Clark et al. 2021).

Tinati et al. also criticise the field. Researchers often shrink Big Data to manageable size, for example by keeping only the largest US police departments’ Facebook pages or by sampling a few tweets, and so analyse it with methods “that cannot explore” its scale, proportionality, dynamism and relationality (Clark et al. 2021).

Big Data shares the main strength of official statistics: it was not generated for research, so it is non-reactive. One of Bryman’s student panellists valued Twitter data because it “was not subject to any researcher biases” (Clark et al. 2021). That is true of reactivity, but it says nothing about who is in the data. Sloan’s census comparison above shows that Twitter users differ from the population by sex, age and class. Unlike archived surveys, platform data usually needs extensive organising before analysis, and it raises consent, confidentiality and data-protection questions (in Europe, under the GDPR) (Clark et al. 2021). On those, see research integrity & data ethics.

Common pitfalls

WarningWhere this goes wrong
  • Reading a recorded count as a count of events. Recorded crime is the end of a long filtering process, and the claimant count is a count of claims. Ask what an event has to go through to be recorded, and whether any of those steps changed during your period. (Clark et al. 2021)
  • Reading a break in a series as a change in behaviour. Crackdowns, new legal definitions and new counting rules all move official series. Check the series’ methodological notes before interpreting a jump, and compare with a series whose method stayed fixed, such as the victimisation survey. (Clark et al. 2021)
  • Inferring individual behaviour from area data. That is the ecological fallacy. An area-level correlation can arise entirely from what varies between areas. If the question is about individuals, you need individual-level data. (Clark et al. 2021)
  • Treating “non-reactive” as “unbiased”. Official records and social media avoid reactivity, but they were made for a purpose and cover only some people: those an agency processes, or those who post. Sloan’s Twitter profile is a warning against generalising from platform users to the population. (Clark et al. 2021)
  • Committing to a data set before checking its variables. If a key concept or confounder is not measured, no analysis can recover it. Read the documentation and the questionnaire first. (Clark et al. 2021)
  • Analysing a complex survey as a simple random sample. National surveys are stratified, clustered and weighted. Ignoring the design gives wrong standard errors and, without weights, possibly wrong estimates. See complex survey estimation.

Appendix · the mathematics

Why an area slope is not an individual slope. Let person i in area j have outcome y_{ij} = \alpha + \beta x_{ij} + \gamma \bar x_j + e_{ij}, where x_{ij} is an individual characteristic (1 if in group A) and \bar x_j its area mean. Here \beta is the individual-level effect and \gamma a contextual effect of the area’s composition, or of anything correlated with it (deprivation, in the figure). Averaging within areas gives \bar y_j = \alpha + (\beta + \gamma)\,\bar x_j + \bar e_j. A regression on area data therefore estimates \beta + \gamma. It recovers the individual effect only if \gamma = 0, and nothing in the aggregate data can tell you whether it is. In the figure \beta = 0, and the whole area slope is \gamma. A within-area comparison, which fixed effects generalise, removes \gamma \bar x_j and isolates \beta.

Why a recorded series confounds three trends. If recorded offences are R_t = N_t \cdot p^{\text{report}}_t \cdot p^{\text{record}}_t, then

\Delta \log R_t = \Delta \log N_t + \Delta \log p^{\text{report}}_t + \Delta \log p^{\text{record}}_t .

The recorded trend equals the true trend only when the reporting and recording rates are constant. In the figure, N falls 25% while p^{\text{report}} rises from 0.40 to 0.56 (+40\%) and p^{\text{record}} falls 20%, so R ends at 0.75 \times 1.40 \times 0.80 = 0.84 of its starting level. A victimisation survey measures N_t directly, which is why its trend is the more trustworthy one.

Panel attrition compounds. USoc kept 27,193 of 40,172 people from wave 1 to wave 4, a three-interval retention of 0.677. If retention were the same at each interval, it would be 0.677^{1/3} \approx 0.878 per wave (our arithmetic). Small per-wave losses add up, and when dropping out is related to the outcome, the survivors are a selected sample.

Data you did not collect is usually **bigger, better sampled and cheaper** than anything you could
gather yourself. But every existing data set was **produced for a purpose**: a survey's research

## Why it matters

Bryman's teaching experience is that "many students assume any research they conduct has to involve
collecting primary data" [@clark_bryman_2021]. Often it should not. Research funders routinely ask
applicants who propose new data collection to show that suitable secondary data **do not already
exist** [@clark_bryman_2021]. A national panel survey with tens of thousands of respondents,
professional fieldwork and documented non-response follow-up will almost always beat a student's
convenience sample. It also leaves more of the project's time for analysis.

The catch is that you inherit decisions you did not make: who was sampled, what was asked, how
categories were coded, and, for official statistics, which events an agency *processed* at all. A
recorded crime rate is not a count of crimes. It is a count of crimes that someone noticed, recognised,
reported, and that the police chose to record [@clark_bryman_2021]. Using existing data well means
reconstructing that production process before you trust the numbers.

## Four ways to use data that already exist

Bryman distinguishes four routes, alongside [content analysis](content-analysis.qmd) of documents
and media, which also works on material that already exists [@clark_bryman_2021]:

| Route | What you analyse | Typical source |
|---|---|---|
| **Secondary analysis** | other researchers' **raw data**, for questions they may not have had in mind | large continuous surveys, archived studies |
| **Meta-analysis** | **published results** (effect sizes, correlations) from many studies | journal articles; see [meta-analysis](../06-field-realities/meta-analysis.qmd) |
| **Official statistics** | records that **state agencies** keep in the course of their work | crime, unemployment, education, health departments |
| **Big Data** | very large, fast, varied **digital traces** | social media, loyalty cards, wearables |

[@clark_bryman_2021]

Even the line between primary and secondary analysis is blurry. A researcher who collects a survey,
publishes from it, and returns years later to re-analyse it for a new question is doing something that
is hard to classify as either [@clark_bryman_2021].

## Secondary analysis: what you gain

Bryman lists six advantages, drawing on Dale et al. (1988) and Johnston (2017) [@clark_bryman_2021]:

- **Cost and time.** Archived data costs a tiny fraction of what fieldwork would.
- **Quality.** Rigorous sampling, national coverage, experienced fieldwork teams, and established
  procedures for chasing non-respondents.
- **Longitudinal, subgroup and cross-cultural analysis** that a single project could not afford.
- **More time for analysis**, which students tend to underestimate.
- **New interpretations.** A secondary analyst can bring in a variable the original team ignored, a new
  theory, or a different technique.
- **Wider obligations.** Participants gave their time, usually for nothing, and much social research is
  "chronically under-analysed". Reuse also reduces **research fatigue**.

Three of these show up clearly in Bryman's examples.

::: {.callout-note title="Longitudinal: does having a child make you greener?"}
Thomas et al. (2018) used **Understanding Society** (USoc), a UK household panel with a core sample of
about 40,000 households, to test the "legacy hypothesis": that becoming a parent makes people care more
about the environment because they think about future generations. Of the **40,172** wave-1 individuals,
**27,193 (67.7%)** stayed in through wave 4, and **1,656** reported a newborn in between. Changes in the
three attitude items and 11 behaviours were small, and **all were negative**: new parents did slightly
*less*. The only positive change was among first-time parents who already had high environmental
concern [@clark_bryman_2021]. A panel of this size and length would have been impossible to collect for
one study. On analysing panels, see [panel data and fixed effects](../05-causal-inference/panel-and-fixed-effects.qmd).
:::

**Subgroups.** Large samples contain large numbers of small categories: grandparents providing
childcare, self-employed workers' pensions. Sloan (2017), one of the book's authors, used the 2011
census and the 2015 British Social Attitudes survey (with an extra question added) to profile who uses
Twitter. Users were disproportionately male, mainly young (though with more older users than earlier
estimates), and strongly patterned by class [@clark_bryman_2021]. One secondary data set was used to
check how representative another kind of data was, a point that returns below under Big Data.

**Cross-cultural comparison** works only if the questions are comparable. The **European Social
Survey** is designed so that questions are understood the same way across countries and languages.
Röder and Mühlau (2014) used rounds 2 and 4 (2004, 2008) for 27 of the 33 nations to study gender
egalitarianism [@clark_bryman_2021]. Beyond Europe, the Demographic and Health Surveys Program runs
more than 400 surveys in over 90 countries, and UNICEF's Multiple Indicator Cluster Surveys provide
comparable data on women and children [@clark_bryman_2021].

A few of the large UK data sets Bryman lists, to give a sense of scale [@clark_bryman_2021]:

| Data set | Design | Scale |
|---|---|---|
| National Child Development Study | cohort: everyone born in Great Britain, 3–9 March 1958 | ~17,000; 10 waves to 2020 |
| 1970 British Cohort Study | cohort: born in one week of 1970 | 17,200 |
| Millennium Cohort Study | cohort: born 2000–02 | 19,000 babies and families |
| Understanding Society (formerly BHPS) | household panel, annual | 40,000 households (BHPS: ~10,000 people in ~5,000 households from 1991) |
| British Social Attitudes | repeated cross-section since 1983 | 3,000+ adults, multi-stage stratified sample |
| Crime Survey for England & Wales | victimisation survey, annual since 2001 | ~50,000 households invited (2018–19) |
| Labour Force Survey | quarterly since 1991 | ~40,000 addresses |
| European Social Survey | biennial since 2001 | 30+ countries |

Archives such as the UK Data Archive at Essex, CESSDA, data.gov, the Australian Data Archive and the
re3data registry hold far more. A single keyword search of the UK archive for "housing" returned
**1,180 studies**, each with documentation on sponsors, sampling, data collection and access conditions
[@clark_bryman_2021]. The surveys in this table are **complex samples** (stratified, clustered,
weighted), so analyse them with [design-based estimation](../02-inference/complex-survey-estimation.qmd),
not as if they were simple random samples.

## Secondary analysis: what you give up

The limitations are the mirror image [@clark_bryman_2021]:

- **Unfamiliarity.** You have to learn the variables, the coding and the file structure. With large,
  complex data sets this takes a long time.
- **Complexity.** Many large surveys are **hierarchical**: USoc records some variables at the household
  level (number of cars, children at home) and others at the individual level (income, employment). You
  must choose a level of analysis and extract the right file. Analysing both levels at once is what
  [mixed-effects models](../03-regression/mixed-effects-models.qmd) are for.
- **No control over quality.** Well-documented surveys can be checked. Others need more caution.
- **Missing key variables.** The data were collected for someone else's questions. A concept that
  became important after fieldwork may not be measured at all.

Bryman's advice on the last point is practical: **look at the variables before committing** to a data
set, then either adapt the research question or collect primary data [@clark_bryman_2021]. In the
language of [regression and causality](../03-regression/regression-and-causality.qmd), a missing
variable is not only a missing topic. If it is a confounder, its absence biases every estimate you make
from that data set.

## Official statistics: a census of what agencies process

States keep continuous records of their activity. Brought together, those records become **official
statistics**: police-recorded crime, the unemployment **claimant count**, school examination results,
class sizes, health-workforce figures, immigration data [@clark_bryman_2021]. Their advantages overlap
with those of secondary analysis, with two additions [@clark_bryman_2021]:

- **A potentially complete picture.** The records may cover a whole population rather than a sample.
- **Low reactivity.** Nobody is answering a researcher's question, so people's awareness of being
  studied does not change what is recorded. Durkheim's *Suicide* (1897) was built on comparative
  official statistics.

That second advantage makes official statistics an **unobtrusive method**, a term from Webb et al.
(1966). Webb and colleagues argued that social research leaned too heavily on reactive methods such as
interviews and questionnaires, and called for **triangulation** of reactive and non-reactive evidence,
not replacement [@clark_bryman_2021]. Their four types, and Lee's (2000) re-sorting of them:

| Webb et al. (1966) | Examples | Lee (2000) |
|---|---|---|
| **Physical traces** | graffiti, rubbish | found data |
| **Archive materials** | official and organisational statistics, diaries, mass media, historical records | retrieved data: *running records* (births, marriages, deaths; personal ads) and *personal and episodic records* (letters, diaries, press photos, inquiry reports) |
| **Simple observation** | observer passive and unseen | captured data |
| **Contrived observation** | setting varied unobtrusively, or hidden recording | captured data |
| — | — | **records produced online** (email, forums; now blogs and tweets) |

[@clark_bryman_2021]

Bryman adds an important caveat. Documents that were not produced for research are non-reactive, but
"we cannot assume that they are unproblematic". They were "always produced for a purpose (even if not
for research purposes)" [@clark_bryman_2021].

## How a number gets made

Crime statistics are Bryman's main case. An offence enters the recorded crime rate only after passing
a series of steps, adapted from Beardsworth et al. It must be witnessed or experienced by a member of
the public, or seen by the police while on patrol. It must be recognised as a crime and reported. The
police must then decide to record it rather than, say, issue a warning [@clark_bryman_2021]. Each step
is a place where an offence can drop out. Police discretion depends on severity, the person's record and
demeanour, and the workload, since every charge brings paperwork. What the police witness depends on
where patrols are sent [@clark_bryman_2021]. The unrecorded remainder is criminology's **"dark
figure"** [@clark_bryman_2021].

This is why the Office for National Statistics describes the **Crime Survey for England and Wales**, a
victimisation survey, as "a better indicator of long-term trends" for the crimes it covers than police
records: the survey is "unaffected by changes in levels of reporting to the police or police recording
practices", and its method has stayed comparable since 1981 [@clark_bryman_2021]. The claimant count has
the same problem in another field. It misses unemployed people who do not claim or whose claims are
rejected, and it includes claimants who in fact work in the informal economy [@clark_bryman_2021].

Over time, three kinds of change move an official series without any change in the underlying
behaviour [@clark_bryman_2021]:

1. **Policy and practice.** A crackdown on drugs or drink-driving shifts resources and makes charging
   more likely, so recorded offences rise.
2. **Definitions.** Marital rape became a crime in England and Wales in 1991. Since then, willingness to
   report sexual assault has also shifted, for example with #MeToo.
3. **Recording rules.** Several burglaries in one block of flats on one day might now be logged as a
   single incident instead of several.

The right-hand panel of the figure below simulates the consequence.

```{python}
#| label: fig-existing-data
#| fig-cap: "Two simulations of how existing data can mislead (illustrative numbers, not real statistics). Left: the ecological fallacy. 60 areas of 2,000 residents. Areas with more group-A residents are more deprived, and deprivation raises everyone's offending, but within every area group A and group B offend at the same rate. The area-level line is steep; the individual-level difference within areas is about zero. Right: a constructed series. The true number of offences, which a stable victimisation survey tracks, falls by a quarter over 20 years. Recorded crime rises in the middle years because the reporting rate rises, then drops when a recording rule changes. It ends 16% down, against a true fall of 25%, and its path in between looks nothing like the true one."
#| code-fold: true
#| code-summary: "Show the simulation code"

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(14)
fig, (axL, axR) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(9.4, 3.9))

# Left: ecological fallacy. Offending depends on area deprivation, not on group.
J, m = 60, 2000
share_A = rng.uniform(0.05, 0.65, J)
depriv = np.clip(0.8 * share_A + rng.normal(0, 0.08, J), 0, 1)
rate, gaps = np.empty(J), np.empty(J)
for j in range(J):
    is_A = rng.random(m) < share_A[j]
    offend = rng.random(m) < 0.02 + 0.08 * depriv[j]     # same for A and B
    rate[j] = 1000 * offend.mean()
    gaps[j] = 1000 * (offend[is_A].mean() - offend[~is_A].mean())
b1, b0 = np.polyfit(share_A, rate, 1)
xs = np.linspace(0.03, 0.67, 2)
axL.scatter(100 * share_A, rate, s=16, color="#b7b6a8", lw=0)
axL.plot(100 * xs, b0 + b1 * xs, color="#161613", lw=1.8)
axL.text(4, rate.max() * 0.97,
         f"area slope: +{b1 / 100:.2f} per 1,000\nper point of group-A share\n\n"
         f"mean A−B gap within areas:\n{gaps.mean():+.1f} per 1,000",
         fontsize=8, va="top", color="#161613")
axL.set_title("Areas correlate; individuals don't", fontsize=10)
axL.set_xlabel("group-A share of area residents (%)")
axL.set_ylabel("offenders per 1,000 residents")

# Right: recorded = true offences x P(reported) x P(recorded)
years = np.arange(2000, 2020)
true = 100 * np.linspace(1.0, 0.75, years.size)
report = np.interp(years, [2000, 2007, 2011, 2019], [0.40, 0.40, 0.56, 0.56])
record = np.where(years >= 2014, 0.80, 1.0)
recorded = true * report * record
recorded = 100 * recorded / recorded[0]
axR.plot(years, true, color="#161613", lw=2, label="true offences (survey tracks this)")
axR.plot(years, recorded, color="#e8531d", lw=2.2, label="police-recorded crime")
axR.annotate("reporting rate\nrises 40% → 56%", xy=(2009, recorded[9]), xytext=(2001.0, 122),
             fontsize=8, color="#5e5e54", arrowprops=dict(arrowstyle="-", color="#5e5e54", lw=0.8))
axR.annotate("recording rule\nchanges", xy=(2014, recorded[14]), xytext=(2015.2, 122),
             fontsize=8, color="#5e5e54", arrowprops=dict(arrowstyle="-", color="#5e5e54", lw=0.8))
axR.set_ylim(60, 135)
axR.set_xticks([2000, 2005, 2010, 2015, 2019])
axR.set_title("A trend made by the recording process", fontsize=10)
axR.set_xlabel("year")
axR.set_ylabel("index (2000 = 100)")
axR.legend(fontsize=8, frameon=False, loc="lower left")

fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

## Associations in official data, and the ecological fallacy

The problems extend from the counts to the **associations** drawn from them. Suppose recorded crime is
higher in areas with a particular ethnic or class composition. Bryman gives two reasons not to conclude
that ethnicity or class is related to offending [@clark_bryman_2021].

The first is **validity**. Recorded differences can come from the recording process itself: members of
the public reporting differently depending on who the suspect is, patrols concentrated in some areas,
discretion exercised unevenly, and less investigation of crimes linked to advantage, such as
white-collar crime. Institutional racism has been documented in UK police forces (MacPherson 1999)
[@clark_bryman_2021].

The second is the **ecological fallacy**: inferring something about **individuals** from **aggregated
data**, in which groups of observations have been replaced by summary statistics [@clark_bryman_2021].
The left panel of the figure shows how. Area offending rises steeply with the share of group A, yet
within every area the two groups offend at the same rate. The area-level relationship is real, but it
comes from something that varies *between areas* (here, deprivation) and says nothing about whether
individual members of group A behave differently. A [Simpson's-paradox](../01-foundations/famous-paradoxes.qmd)
reversal is the same problem in its most extreme form.

::: {.callout-note title="'You are where you live'? Low-participation neighbourhoods"}
UK universities and government use **low participation neighbourhoods** (LPNs), areas defined from
historic aggregate data as places where young people are unlikely to enter higher education, to target
disadvantaged applicants. Harrison and McCaig (2015) checked this against a range of official data.
**More disadvantaged families live outside LPNs than inside them**, and LPNs contain a
higher-than-expected share of relatively advantaged families. Targeting by area slightly improves the
odds of "finding" the right students, but the label lacks "the predictive power that one might assume"
[@clark_bryman_2021]. An area statistic is a weak proxy for an individual's circumstances.
:::

None of this is a reason to avoid official statistics. Bryman's position is to use them, state their
limitations in the write-up, and take those limitations into account in the claims you make. He also
points out that reliability and validity are questions for *every* data source, not only official
ones [@clark_bryman_2021].

## Big Data: non-reactive, but not representative

"Big Data" is a catch-all for data sets too large to process by conventional means, and sometimes for
the predictive analytics run on them: loyalty-card transactions, wearables, mobile transactions,
automated cameras (Kitchin 2014). In social research so far it has mostly meant **social media**
[@clark_bryman_2021]. Sloan (2017) names three properties that create the challenges: the **size** of the
data, the **speed** at which it is produced, and its **variety** of forms (text, images, audio, video,
hyperlinks) [@clark_bryman_2021].

There are two main ways to analyse it [@clark_bryman_2021]:

- **Content.** What the posts say, often on a sample. Examples include Humphreys et al. (2014) on the
  personal information people reveal on Twitter, Greaves et al. (2014) on tweets to NHS hospitals, and
  Bharti et al. (2016) on sarcasm. Turning text into variables is covered in
  [text as data](../04-predictive/text-as-data.qmd).
- **Structure and process.** How information flows. Tinati et al. (2014) built a tool to follow
  **12,831 tweets from 4,737 users** about the 2011 #feesprotest against higher English tuition fees
  (8 October – 21 November). It showed which users were most influential, how content shifted from calls
  to participate towards complaints about policing, and that some users who wrote little themselves
  mattered a great deal because they retweeted regularly. This kind of analysis needs more specialist
  skills, such as [network methods](../complexity/index.qmd).

Scourfield et al. (2019) give a **comparative** design. They identified every death by suicide or road
traffic accident of an 11–18-year-old in England reported in newspapers from February to July 2014, then
searched a database of all tweets worldwide for the names of the deceased. Suicides drew more tweets than
newspaper reports, Twitter and newspaper coverage were more closely correlated for suicides than for road
deaths, and bullying-related suicides were especially newsworthy [@clark_bryman_2021].

Tinati et al. also criticise the field. Researchers often **shrink** Big Data to manageable size, for
example by keeping only the largest US police departments' Facebook pages or by sampling a few tweets,
and so analyse it with methods "that cannot explore" its scale, proportionality, dynamism and
relationality [@clark_bryman_2021].

Big Data shares the main strength of official statistics: it was not generated for research, so it is
**non-reactive**. One of Bryman's student panellists valued Twitter data because it "was not subject to
any researcher biases" [@clark_bryman_2021]. That is true of *reactivity*, but it says nothing about
*who* is in the data. Sloan's census comparison above shows that Twitter users differ from the
population by sex, age and class. Unlike archived surveys, platform data usually needs extensive
organising before analysis, and it raises consent, confidentiality and data-protection questions (in
Europe, under the GDPR) [@clark_bryman_2021]. On those, see
[research integrity & data ethics](../06-field-realities/research-integrity-and-data-ethics.qmd).

## Common pitfalls

::: {.callout-warning title="Where this goes wrong"}
- **Reading a recorded count as a count of events.** Recorded crime is the end of a long filtering
  process, and the claimant count is a count of claims. Ask what an event has to go through to be
  recorded, and whether any of those steps changed during your period. [@clark_bryman_2021]
- **Reading a break in a series as a change in behaviour.** Crackdowns, new legal definitions and new
  counting rules all move official series. Check the series' methodological notes before interpreting a
  jump, and compare with a series whose method stayed fixed, such as the victimisation survey.
  [@clark_bryman_2021]
- **Inferring individual behaviour from area data.** That is the ecological fallacy. An area-level
  correlation can arise entirely from what varies between areas. If the question is about individuals,
  you need individual-level data. [@clark_bryman_2021]
- **Treating "non-reactive" as "unbiased".** Official records and social media avoid reactivity, but
  they were made for a purpose and cover only some people: those an agency processes, or those who post.
  Sloan's Twitter profile is a warning against generalising from platform users to the population.
  [@clark_bryman_2021]
- **Committing to a data set before checking its variables.** If a key concept or confounder is not
  measured, no analysis can recover it. Read the documentation and the questionnaire first.
  [@clark_bryman_2021]
- **Analysing a complex survey as a simple random sample.** National surveys are stratified, clustered
  and weighted. Ignoring the design gives wrong standard errors and, without weights, possibly wrong
  estimates. See [complex survey estimation](../02-inference/complex-survey-estimation.qmd).
:::

## Appendix · the mathematics {.unnumbered}

::: {.math-appendix}

**Why an area slope is not an individual slope.** Let person $i$ in area $j$ have outcome
$y_{ij} = \alpha + \beta x_{ij} + \gamma \bar x_j + e_{ij}$, where $x_{ij}$ is an individual
characteristic (1 if in group A) and $\bar x_j$ its area mean. Here $\beta$ is the individual-level
effect and $\gamma$ a **contextual** effect of the area's composition, or of anything correlated with
it (deprivation, in the figure). Averaging within areas gives
$\bar y_j = \alpha + (\beta + \gamma)\,\bar x_j + \bar e_j$. A regression on area data therefore
estimates $\beta + \gamma$. It recovers the individual effect only if $\gamma = 0$, and nothing in the
aggregate data can tell you whether it is. In the figure $\beta = 0$, and the whole area slope is
$\gamma$. A within-area comparison, which [fixed effects](../05-causal-inference/panel-and-fixed-effects.qmd)
generalise, removes $\gamma \bar x_j$ and isolates $\beta$.

**Why a recorded series confounds three trends.** If recorded offences are
$R_t = N_t \cdot p^{\text{report}}_t \cdot p^{\text{record}}_t$, then

$$
\Delta \log R_t = \Delta \log N_t + \Delta \log p^{\text{report}}_t + \Delta \log p^{\text{record}}_t .
$$

The recorded trend equals the true trend only when the reporting and recording rates are constant. In
the figure, $N$ falls 25% while $p^{\text{report}}$ rises from 0.40 to 0.56 ($+40\%$) and
$p^{\text{record}}$ falls 20%, so $R$ ends at $0.75 \times 1.40 \times 0.80 = 0.84$ of its starting
level. A victimisation survey measures $N_t$ directly, which is why its trend is the more trustworthy one.

**Panel attrition compounds.** USoc kept 27,193 of 40,172 people from wave 1 to wave 4, a
three-interval retention of 0.677. If retention were the same at each interval, it would be
$0.677^{1/3} \approx 0.878$ per wave (our arithmetic). Small per-wave losses add up, and when dropping out
is related to the outcome, the survivors are a [selected sample](../03-regression/censoring-truncation-and-selection.qmd).

:::
