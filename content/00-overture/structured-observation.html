Structured Observation: Counting What People Do, Not What They Say

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source

Instead of asking people about their behaviour, watch it and code it into categories fixed in advance. It avoids the gap between what people say and what they do, but it brings its own problems: sampling moments as well as people, observers who disagree, and people who behave differently when watched.

Structured observation records behaviour directly, using an observation schedule: a fixed set of categories and explicit rules for when and how to code each one. It gets round the gap between stated and actual behaviour, and it can time and order events in a way no questionnaire can. In exchange, you have to sample moments and settings as carefully as people, train observers until they agree, worry that being watched changes the behaviour, and accept that the method sees what people do but not why.

Why it matters

Surveys measure what people say they do, and the asking questions entry shows how far that can be from what they actually do. The classic case is LaPiere’s 1930s tour with a Chinese couple: almost no hotel or restaurant turned them away, yet more than 90% of those that answered a later questionnaire said they would refuse Chinese guests (Clark et al. 2021).

A recent example makes the same point with numbers on both sides. Abramova et al. (2017) studied phubbing (ignoring the people around you to attend to your phone) among students at a German university. Two observers sat behind pre-selected seats in a lecture hall and recorded each phone episode: when it started and ended, what kind it was (texting, browsing and so on), and how neighbours reacted. They made 60 observations, averaging 1 hour 22 minutes each. The typical student phubbed about eight times per lecture, and over 90% both texted and browsed. After the lecture, 56 of the students estimated their own phone time. Only 39.3% were within five minutes of the observed figure; 21.4% overestimated and 41.1% underestimated, some by 10–20 minutes (Clark et al. 2021). (The three shares as printed add to 101.8%, so treat them as approximate.)

Bryman’s explanation for gaps like these is the usual list: answers that are socially desirable, especially when the survey is not anonymous, and memory that fades with time. Covert observation avoids both (Clark et al. 2021). For overt behaviour where meaning matters less, Bryman judges structured observation “almost certainly” more accurate than self-report (Clark et al. 2021).

What kind of observation is this?

Observation in social research comes in several forms (Clark et al. 2021):

  • Structured (or systematic) observation follows explicit rules for observing and recording. Every participant is observed for a set period using the same rules. It is deductive: the researcher decides in advance which behaviours to count. The data look like questionnaire data (each observed aspect of behaviour becomes a variable), and the design is usually cross-sectional.
  • Participant observation means joining a group or setting for a long time to understand the meanings its members give to what they do. It is unstructured and belongs to qualitative research.
  • Non-participant observation means watching without taking part. Structured observers are usually non-participants.
  • Unstructured observation records behaviour in as much detail as possible, to build a narrative rather than a count.
  • Simple and contrived observation (Webb et al. 1966) are both unobtrusive. In simple observation the observer has no influence on the situation. In contrived observation the observer changes the situation to see what happens, which leads to the field stimulations below.

This entry is about the first kind. It is the observational counterpart of a closed-question questionnaire and of content analysis, which applies the same logic to documents (Clark et al. 2021).

The observation schedule

The observation schedule (or coding scheme) lists the categories of behaviour and the rules for assigning behaviour to them. The best-known example is the Flanders Interaction Analysis Categories (FIAC) for classrooms. Every three seconds, the observer writes down the number of the category that describes what is happening (teacher lecturing, teacher praising, pupil responding, silence or confusion, and so on), which gives 20 codes a minute (Clark et al. 2021). From those codes you can compare how much teachers talk relative to pupils, how much time is silent or confused, and how classes and teaching styles differ.

Bryman sketches a schedule for a university tutorial with 13 categories: seven for the tutor (asking a question of the group or of one student, responding to a question or a comment, discussing the topic, making arrangements, silence) and six for students. Coding every five seconds gives a coding sheet in which each row of 12 cells is one minute (Clark et al. 2021). Time spent on each activity can then be related to group size, room layout, subject, or the tutor’s age and gender.

Five principles for designing a schedule (Clark et al. 2021):

  1. Say exactly who or what the observer is to watch.
  2. Say exactly which behaviours to record.
  3. Make categories mutually exclusive (no overlaps) and inclusive (cover all likely behaviour). Bryman’s example: a student knocks on the door with a quick question. None of the 13 categories fits, so the observer must guess whether to stop coding. The fix is an “interruption” category.
  4. Keep the recording system easy to operate. Many fine-grained categories make the observer’s choices slower and the record worse.
  5. Give clear guidance wherever the observer must interpret, for instance to tell “responding to another student” from “discussing the topic”.

Bryman also advises a period of unstructured observation before writing the schedule, then piloting it, so the categories match the setting rather than the researcher’s assumptions about it (Clark et al. 2021).

A worked schedule: class size and attention. Blatchford et al. (2003) asked whether larger classes make young children (aged 4–5) less attentive. Observers watched one child at a time, on five-minute sheets divided into continuous ten-second intervals. The categories described three “social modes” (with the teacher, with other children, not interacting), and within each mode whether the child was working, dealing with procedure, socialising or off task. Six children were chosen at random in each class (three boys and three girls), to be observed five times a day on three days. In practice each child got about 14 sheets, or 69 minutes of observation (Clark et al. 2021). (Fourteen five-minute sheets is 70 minutes, so the two figures agree.)

Strategies for recording

There are three ways to decide when to record (Clark et al. 2021):

  • Incidents. Wait for something to happen and record what follows. LaPiere waited for the couple to ask for a room or a table and recorded whether they were served.
  • Short periods. Observe for short, fixed spells, as in Blatchford’s five-minute sheets. Returning at several times (time sampling) helps show whether what you saw is typical.
  • Continuous recording. Watch and code more or less continuously for long periods, as with FIAC. Martin and Bateson (2007) note that this gives both the frequency and the duration of behaviours, and contrast it with time sampling, where observations are taken at different (possibly random) times.

Bryman’s warning about time sampling is concrete. If the managers you are studying meet every day at 10 a.m. and that is when you always observe, you will conclude that managing consists of meetings (Clark et al. 2021). The figure shows how bad this gets and what random timing buys.

The fixed-time observer is not unlucky. They are certain to be wrong, and more observation at the same time of day makes them more confident, not more correct. Random moments fix the bias. With five moments a day for a week the 90% range is roughly 8% to 40%, and with 20 a day about 14% to 34%. Beyond that, extra moments buy little, because the remaining uncertainty comes from which days were observed. The cure for that is more days, which is the logic of Blatchford’s design (each child observed on three different days) (Clark et al. 2021).

Sampling: people, times and places

Sampling in structured observation is about more than people (Clark et al. 2021).

  • People. Where a sampling frame exists, sample at random, possibly within strata, as Blatchford did with three boys and three girls per class (sampling & survey design).
  • Times. If individuals are observed several times, choose the times at random, so nobody is always seen at the end of the day when they are tired. Observing on different days keeps one unusual day from dominating a child’s record.
  • No frame. Passers-by in a street or meetings between social workers and clients cannot be listed in advance, so probability sampling is not possible.
  • The calendar. Even a random sample is only representative of the period observed. Schools near exam time behave differently from schools in October.
  • Sites. Access to schools and firms is hard to get, so the sites that agree are unlikely to be representative of all such sites.

Martin and Bateson (2007) distinguish four ways of choosing whom to watch at a given moment (Clark et al. 2021):

Method What the observer does Example in Bryman
Ad libitum records whatever is happening FIAC
Focal follows one individual for a set period and records every relevant behaviour Blatchford; Abramova
Scan scans the whole group at intervals and records what each person is doing (only one or two behaviours are practical) —
Behaviour watches the whole group and records who does a particular kind of thing —

Most structured observation uses the first two (Clark et al. 2021).

Field stimulations: intervene, then observe

A field stimulation (Salancik’s 1979 term) is a study in which the researcher intervenes in a natural setting and records what happens, without the participants knowing they are being studied (Clark et al. 2021). That removes reactivity, but it raises ethical questions, chiefly deception (the best-known case is Rosenhan’s 1973 pseudo-patients in mental hospitals), and it limits coding, because an observer who keeps consulting a schedule gives the game away (Clark et al. 2021).

Bryman’s example is Guéguen and Jacob’s (2012) study of waitresses’ lipstick. In three French restaurants, diners were randomly assigned to one of seven waitresses wearing red, pink, brown or no lipstick, observed over 40 periods (five days a week for eight weeks). When a woman paid, tipping did not differ. When a man paid, 50.6% tipped the waitress wearing red, against 39.7%, 34.5% and 30.3% for pink, brown and none, and tips for red were 50% larger than for no lipstick (Clark et al. 2021). Because diners were assigned at random, this is a randomised field experiment with an observational outcome. The usual experimental cautions apply, including the one in the pitfalls below about reading “significant for men, not for women” as a difference between men and women.

Analysing the counts

The analysis is ordinary quantitative analysis of a cross-section: descriptive statistics first, then tests of association where the sample allows (Clark et al. 2021). Nosowska et al. (2014) observed older people in public spaces in six cities in developing countries (Syria, Jordan, Egypt, Thailand, Kenya and Tanzania) and in Sheffield. Of the 310 older people observed, 184 (59.4%) were men and 126 (40.6%) women. Men outnumbered women in public everywhere except Sheffield. A test of association found that whether older men were accompanied differed significantly by city, with Sheffield highest at 42.0% (Clark et al. 2021).

The study is also a lesson in measurement. Observers recorded anyone who looked over 60 as an older person. Apparent age is unreliable, more so from a distance, and 60 means something different in countries with very different life expectancies (Clark et al. 2021). Every coded variable in an observation study is an operational definition with this kind of gap between the concept and the indicator.

Strengths and limits

Strengths. McCall (1984), comparing structured observation with interviews and questionnaires, credits it with more reliable information about events; more precise timing, duration and frequency; more accurate time order between variables; and more accurate and economical reconstruction of large-scale social episodes (Clark et al. 2021). It can also measure the gap between stated and observed behaviour, and it works best alongside other methods. The phubbing study added questionnaires, and the class-size study was one part of a larger project with teacher questionnaires each term, end-of-year questionnaires and case studies with interviews (Clark et al. 2021). (See mixed-methods designs.)

Limits (Clark et al. 2021):

  • It sees behaviour, not reasons. When intentions matter, observers have to impute them: FIAC’s “teacher praises or encourages” assumes a motive, and a later Blatchford study coded “off-task (passive)” behaviour such as day-dreaming. Surveys, for all their faults, can ask about attitudes, backgrounds and reasons.
  • Some settings are closed. Intimate behaviour, confidential meetings, police stations and detention centres are hard or impossible to observe.
  • Reliability is work. Codes must agree between observers (inter-observer consistency) and for each observer over time (intra-observer consistency). Fatigue and lapses of attention erode both. But trained observers can use complex schedules reliably (Delamont and Hamilton 1984), and the agreement can be measured with the statistics described in content analysis.
  • Validity has two parts. Does the category capture the concept, and is the schedule applied as intended? If observers vary, the measure is unreliable and so cannot be valid.
  • Being watched changes behaviour. This is the reactive effect, discussed next.
  • The schedule can impose the wrong frame. Like closed questions, fixed categories can miss what matters in an unfamiliar setting. Unstructured observation first is the remedy.
  • Data come in fragments. Coding activity into short segments can hide things that run through many of them. Studies of managers that find little planning (Mintzberg 1973) may reflect the method, since planning is spread across many activities (Snyder and Glueck 1980).
  • Context is left out. A teacher’s background and experience shape their teaching but appear nowhere in the codes unless collected separately.
  • Ethics. Is permission needed from whoever controls the setting, as Blatchford needed from schools? Must the people observed be told? Covert observation involves deception. Consent is sometimes impossible to obtain (crowds, shoppers), and then the ethics application must explain the decision and assess the risks (ethics).

The reactive effect. Webb et al. (1966) described the “reactive measurement effect”: knowing they are being studied can change what subjects do. They named four components (Clark et al. 2021):

  1. The guinea-pig effect: awareness of being tested, for example a wish to make a good impression.
  2. Role selection: subjects look for cues about the study’s aims and play the part they think is wanted.
  3. Measurement as a change agent: the observer’s presence changes the setting itself (a stranger occupies a chair in the corner of the classroom).
  4. Response sets: consistent but inappropriate answering, such as social desirability or yea-saying. This mostly affects questionnaires and interviews.

McCall reports evidence that reactivity occurs, but also that people get used to being observed, so the observer becomes less intrusive over time (Clark et al. 2021). Webb et al. argued for more unobtrusive measures, of which existing data are one kind (Clark et al. 2021).

Common pitfalls

WarningWhere this goes wrong
  • Observing at the same time every day. A fixed schedule turns a daily routine into the whole picture. Randomise observation times, and spread them over days. (Clark et al. 2021)
  • Adding moments when you need more days. Within-day sampling error falls quickly. What remains is day-to-day variation, which only more days (or settings) can reduce. (Our simulation.)
  • Writing the schedule before you know the setting. Do some unstructured observation first, then pilot. Categories that aren’t inclusive leave observers to improvise. (Clark et al. 2021)
  • Imputing motives and calling it observation. “Praises”, “off task” and “day-dreaming” are interpretations. Give rules for them, and test whether observers agree. (Clark et al. 2021)
  • Reporting one observer’s codes without a reliability check. Measure inter-observer agreement with a chance-corrected statistic, and re-check one observer’s consistency over time. (Clark et al. 2021)
  • Treating apparent attributes as measured ones. Age, class or ethnicity judged by eye are operational guesses (Nosowska’s “looks over 60”). Report the rule and its likely errors. (Clark et al. 2021)
  • Reading “significant for one group, not for the other” as a group difference. Significant for male payers and not for female payers is not itself a test of whether the effect differs by sex; test the interaction (comparing groups). (Our caution, not Bryman’s.)
  • Forgetting that a field stimulation is a deception. Avoiding reactivity does not remove the duty to justify covert intervention to an ethics committee. (Clark et al. 2021)

Appendix · the mathematics

Momentary time sampling. Let B(t)\in\{0,1\} indicate that the behaviour is happening at time t in an observation window of length T. The quantity of interest is the share of time p = \frac{1}{T}\int_0^T B(t)\,dt. Observing at n independent uniformly random moments t_1,\dots,t_n gives \hat p = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^n B(t_j),\qquad E[\hat p] = p,\qquad \mathrm{Var}(\hat p) = \frac{p(1-p)}{n}, because each B(t_j) is a Bernoulli(p) draw. Observing at a fixed time t_0 instead estimates \Pr\{B(t_0)=1\}, with bias \Pr\{B(t_0)=1\} - p, which no number of repeat visits removes. In the figure, \Pr\{B(10{:}15)=1\} = 1 while p \approx 0.23.

Moments versus days. If day d has its own share p_d with mean p and variance \sigma^2_{\text{day}}, and you observe k random moments on each of D days, then \mathrm{Var}(\hat p) = \frac{E[p_d(1-p_d)]}{kD} + \frac{\sigma^2_{\text{day}}}{D}. The first term falls with moments per day, the second only with days. Continuous recording (k\to\infty) leaves \sigma^2_{\text{day}}/D, the floor shaded in the figure. With p\approx 0.23 and a between-day SD of about 0.10 in the simulation, five days give a floor SD of about 0.10/\sqrt5 \approx 0.044, a 90% range of roughly \pm 0.07, which matches the shaded band.

Interval coding. Schedules like FIAC (every 3 seconds) or Blatchford’s (every 10 seconds) record one code per interval. The share of intervals coded c estimates the share of time in c when intervals are short compared with episodes. When episodes are shorter than the interval, they are under-counted or missed. Continuous recording of start and end times, as in the phubbing study, gives frequency and duration directly.

::: {.lead-kernel}
Structured observation records **behaviour directly**, using an observation schedule: a fixed set of
categories and explicit rules for when and how to code each one. It gets round the gap between stated
and actual behaviour, and it can time and order events in a way no questionnaire can. In exchange, you
have to sample *moments and settings* as carefully as people, train observers until they agree, worry
that being watched changes the behaviour, and accept that the method sees what people do but not why.

## Why it matters

Surveys measure what people *say* they do, and the [asking questions](asking-questions.qmd) entry shows
how far that can be from what they actually do. The classic case is LaPiere's 1930s tour with a Chinese
couple: almost no hotel or restaurant turned them away, yet more than 90% of those that answered a later
questionnaire said they would refuse Chinese guests [@clark_bryman_2021].

A recent example makes the same point with numbers on both sides. Abramova et al. (2017) studied
**phubbing** (ignoring the people around you to attend to your phone) among students at a German
university. Two observers sat behind pre-selected seats in a lecture hall and recorded each phone
episode: when it started and ended, what kind it was (texting, browsing and so on), and how neighbours
reacted. They made 60 observations, averaging 1 hour 22 minutes each. The typical student phubbed about
eight times per lecture, and over 90% both texted and browsed. After the lecture, 56 of the students
estimated their own phone time. Only 39.3% were within five minutes of the observed figure; 21.4%
overestimated and 41.1% underestimated, some by 10–20 minutes [@clark_bryman_2021]. (The three shares as
printed add to 101.8%, so treat them as approximate.)

Bryman's explanation for gaps like these is the usual list: answers that are socially desirable,
especially when the survey is not anonymous, and memory that fades with time. Covert observation
avoids both [@clark_bryman_2021]. For overt behaviour where meaning matters less, Bryman judges structured
observation "almost certainly" more accurate than self-report [@clark_bryman_2021].

## What kind of observation is this?

Observation in social research comes in several forms [@clark_bryman_2021]:

- **Structured** (or **systematic**) observation follows explicit rules for observing and recording.
  Every participant is observed for a set period using the same rules. It is deductive: the researcher
  decides in advance which behaviours to count. The data look like questionnaire data (each observed
  aspect of behaviour becomes a variable), and the design is usually cross-sectional.
- **Participant observation** means joining a group or setting for a long time to understand the meanings
  its members give to what they do. It is unstructured and belongs to
  [qualitative research](qualitative-evidence.qmd).
- **Non-participant observation** means watching without taking part. Structured observers are usually
  non-participants.
- **Unstructured observation** records behaviour in as much detail as possible, to build a narrative
  rather than a count.
- **Simple and contrived observation** (Webb et al. 1966) are both unobtrusive. In simple observation the
  observer has no influence on the situation. In contrived observation the observer changes the situation
  to see what happens, which leads to the field stimulations below.

This entry is about the first kind. It is the observational counterpart of a closed-question
questionnaire and of [content analysis](content-analysis.qmd), which applies the same logic to documents
[@clark_bryman_2021].

## The observation schedule

The **observation schedule** (or coding scheme) lists the categories of behaviour and the rules for
assigning behaviour to them. The best-known example is the **Flanders Interaction Analysis Categories
(FIAC)** for classrooms. Every three seconds, the observer writes down the number of the category that
describes what is happening (teacher lecturing, teacher praising, pupil responding, silence or
confusion, and so on), which gives 20 codes a minute [@clark_bryman_2021]. From those codes you can compare
how much teachers talk relative to pupils, how much time is silent or confused, and how classes and
teaching styles differ.

Bryman sketches a schedule for a university tutorial with 13 categories: seven for the tutor (asking a
question of the group or of one student, responding to a question or a comment, discussing the topic,
making arrangements, silence) and six for students. Coding every five seconds gives a coding sheet in
which each row of 12 cells is one minute [@clark_bryman_2021]. Time spent on each activity can then be
related to group size, room layout, subject, or the tutor's age and gender.

Five principles for designing a schedule [@clark_bryman_2021]:

1. Say exactly **who or what** the observer is to watch.
2. Say exactly **which behaviours** to record.
3. Make categories **mutually exclusive** (no overlaps) and **inclusive** (cover all likely behaviour).
   Bryman's example: a student knocks on the door with a quick question. None of the 13 categories fits,
   so the observer must guess whether to stop coding. The fix is an "interruption" category.
4. Keep the recording system **easy to operate**. Many fine-grained categories make the observer's choices
   slower and the record worse.
5. Give **clear guidance** wherever the observer must interpret, for instance to tell "responding to
   another student" from "discussing the topic".

Bryman also advises a period of **unstructured observation before writing the schedule**, then piloting it,
so the categories match the setting rather than the researcher's assumptions about it [@clark_bryman_2021].

**A worked schedule: class size and attention.** Blatchford et al. (2003) asked whether larger classes make
young children (aged 4–5) less attentive. Observers watched one child at a time, on five-minute sheets
divided into continuous ten-second intervals. The categories described three "social modes" (with the
teacher, with other children, not interacting), and within each mode whether the child was working,
dealing with procedure, socialising or off task. Six children were chosen at random in each class (three
boys and three girls), to be observed five times a day on three days. In practice each child got about 14
sheets, or 69 minutes of observation [@clark_bryman_2021]. (Fourteen five-minute sheets is 70 minutes, so
the two figures agree.)

## Strategies for recording

There are three ways to decide *when* to record [@clark_bryman_2021]:

- **Incidents.** Wait for something to happen and record what follows. LaPiere waited for the couple to
  ask for a room or a table and recorded whether they were served.
- **Short periods.** Observe for short, fixed spells, as in Blatchford's five-minute sheets. Returning at
  several times (**time sampling**) helps show whether what you saw is typical.
- **Continuous recording.** Watch and code more or less continuously for long periods, as with FIAC. Martin
  and Bateson (2007) note that this gives both the **frequency** and the **duration** of behaviours, and
  contrast it with time sampling, where observations are taken at different (possibly random) times.

Bryman's warning about time sampling is concrete. If the managers you are studying meet every day at
10 a.m. and that is when you always observe, you will conclude that managing consists of meetings
[@clark_bryman_2021]. The figure shows how bad this gets and what random timing buys.

```{python}
#| label: fig-observation-timing
#| fig-cap: "Sampling moments, simulated (our own illustration of Bryman's 10 a.m. warning). Each working day a manager has a fixed 10:00–10:45 meeting plus a random number of other meetings, which take about 23% of the day on average. Left: three sample days, with the fixed 10:15 observation time (orange) and ten random observation moments (grey ticks). Right: estimates of the share of time in meetings from one five-day week. Observing at 10:15 every day gives 100%. Random moments are unbiased, and their 90% range narrows as moments are added, but it cannot get narrower than continuous recording of the same five days (shaded): the week itself varies."
#| code-fold: true

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(12)
L = 480                                    # minutes, 9:00-17:00

def workday():
    m = np.zeros(L, bool)
    m[60:105] = True                       # the fixed 10:00-10:45 meeting
    for _ in range(rng.poisson(2)):        # other meetings, 20-60 minutes, anywhere in the day
        d = rng.integers(20, 61)
        s = rng.integers(0, L - d)
        m[s:s + d] = True
    return m

days = np.array([workday() for _ in range(20000)])
truth = days.mean()
fixed_t = 75                               # 10:15
fixed_est = days[:, fixed_t].mean()

D, R = 5, 4000                             # days observed, simulated weeks
ks = np.array([1, 2, 5, 10, 20, 40])
mid, lo, hi = [], [], []
for k in ks:
    pick = rng.integers(0, len(days), (R, D))
    t = rng.integers(0, L, (R, D, k))
    est = days[pick[:, :, None], t].mean(axis=(1, 2))
    mid.append(est.mean()); a, b = np.percentile(est, [5, 95]); lo.append(a); hi.append(b)
mid, lo, hi = map(np.array, (mid, lo, hi))
cont = days[rng.integers(0, len(days), (R, D))].mean(axis=(1, 2))
c_lo, c_hi = np.percentile(cont, [5, 95])

fig, (axL, axR) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(9.6, 3.9), gridspec_kw={"width_ratios": [1.15, 1]})

for j in range(3):
    m = days[j]
    edges = np.flatnonzero(np.diff(np.r_[0, m.astype(int), 0]))
    for s, e in zip(edges[::2], edges[1::2]):
        axL.broken_barh([(s, e - s)], (j - 0.3, 0.6), color="#b7b6a8")
    ticks = np.sort(rng.integers(0, L, 10))
    axL.vlines(ticks, j - 0.42, j - 0.3, color="#5e5e54", lw=1.2)
axL.axvline(fixed_t, color="#e8531d", lw=2)
axL.text(fixed_t + 6, 2.55, "always observe at 10:15", color="#e8531d", fontsize=8)
axL.set_xticks(np.arange(0, L + 1, 60), [f"{9 + h}:00" for h in range(9)], fontsize=8)
axL.set_yticks([0, 1, 2], ["day 1", "day 2", "day 3"], fontsize=8.5)
axL.set_ylim(-0.6, 2.8)
axL.set_title("Meetings (shaded) and when the observer looks", fontsize=10)

axR.axhspan(100 * c_lo, 100 * c_hi, color="#b7b6a8", alpha=0.45, lw=0)
axR.axhline(100 * truth, color="#161613", lw=1.2)
axR.fill_between(ks, 100 * lo, 100 * hi, color="#5e5e54", alpha=0.25, lw=0)
axR.plot(ks, 100 * mid, color="#161613", lw=1.8, marker="o", ms=4)
axR.axhline(100 * fixed_est, color="#e8531d", lw=2, ls="--")
axR.text(1.05, 100 * fixed_est - 7, "fixed time each day: 100%", color="#e8531d", fontsize=8)
axR.text(40, 100 * truth + 1.5, f"true share {100 * truth:.0f}%", fontsize=8, ha="right")
axR.text(40, 100 * c_lo - 5, "continuous recording, 5 days", fontsize=8, ha="right", color="#5e5e54")
axR.set_xscale("log")
axR.set_xticks(ks, [str(k) for k in ks])
axR.set_ylim(0, 105)
axR.set_xlabel("random observation moments per day (5 days)")
axR.set_ylabel("estimated time in meetings (%)")
axR.set_title("Random moments are unbiased; fixed ones are not", fontsize=10)

fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

The fixed-time observer is not unlucky. They are certain to be wrong, and more observation at the same
time of day makes them more confident, not more correct. Random moments fix the bias. With five moments a
day for a week the 90% range is roughly 8% to 40%, and with 20 a day about 14% to 34%. Beyond that, extra
moments buy little, because the remaining uncertainty comes from which days were observed. The cure for that
is more days, which is the logic of Blatchford's design (each child observed on three different days)
[@clark_bryman_2021].

## Sampling: people, times and places

Sampling in structured observation is about more than people [@clark_bryman_2021].

- **People.** Where a sampling frame exists, sample at random, possibly within strata, as Blatchford did
  with three boys and three girls per class ([sampling & survey design](../02-inference/sampling-and-survey-design.qmd)).
- **Times.** If individuals are observed several times, choose the times at random, so nobody is always
  seen at the end of the day when they are tired. Observing on different days keeps one unusual day from
  dominating a child's record.
- **No frame.** Passers-by in a street or meetings between social workers and clients cannot be listed in
  advance, so probability sampling is not possible.
- **The calendar.** Even a random sample is only representative of the period observed. Schools near
  exam time behave differently from schools in October.
- **Sites.** Access to schools and firms is hard to get, so the sites that agree are unlikely to be
  representative of all such sites.

Martin and Bateson (2007) distinguish four ways of choosing whom to watch at a given moment
[@clark_bryman_2021]:

| Method | What the observer does | Example in Bryman |
|---|---|---|
| **Ad libitum** | records whatever is happening | FIAC |
| **Focal** | follows one individual for a set period and records every relevant behaviour | Blatchford; Abramova |
| **Scan** | scans the whole group at intervals and records what each person is doing (only one or two behaviours are practical) | — |
| **Behaviour** | watches the whole group and records who does a particular kind of thing | — |

Most structured observation uses the first two [@clark_bryman_2021].

## Field stimulations: intervene, then observe

A **field stimulation** (Salancik's 1979 term) is a study in which the researcher intervenes in a natural
setting and records what happens, without the participants knowing they are being studied
[@clark_bryman_2021]. That removes reactivity, but it raises ethical questions, chiefly deception (the
best-known case is Rosenhan's 1973 pseudo-patients in mental hospitals), and it limits coding, because an
observer who keeps consulting a schedule gives the game away [@clark_bryman_2021].

Bryman's example is Guéguen and Jacob's (2012) study of waitresses' lipstick. In three French restaurants,
diners were randomly assigned to one of seven waitresses wearing red, pink, brown or no lipstick, observed
over 40 periods (five days a week for eight weeks). When a woman paid, tipping did not differ. When a man
paid, 50.6% tipped the waitress wearing red, against 39.7%, 34.5% and 30.3% for pink, brown and none, and
tips for red were 50% larger than for no lipstick [@clark_bryman_2021]. Because diners were assigned at
random, this is a [randomised field experiment](../05-causal-inference/randomized-experiments.qmd) with an
observational outcome. The usual experimental cautions apply, including the one in the pitfalls below
about reading "significant for men, not for women" as a difference between men and women.

## Analysing the counts

The analysis is ordinary quantitative analysis of a cross-section: descriptive statistics first, then
tests of association where the sample allows [@clark_bryman_2021]. Nosowska et al. (2014) observed older
people in public spaces in six cities in developing countries (Syria, Jordan, Egypt, Thailand, Kenya and
Tanzania) and in Sheffield. Of the 310 older people observed, 184 (59.4%) were men and 126 (40.6%) women.
Men outnumbered women in public everywhere except Sheffield. A test of association found that whether older
men were accompanied differed significantly by city, with Sheffield highest at 42.0% [@clark_bryman_2021].

The study is also a lesson in measurement. Observers recorded anyone who *looked* over 60 as an older
person. Apparent age is unreliable, more so from a distance, and 60 means something different in countries
with very different life expectancies [@clark_bryman_2021]. Every coded variable in an observation study is
an operational definition with this kind of gap between the
[concept and the indicator](concepts-and-measurement.qmd).

## Strengths and limits

**Strengths.** McCall (1984), comparing structured observation with interviews and questionnaires,
credits it with more reliable information about events; more precise timing, duration and frequency;
more accurate time order between variables; and more accurate and economical reconstruction of
large-scale social episodes [@clark_bryman_2021]. It can also measure the gap between stated and observed
behaviour, and it works best alongside other methods. The phubbing study added questionnaires, and the
class-size study was one part of a larger project with teacher questionnaires each term, end-of-year
questionnaires and case studies with interviews [@clark_bryman_2021]. (See
[mixed-methods designs](mixed-methods-designs.qmd).)

**Limits** [@clark_bryman_2021]:

- **It sees behaviour, not reasons.** When intentions matter, observers have to impute them: FIAC's
  "teacher praises or encourages" assumes a motive, and a later Blatchford study coded "off-task
  (passive)" behaviour such as day-dreaming. Surveys, for all their faults, can ask about attitudes,
  backgrounds and reasons.
- **Some settings are closed.** Intimate behaviour, confidential meetings, police stations and detention
  centres are hard or impossible to observe.
- **Reliability is work.** Codes must agree **between observers** (inter-observer consistency) and **for
  each observer over time** (intra-observer consistency). Fatigue and lapses of attention erode both. But
  trained observers can use complex schedules reliably (Delamont and Hamilton 1984), and the agreement can
  be measured with the statistics described in [content analysis](content-analysis.qmd).
- **Validity has two parts.** Does the category capture the concept, and is the schedule applied as
  intended? If observers vary, the measure is unreliable and so cannot be valid.
- **Being watched changes behaviour.** This is the reactive effect, discussed next.
- **The schedule can impose the wrong frame.** Like closed questions, fixed categories can miss what
  matters in an unfamiliar setting. Unstructured observation first is the remedy.
- **Data come in fragments.** Coding activity into short segments can hide things that run through many
  of them. Studies of managers that find little planning (Mintzberg 1973) may reflect the method, since
  planning is spread across many activities (Snyder and Glueck 1980).
- **Context is left out.** A teacher's background and experience shape their teaching but appear nowhere in
  the codes unless collected separately.
- **Ethics.** Is permission needed from whoever controls the setting, as Blatchford needed from schools? Must
  the people observed be told? Covert observation involves deception. Consent is sometimes impossible to
  obtain (crowds, shoppers), and then the ethics application must explain the decision and assess the risks
  ([ethics](ethics-and-human-subjects.qmd)).

**The reactive effect.** Webb et al. (1966) described the "reactive measurement effect": knowing they are
being studied can change what subjects do. They named four components [@clark_bryman_2021]:

1. **The guinea-pig effect:** awareness of being tested, for example a wish to make a good impression.
2. **Role selection:** subjects look for cues about the study's aims and play the part they think is wanted.
3. **Measurement as a change agent:** the observer's presence changes the setting itself (a stranger
   occupies a chair in the corner of the classroom).
4. **Response sets:** consistent but inappropriate answering, such as social desirability or yea-saying.
   This mostly affects questionnaires and interviews.

McCall reports evidence that reactivity occurs, but also that people get used to being observed, so the
observer becomes less intrusive over time [@clark_bryman_2021]. Webb et al. argued for more **unobtrusive
measures**, of which [existing data](using-existing-data.qmd) are one kind [@clark_bryman_2021].

## Common pitfalls

::: {.callout-warning title="Where this goes wrong"}
- **Observing at the same time every day.** A fixed schedule turns a daily routine into the whole picture.
  Randomise observation times, and spread them over days. [@clark_bryman_2021]
- **Adding moments when you need more days.** Within-day sampling error falls quickly. What remains is
  day-to-day variation, which only more days (or settings) can reduce. (Our simulation.)
- **Writing the schedule before you know the setting.** Do some unstructured observation first, then pilot.
  Categories that aren't inclusive leave observers to improvise. [@clark_bryman_2021]
- **Imputing motives and calling it observation.** "Praises", "off task" and "day-dreaming" are
  interpretations. Give rules for them, and test whether observers agree. [@clark_bryman_2021]
- **Reporting one observer's codes without a reliability check.** Measure inter-observer agreement with a
  chance-corrected statistic, and re-check one observer's consistency over time. [@clark_bryman_2021]
- **Treating apparent attributes as measured ones.** Age, class or ethnicity judged by eye are
  operational guesses (Nosowska's "looks over 60"). Report the rule and its likely errors.
  [@clark_bryman_2021]
- **Reading "significant for one group, not for the other" as a group difference.** Significant for male
  payers and not for female payers is not itself a test of whether the effect differs by sex; test the
  interaction ([comparing groups](../02-inference/comparing-groups.qmd)). (Our caution, not Bryman's.)
- **Forgetting that a field stimulation is a deception.** Avoiding reactivity does not remove the duty to
  justify covert intervention to an ethics committee. [@clark_bryman_2021]
:::

## Appendix · the mathematics {.unnumbered}

::: {.math-appendix}

**Momentary time sampling.** Let $B(t)\in\{0,1\}$ indicate that the behaviour is happening at time $t$ in an
observation window of length $T$. The quantity of interest is the share of time
$p = \frac{1}{T}\int_0^T B(t)\,dt$. Observing at $n$ independent uniformly random moments $t_1,\dots,t_n$ gives
$$
\hat p = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^n B(t_j),\qquad E[\hat p] = p,\qquad \mathrm{Var}(\hat p) = \frac{p(1-p)}{n},
$$
because each $B(t_j)$ is a Bernoulli($p$) draw. Observing at a fixed time $t_0$ instead estimates
$\Pr\{B(t_0)=1\}$, with bias $\Pr\{B(t_0)=1\} - p$, which no number of repeat visits removes. In the figure,
$\Pr\{B(10{:}15)=1\} = 1$ while $p \approx 0.23$.

**Moments versus days.** If day $d$ has its own share $p_d$ with mean $p$ and variance $\sigma^2_{\text{day}}$, and
you observe $k$ random moments on each of $D$ days, then
$$
\mathrm{Var}(\hat p) = \frac{E[p_d(1-p_d)]}{kD} + \frac{\sigma^2_{\text{day}}}{D}.
$$
The first term falls with moments per day, the second only with days. Continuous recording ($k\to\infty$) leaves
$\sigma^2_{\text{day}}/D$, the floor shaded in the figure. With $p\approx 0.23$ and a between-day SD of about 0.10 in
the simulation, five days give a floor SD of about $0.10/\sqrt5 \approx 0.044$, a 90% range of roughly
$\pm 0.07$, which matches the shaded band.

**Interval coding.** Schedules like FIAC (every 3 seconds) or Blatchford's (every 10 seconds) record one code per
interval. The share of intervals coded $c$ estimates the share of time in $c$ when intervals are short compared
with episodes. When episodes are shorter than the interval, they are under-counted or missed. Continuous
recording of start and end times, as in the phubbing study, gives frequency and duration directly.

:::
