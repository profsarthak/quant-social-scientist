A Field Guide to Research Designs

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source

The design decides what you’re allowed to conclude — and no technique can rescue the wrong one.

A research design is the skeleton of a study: when you collect data, from whom, and with what comparison. It sets a hard ceiling on the strength of any conclusion you can draw. Choose it before you analyse anything, because the most sophisticated statistics in the world cannot buy back a causal claim a cross-sectional design was never able to make.

Why it matters

Beginners think credibility comes from the analysis. It comes from the design. The design determines whether you can say “X causes Y” or only “X and Y go together.” This entry is the map of the main designs and what each can — and cannot — license. It’s also the bridge to the summit of the manual: causal inference is, at heart, the art of designing (or reconstructing) a fair comparison.

The designs, and what each buys you

A research design reflects the priority you place on causal connection, generalisation, and understanding change over time (Bryman 2016).

Experimental

Manipulate an independent variable and use random assignment to make the treatment and control groups comparable.

  • Strengths: the benchmark for internal validity — it rules out alternative causal explanations. Field experiments, run in real settings, add ecological validity.
  • Limits: many social variables (gender, ethnicity, class) can’t be manipulated for ethical or practical reasons; lab experiments can suffer reactive effects when people know they’re studied, hurting generalisability.

Cross-sectional / survey

Data on many cases at a single point in time, to find patterns of association.

  • Strengths: ideal for non-manipulable variables (age, background); with random probability sampling, strong external validity.
  • Limits: weak internal validity — everything is measured at once, so the direction of causation is ambiguous. It shows relationships, not causes.

Longitudinal / panel

The same sample studied on ≥2 occasions (panel = same people; cohort = people sharing a trait, e.g. birth week).

  • Strengths: establishes temporal order, enabling stronger causal inference than cross-sectional designs; essential for charting social change.
  • Limits: expensive and slow; suffers attrition as participants drop out, which can make the survivors unrepresentative.

Comparative

Two or more contrasting cases (e.g. nations) studied with near-identical methods.

  • Strengths: reveals how a phenomenon varies across socio-cultural settings, guarding against findings that are culturally specific.
  • Limits: hard to ensure data are truly comparable across contexts (translation of instruments is a classic trap).

Case study

Intensive analysis of a single case — a community, organisation, or person.

  • Strengths: deep, idiographic insight; supports analytic generalisation (to theory, not population).
  • Limits: weak external validity — one case rarely represents a wider population.

The two rulers: internal vs external validity

Every design is judged on two different axes, and they often trade off:

  • Internal validity — confidence the IV genuinely caused the change in the DV (Bryman 2016). Experiments win (a control group neutralises threats like history and testing effects); cross-sectional designs are weak (association ≠ sequence).
  • External validity — whether results generalise beyond the study (Bryman 2016). Large random-sample surveys win; lab experiments often lose (artificial settings, unrepresentative participants — frequently students). Field experiments sit in the sweet spot: real participants and contexts give them stronger external validity than the lab while keeping randomisation’s internal validity.
TipThere’s usually no free lunch

The lab maximises internal validity at the cost of external; the survey does the reverse. Much of research craft is choosing which validity your question can’t live without, and paying the other’s price knowingly. Field experiments are prized precisely because they compromise least.

Thirteen ways to be wrong: THIS MESS DREAD

“Internal validity” is easy to say and hard to check. Martin and Bridgmon make it checkable. They treat it as ruling out rival explanations, and they give the rivals a mnemonic, THIS MESS DREAD: thirteen named ways a study can show a change that the treatment did not cause (Martin and Bridgmon 2012). The list comes from the Campbell tradition (Campbell & Stanley 1963; Cook & Campbell 1979; Shadish, Cook & Campbell 2002, as cited there). It splits into two groups, and the split is the useful part:

  • THIS MESS, the first eight threats, is handled by the design: random assignment, a control group, a pretest.
  • DREAD, the last five, is handled by procedures: how you run the study day to day.
Threats to internal validity, after Martin & Bridgmon’s Table 4.1 (paraphrased).
Letter Threat The rival story it tells
T Testing People score differently at posttest because they already sat the pretest.
H History Something outside the study happened at the same time (their example is a hurricane).
I Instrumentation The measuring instrument changed or drifted between pretest and posttest.
S Selection (differential) The groups differed before treatment, usually because assignment was not random.
M Maturation People change on their own over time, physically or psychologically.
E Experimental mortality (attrition) People drop out unevenly, so the groups you compare at the end are not the groups you started with.
S Statistical regression Extreme scorers drift back towards the mean when you measure them again.
S Selection × another threat Two threats combine, e.g. one group matures faster than the other.
D Diffusion of the treatment The control group finds its way to the attractive treatment.
R Compensatory rivalry Controls try harder because they feel they are competing with the treated group.
E Compensatory equalisation People outside the study give the control group equivalent goodies to make things fair.
A Ambiguous temporal precedence It is unclear which variable came first, so cause and effect could be reversed.
D Resentful demoralisation Controls do worse because they know someone else got the good treatment.

Read down the DREAD rows and you will see that, apart from ambiguous temporal precedence, they are all about contact between the arms. Each one happens because the control group knows, or finds out, what the treated group is getting. So randomisation does nothing to stop them. A perfectly randomised trial can still be spoiled when the control group gets hold of the treatment, competes with the treated group, is compensated by someone outside the study, or gives up.

Reading a design in four letters

Designs are written in a compact notation. Each row is a group. R means randomly assigned and NR not randomly assigned. O is an observation (a measurement of the outcome). X is a treatment and C a control condition (Martin and Bridgmon 2012). Once you can read the rows, you can check which threats a design closes.

Randomised treatments with posttest only.

R   X1   O
R   X2   O
R   C    O

Their example randomises people with depressive symptoms to CBT, interpersonal therapy or control, then measures the CES-D after eight weeks, analysed with a one-way ANOVA. Random assignment, a control group and a manipulated treatment close most of THIS MESS. The weak spot is the last S. If one arm loses more people than another (attrition × selection), the arms are no longer equivalent at the end, and with no pretest you cannot tell what the drop-outs looked like (Martin and Bridgmon 2012).

Add a pretest.

R   O   X1   O
R   O   X2   O
R   O   C    O

The pretest tells you who left and what they had looked like, so you can account for them. With it, Martin and Bridgmon count all of THIS MESS as controlled. The analysis becomes a 3 × 2 mixed design (therapy × time), in which the therapy × time interaction carries the treatment effect (see repeated-measures ANOVA and factorial ANOVA) (Martin and Bridgmon 2012).

Nonequivalent control group with a time series (a quasi-experiment).

NR   O1 O2 O3   X   O4 O5 O6
NR   O1 O2 O3   C   O4 O5 O6

There is no randomisation, but there is a comparison group and several measurements on each side of the treatment. That rules out most threats. The one left open is selection × history: an outside event that hits one group harder than the other (Martin and Bridgmon 2012). This is the same logic, and the same weak point, as difference-in-differences and its parallel-trends assumption.

One group, with the treatment removed and restored. In the weight-loss example, people are weighed at baseline, after three months with a support partner, after three months without one, and so on. The causal evidence comes from the removal and reinstatement: does the change stop when the treatment stops and resume when it comes back? History, maturation and reactivity (participants noticing the pattern) stay open because there is no control group (Martin and Bridgmon 2012). The full worked analysis is in repeated-measures ANOVA.

Why the pretest–posttest-only study fools people

The weakest design of all is one group, measured before and after. Two threats in it produce an “effect” even when there is nothing to find. Statistical regression does it whenever people are enrolled because they scored badly, which is how many clinics and remedial programmes recruit. Maturation does it whenever people tend to get better on their own. The simulation below runs a treatment that does nothing at all.

Show the figure code 
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(4)
n, mu, sd_true, sd_err, maturation = 4000, 50.0, 10.0, 6.0, 3.0
true = rng.normal(mu, sd_true, n)
pre = true + rng.normal(0, sd_err, n)
enrol = pre >= np.quantile(pre, 0.85)              # recruit the worst-scoring 15%
post = true - maturation + rng.normal(0, sd_err, n)  # no treatment effect at all

pre_e, post_e = pre[enrol], post[enrol]
arm = rng.permutation(enrol.sum()) % 2 == 0        # randomise enrolees 50/50
drop = pre_e.mean() - post_e.mean()
diff = ((post_e[arm].mean() - pre_e[arm].mean())
        - (post_e[~arm].mean() - pre_e[~arm].mean()))   # difference in change

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(9, 3.8), sharey=True)
ax = axes[0]
ax.plot([0, 1], [pre_e.mean(), post_e.mean()], marker="o", color="#161613", lw=2)
ax.annotate("", xy=(1.08, post_e.mean()), xytext=(1.08, pre_e.mean()),
            arrowprops=dict(arrowstyle="<->", color="#e8531d", lw=2))
ax.text(1.13, (pre_e.mean() + post_e.mean()) / 2, f"apparent\n'effect'\n{drop:.1f} pts",
        color="#e8531d", va="center", fontsize=9)
ax.set_title("One group, pre → post", fontsize=11)

ax = axes[1]
ax.plot([0, 1], [pre_e[arm].mean(), post_e[arm].mean()], marker="o", lw=2,
        color="#5e5e54", label="treated")
ax.plot([0, 1], [pre_e[~arm].mean(), post_e[~arm].mean()], marker="s", lw=2, ls="--",
        color="#161613", label="randomised control")
ax.text(-0.15, post_e.mean() + 0.5, f"difference in change\n= {diff:+.1f} pts",
        ha="left", fontsize=9)
ax.legend(frameon=False, fontsize=9, loc="upper right")
ax.set_title("Randomised control added", fontsize=11)

for ax in axes:
    ax.set_xticks([0, 1]); ax.set_xticklabels(["pretest", "posttest"])
    ax.set_xlim(-0.2, 1.55)
axes[0].set_ylabel("mean symptom score (higher = worse)")
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

Take the left panel to an evaluation committee and it looks like a success. The right panel shows the drop was there all along: the control group falls just as far. This is what Martin and Bridgmon mean when they say the design does the work. You do not need to know how much of the change was maturation and how much was regression. A randomised control group goes through both, and taking the difference cancels them. Notice also that nothing in this simulation is unusual. All it took was recruiting on a noisy measurement, which most screening programmes do. (The arithmetic of regression to the mean is in the paradoxes entry and in the appendix below.)

Did the treatment actually happen?

Internal validity also needs the treatment to have been delivered as designed, which Martin and Bridgmon call treatment integrity or treatment fidelity. Following Shadish, Cook and Campbell, they break it into three parts:

  • delivery: was the treatment implemented accurately and consistently?
  • receipt: did the participants actually get it?
  • adherence: did they follow through with it?

If the “light therapy” arm never switched the lamps on, a null result tells you about the lamps, not the theory (Martin and Bridgmon 2012).

For DREAD they list seven procedures (Martin and Bridgmon 2012):

  1. Have different people deliver each condition.
  2. Keep the treated and control groups apart.
  3. Blind participants (single-blind), deliverers as well (double-blind), and evaluators too (triple-blind).
  4. Debrief participants with interviews about what they experienced and expected.
  5. Think twice before offering experimental rewards.
  6. Define every condition clearly.
  7. Let theory and past research guide the claimed causal direction. This guards the A (ambiguous temporal precedence).

When randomisation is impossible, Shadish, Cook and Campbell’s six ways to strengthen a quasi-experiment are listed (Martin and Bridgmon 2012):

  1. Add a control or comparison group.
  2. Add pretests and posttests.
  3. Remove the treatment and reinstate it.
  4. Replicate.
  5. Reverse the treatment.
  6. Match cases.

Two statistical tools of the same kind get named alongside them: propensity-score matching and the regression-discontinuity cutoff. Part V is, in effect, this list worked out in full.

Why the randomised experiment is the benchmark

When you can run one, randomisation is a near-magical tool — it is the standard every other design is measured against (Gerber and Green 2012). It works because random assignment makes treatment statistically independent of everything about the subjects:

  • Comparable groups / a fair test. No systematic advantage to treatment or control; with a large enough sample, individual differences wash out (Gerber and Green 2012).
  • Eliminates confounders — even unobserved ones. Randomisation severs the link between treatment and every variable that predicts the outcome, solving the lurking-variable problem that plagues observational research (Gerber and Green 2012). This is the part no statistical “control” can match: you can only control for confounders you thought of and measured; randomisation handles the ones you didn’t.
  • Unbiased estimation. Replicated infinitely, the average estimate equals the true treatment effect (Gerber and Green 2012).

Concrete example — women heading Indian village councils. Because seats were randomly reserved for female leaders (by law), any difference in, say, sanitation spending between those councils and male-led ones could be attributed to the leader’s gender rather than to pre-existing village differences (Gerber and Green 2012). Randomisation turned an unanswerable observational question into a credible causal one. (You’ll meet this study’s statistics again in sampling distributions.)

Common pitfalls

WarningWhere this goes wrong
  • Causal claims from cross-sectional data. “X correlates with Y at one time point” is not “X causes Y.” This is the most common over-reach in applied social science.
  • Ignoring attrition in panels. If drop-out is related to the outcome, your surviving sample silently biases the result.
  • Generalising a case study to a population. Case studies generalise to theory, not to people-in-general.
  • Trusting a lab result in the wild. High internal validity doesn’t imply the effect survives outside the artificial setting.
  • Assuming you can always randomise. Often you can’t (ethics, law, practicality) — which is exactly why Part V exists: to approximate the experiment you couldn’t run.
  • Reading a pre–post drop as a treatment effect. If people were enrolled because they scored badly, some of the improvement is regression to the mean. If they would have got better anyway, some of it is maturation. Without a control group the two cannot be separated from the treatment (see the figure).
  • Thinking randomisation closes DREAD. Diffusion, rivalry, compensation and demoralisation all happen after assignment, through contact between the arms. Separate the arms, use different staff to deliver each, blind where you can, and debrief participants.
  • Ignoring uneven drop-out in a posttest-only trial. Randomised arms that lose different numbers of people are no longer comparable, and without a pretest you cannot see who left.
  • Treating THIS MESS DREAD as boilerplate. Saying “threats were addressed” proves nothing. For each threat, name the specific feature of the design or procedure that rules it out, or say plainly that it stays open. Maxwell makes the same argument for qualitative work (qualitative evidence).

Practitioner’s notes — tips & tricks

  • Start from the conclusion you need, then pick the design. If you need “X causes Y,” a survey won’t do — design for the claim, don’t retrofit it.
  • State your validity priority explicitly. Write down whether internal or external validity matters more for this decision; it resolves most design arguments.
  • When you can’t randomise, say so — and switch to causal-inference tools. The honest move is to name the missing experiment and adopt a design (Part V) that approximates it.
  • Budget for attrition up front in any longitudinal study; plan retention like you plan sampling.

Appendix · the mathematics

Why a pre–post drop isn’t an effect. Write each observed score as true score plus error, O_t = T + e_t, with errors independent across occasions and of T. Measurement reliability is the share of observed variance that is true-score variance: \rho = \frac{\sigma_T^2}{\sigma_T^2 + \sigma_e^2}. With normal scores, the expected posttest given the pretest, with no treatment effect and a common maturation shift m, is E[O_2 \mid O_1] = \mu + \rho\,(O_1 - \mu) - m . So for a group selected with mean pretest \bar O_1, the expected one-group pre–post change is \bar O_1 - E[\bar O_2] = \underbrace{(1-\rho)(\bar O_1 - \mu)}_{\text{statistical regression}} + \underbrace{m}_{\text{maturation}} + \underbrace{\tau}_{\text{treatment}} . Regression to the mean grows with how extreme the selection was (\bar O_1 - \mu) and how unreliable the measure is (1-\rho). In the figure, \rho = 100/136 \approx 0.74, and selecting the top 15% puts \bar O_1 about 18 points above \mu. That gives roughly 0.26 \times 18 \approx 4.8 points of regression plus 3 of maturation, about 7.8 points in all, with \tau = 0. The simulated drop is 8.1 points, which is within sampling noise of that figure.

Why the randomised control cancels it. If the control arm is drawn by lot from the same selected pool, its pretest mean matches the treated arm’s in expectation. It also shares the same \rho and the same m, so the first two terms are identical in both arms. The treated-minus-control difference in change then estimates \tau alone. That is the difference-in-differences logic, with randomisation guaranteeing that the trends are parallel.

::: {.lead-kernel}
A research design is the skeleton of a study: *when* you collect data, from *whom*, and with
*what comparison*. It sets a hard ceiling on the strength of any conclusion you can draw. Choose
it before you analyse anything, because the most sophisticated statistics in the world cannot
buy back a causal claim a cross-sectional design was never able to make.
:::

## Why it matters

Beginners think credibility comes from the analysis. It comes from the **design**. The design
determines whether you can say "X *causes* Y" or only "X and Y *go together*." This entry is the
map of the main designs and what each can — and cannot — license. It's also the bridge to the
summit of the manual: causal inference is, at heart, the art of *designing* (or reconstructing)
a fair comparison.

## The designs, and what each buys you

A research design reflects the priority you place on **causal connection, generalisation, and
understanding change over time** [@bryman_social_research].

### Experimental
**Manipulate an independent variable and use *random assignment*** to make the treatment and
control groups comparable.

- **Strengths:** the **benchmark for internal validity** — it rules out alternative causal
  explanations. *Field* experiments, run in real settings, add **ecological validity**.
- **Limits:** many social variables (gender, ethnicity, class) **can't be manipulated** for
  ethical or practical reasons; lab experiments can suffer **reactive effects** when people know
  they're studied, hurting generalisability.

### Cross-sectional / survey
**Data on many cases at a single point in time**, to find patterns of association.

- **Strengths:** ideal for **non-manipulable** variables (age, background); with **random
  probability sampling**, strong **external validity**.
- **Limits:** **weak internal validity** — everything is measured at once, so the *direction* of
  causation is ambiguous. It shows relationships, not causes.

### Longitudinal / panel
**The same sample studied on ≥2 occasions** (panel = same people; cohort = people sharing a
trait, e.g. birth week).

- **Strengths:** establishes **temporal order**, enabling stronger causal inference than
  cross-sectional designs; essential for charting **social change**.
- **Limits:** **expensive and slow**; suffers **attrition** as participants drop out, which can
  make the survivors unrepresentative.

### Comparative
**Two or more contrasting cases** (e.g. nations) studied with near-identical methods.

- **Strengths:** reveals how a phenomenon varies across socio-cultural settings, guarding
  against findings that are **culturally specific**.
- **Limits:** hard to ensure data are **truly comparable** across contexts (translation of
  instruments is a classic trap).

### Case study
**Intensive analysis of a single case** — a community, organisation, or person.

- **Strengths:** deep, **idiographic** insight; supports **analytic generalisation** (to
  theory, not population).
- **Limits:** **weak external validity** — one case rarely represents a wider population.

## The two rulers: internal vs external validity

Every design is judged on two different axes, and they often **trade off**:

- **Internal validity** — confidence the IV genuinely caused the change in the DV
  [@bryman_social_research]. Experiments win (a control group neutralises threats like *history*
  and *testing effects*); cross-sectional designs are weak (association ≠ sequence).
- **External validity** — whether results **generalise** beyond the study
  [@bryman_social_research]. Large random-sample surveys win; lab experiments often lose
  (artificial settings, unrepresentative participants — frequently students). **Field
  experiments** sit in the sweet spot: real participants and contexts give them stronger
  external validity than the lab while keeping randomisation's internal validity.

::: {.callout-tip title="There's usually no free lunch"}
The lab maximises *internal* validity at the cost of *external*; the survey does the reverse.
Much of research craft is choosing **which validity your question can't live without**, and
paying the other's price knowingly. Field experiments are prized precisely because they
compromise least.
:::

## Thirteen ways to be wrong: THIS MESS DREAD

"Internal validity" is easy to say and hard to check. Martin and Bridgmon make it checkable. They
treat it as **ruling out rival explanations**, and they give the rivals a mnemonic,
**THIS MESS DREAD**: thirteen named ways a study can show a change that the treatment did not cause
[@martin_bridgmon]. The list comes from the Campbell tradition (Campbell & Stanley 1963; Cook &
Campbell 1979; Shadish, Cook & Campbell 2002, as cited there). It splits into two groups, and the
split is the useful part:

- **THIS MESS**, the first eight threats, is handled by the **design**: random assignment, a control
  group, a pretest.
- **DREAD**, the last five, is handled by **procedures**: how you run the study day to day.

| Letter | Threat | The rival story it tells |
|---|---|---|
| **T** | Testing | People score differently at posttest because they already sat the pretest. |
| **H** | History | Something outside the study happened at the same time (their example is a hurricane). |
| **I** | Instrumentation | The measuring instrument changed or drifted between pretest and posttest. |
| **S** | Selection (differential) | The groups differed before treatment, usually because assignment was not random. |
| **M** | Maturation | People change on their own over time, physically or psychologically. |
| **E** | Experimental mortality (attrition) | People drop out unevenly, so the groups you compare at the end are not the groups you started with. |
| **S** | Statistical regression | Extreme scorers drift back towards the mean when you measure them again. |
| **S** | Selection × another threat | Two threats combine, e.g. one group matures faster than the other. |
| **D** | Diffusion of the treatment | The control group finds its way to the attractive treatment. |
| **R** | Compensatory rivalry | Controls try harder because they feel they are competing with the treated group. |
| **E** | Compensatory equalisation | People outside the study give the control group equivalent goodies to make things fair. |
| **A** | Ambiguous temporal precedence | It is unclear which variable came first, so cause and effect could be reversed. |
| **D** | Resentful demoralisation | Controls do worse because they know someone else got the good treatment. |

: Threats to internal validity, after Martin & Bridgmon's Table 4.1 (paraphrased). {.striped}

Read down the DREAD rows and you will see that, apart from ambiguous temporal precedence, they are
all about **contact between the arms**. Each one happens because the control group knows, or finds
out, what the treated group is getting. So randomisation does nothing to stop them. A perfectly
randomised trial can still be spoiled when the control group gets hold of the treatment, competes
with the treated group, is compensated by someone outside the study, or gives up.

### Reading a design in four letters

Designs are written in a compact notation. Each row is a group. **R** means randomly assigned and
**NR** not randomly assigned. **O** is an observation (a measurement of the outcome). **X** is a
treatment and **C** a control condition [@martin_bridgmon]. Once you can read the rows, you can
check which threats a design closes.

**Randomised treatments with posttest only.**

```
R   X1   O
R   X2   O
R   C    O
```

Their example randomises people with depressive symptoms to CBT, interpersonal therapy or control,
then measures the CES-D after eight weeks, analysed with a one-way ANOVA. Random assignment,
a control group and a manipulated treatment close most of THIS MESS. The weak spot is the last S.
If one arm loses more people than another (attrition × selection), the arms are no longer
equivalent at the end, and with no pretest you cannot tell what the drop-outs looked like
[@martin_bridgmon].

**Add a pretest.**

```
R   O   X1   O
R   O   X2   O
R   O   C    O
```

The pretest tells you who left and what they had looked like, so you can account for them. With it,
Martin and Bridgmon count all of THIS MESS as controlled. The analysis becomes a 3 × 2 mixed design
(therapy × time), in which the **therapy × time interaction** carries the treatment effect (see
[repeated-measures ANOVA](../03-regression/repeated-measures-anova.qmd) and
[factorial ANOVA](../03-regression/factorial-anova-and-ancova.qmd)) [@martin_bridgmon].

**Nonequivalent control group with a time series** (a quasi-experiment).

```
NR   O1 O2 O3   X   O4 O5 O6
NR   O1 O2 O3   C   O4 O5 O6
```

There is no randomisation, but there is a comparison group and several measurements on each side of
the treatment. That rules out most threats. The one left open is **selection × history**: an outside
event that hits one group harder than the other [@martin_bridgmon]. This is the same
logic, and the same weak point, as [difference-in-differences](../05-causal-inference/difference-in-differences.qmd)
and its parallel-trends assumption.

**One group, with the treatment removed and restored.** In the weight-loss example, people are
weighed at baseline, after three months with a support partner, after three months without one,
and so on. The causal evidence comes from the **removal and reinstatement**: does the change stop
when the treatment stops and resume when it comes back? History, maturation and reactivity
(participants noticing the pattern) stay open because there is no control group
[@martin_bridgmon]. The full worked analysis is in
[repeated-measures ANOVA](../03-regression/repeated-measures-anova.qmd).

### Why the pretest–posttest-only study fools people

The weakest design of all is one group, measured before and after. Two threats in it produce an
"effect" even when there is nothing to find. **Statistical regression** does it whenever people are
enrolled *because* they scored badly, which is how many clinics and remedial programmes recruit.
**Maturation** does it whenever people tend to get better on their own. The simulation below runs a
treatment that does nothing at all.

```{python}
#| label: fig-this-mess
#| fig-cap: "A treatment with zero true effect, run two ways (our simulation, not data from the sources). 4,000 people take a noisy symptom screening test (true score mean 50, SD 10; measurement-error SD 6, so reliability ≈ 0.74). Everyone in the top 15% is enrolled, and every enrolee improves by 3 points over the study period (maturation). Left: in the one-group pretest–posttest design, scores fall sharply, and the orange gap looks like a treatment effect. It is regression to the mean plus maturation. Right: the same enrolees are randomised to treatment or control. Both arms fall by the same amount, so the treated-minus-control difference in change is about zero."
#| code-fold: true
#| code-summary: "Show the figure code"
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(4)
n, mu, sd_true, sd_err, maturation = 4000, 50.0, 10.0, 6.0, 3.0
true = rng.normal(mu, sd_true, n)
pre = true + rng.normal(0, sd_err, n)
enrol = pre >= np.quantile(pre, 0.85)              # recruit the worst-scoring 15%
post = true - maturation + rng.normal(0, sd_err, n)  # no treatment effect at all

pre_e, post_e = pre[enrol], post[enrol]
arm = rng.permutation(enrol.sum()) % 2 == 0        # randomise enrolees 50/50
drop = pre_e.mean() - post_e.mean()
diff = ((post_e[arm].mean() - pre_e[arm].mean())
        - (post_e[~arm].mean() - pre_e[~arm].mean()))   # difference in change

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(9, 3.8), sharey=True)
ax = axes[0]
ax.plot([0, 1], [pre_e.mean(), post_e.mean()], marker="o", color="#161613", lw=2)
ax.annotate("", xy=(1.08, post_e.mean()), xytext=(1.08, pre_e.mean()),
            arrowprops=dict(arrowstyle="<->", color="#e8531d", lw=2))
ax.text(1.13, (pre_e.mean() + post_e.mean()) / 2, f"apparent\n'effect'\n{drop:.1f} pts",
        color="#e8531d", va="center", fontsize=9)
ax.set_title("One group, pre → post", fontsize=11)

ax = axes[1]
ax.plot([0, 1], [pre_e[arm].mean(), post_e[arm].mean()], marker="o", lw=2,
        color="#5e5e54", label="treated")
ax.plot([0, 1], [pre_e[~arm].mean(), post_e[~arm].mean()], marker="s", lw=2, ls="--",
        color="#161613", label="randomised control")
ax.text(-0.15, post_e.mean() + 0.5, f"difference in change\n= {diff:+.1f} pts",
        ha="left", fontsize=9)
ax.legend(frameon=False, fontsize=9, loc="upper right")
ax.set_title("Randomised control added", fontsize=11)

for ax in axes:
    ax.set_xticks([0, 1]); ax.set_xticklabels(["pretest", "posttest"])
    ax.set_xlim(-0.2, 1.55)
axes[0].set_ylabel("mean symptom score (higher = worse)")
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

Take the left panel to an evaluation committee and it looks like a success. The right panel shows
the drop was there all along: the control group falls just as far. This is what Martin and Bridgmon
mean when they say the design does the work. You do not need to know how much of the change was
maturation and how much was regression. A randomised control group goes through both, and taking
the difference cancels them. Notice also that nothing in this simulation is unusual. All it took
was recruiting on a noisy measurement, which most screening programmes do. (The arithmetic of
regression to the mean is in the [paradoxes](../01-foundations/famous-paradoxes.qmd) entry and in
the appendix below.)

### Did the treatment actually happen?

Internal validity also needs the treatment to have been delivered as designed, which Martin and
Bridgmon call **treatment integrity** or **treatment fidelity**. Following Shadish, Cook and
Campbell, they break it into three parts:

- **delivery**: was the treatment implemented accurately and consistently?
- **receipt**: did the participants actually get it?
- **adherence**: did they follow through with it?

If the "light therapy" arm never switched the lamps on, a null result tells you about the lamps,
not the theory [@martin_bridgmon].

For DREAD they list seven procedures [@martin_bridgmon]:

1. Have different people deliver each condition.
2. Keep the treated and control groups apart.
3. Blind participants (single-blind), deliverers as well (double-blind), and evaluators too
   (triple-blind).
4. Debrief participants with interviews about what they experienced and expected.
5. Think twice before offering experimental rewards.
6. Define every condition clearly.
7. Let theory and past research guide the claimed causal direction. This guards the A
   (ambiguous temporal precedence).

When randomisation is impossible, Shadish, Cook and Campbell's six ways to strengthen a
quasi-experiment are listed [@martin_bridgmon]:

1. Add a control or comparison group.
2. Add pretests and posttests.
3. Remove the treatment and reinstate it.
4. Replicate.
5. Reverse the treatment.
6. Match cases.

Two statistical tools of the same kind get named alongside them:
[propensity-score matching](../05-causal-inference/matching-and-propensity-scores.qmd) and the
[regression-discontinuity](../05-causal-inference/regression-discontinuity.qmd) cutoff. Part V is,
in effect, this list worked out in full.

## Why the randomised experiment is the benchmark

When you can run one, randomisation is a near-magical tool — it is the standard every other
design is measured against [@gerber_green]. It works because random assignment makes treatment
**statistically independent** of everything about the subjects:

- **Comparable groups / a fair test.** No systematic advantage to treatment or control; with a
  large enough sample, individual differences **wash out** [@gerber_green].
- **Eliminates confounders — even unobserved ones.** Randomisation severs the link between
  treatment and *every* variable that predicts the outcome, solving the lurking-variable problem
  that plagues observational research [@gerber_green]. This is the part no statistical "control"
  can match: you can only control for confounders you *thought of and measured*; randomisation
  handles the ones you didn't.
- **Unbiased estimation.** Replicated infinitely, the average estimate equals the true treatment
  effect [@gerber_green].

**Concrete example — women heading Indian village councils.** Because seats were randomly
reserved for female leaders (by law), any difference in, say, sanitation spending between those
councils and male-led ones could be attributed to the *leader's gender* rather than to
pre-existing village differences [@gerber_green]. Randomisation turned an unanswerable
observational question into a credible causal one. *(You'll meet this study's statistics again
in [sampling distributions](../02-inference/sampling-distributions-clt.qmd).)*

## Common pitfalls

::: {.callout-warning title="Where this goes wrong"}
- **Causal claims from cross-sectional data.** "X correlates with Y at one time point" is not
  "X causes Y." This is the most common over-reach in applied social science.
- **Ignoring attrition in panels.** If drop-out is related to the outcome, your surviving sample
  silently biases the result.
- **Generalising a case study to a population.** Case studies generalise to *theory*, not to
  *people-in-general*.
- **Trusting a lab result in the wild.** High internal validity doesn't imply the effect
  survives outside the artificial setting.
- **Assuming you can always randomise.** Often you can't (ethics, law, practicality) — which is
  exactly why Part V exists: to approximate the experiment you couldn't run.
- **Reading a pre–post drop as a treatment effect.** If people were enrolled *because* they
  scored badly, some of the improvement is regression to the mean. If they would have got better
  anyway, some of it is maturation. Without a control group the two cannot be separated from the
  treatment (see the figure).
- **Thinking randomisation closes DREAD.** Diffusion, rivalry, compensation and demoralisation
  all happen *after* assignment, through contact between the arms. Separate the arms, use
  different staff to deliver each, blind where you can, and debrief participants.
- **Ignoring uneven drop-out in a posttest-only trial.** Randomised arms that lose different
  numbers of people are no longer comparable, and without a pretest you cannot see who left.
- **Treating THIS MESS DREAD as boilerplate.** Saying "threats were addressed" proves nothing.
  For each threat, name the specific feature of the design or procedure that rules it out, or
  say plainly that it stays open. Maxwell makes the same argument for qualitative work
  ([qualitative evidence](qualitative-evidence.qmd)).
:::

## Practitioner's notes — tips & tricks {.unnumbered}

- **Start from the conclusion you need, then pick the design.** If you need "X causes Y," a
  survey won't do — design for the claim, don't retrofit it.
- **State your validity priority explicitly.** Write down whether internal or external validity
  matters more for *this* decision; it resolves most design arguments.
- **When you can't randomise, say so — and switch to causal-inference tools.** The honest move
  is to name the missing experiment and adopt a design (Part V) that approximates it.
- **Budget for attrition up front** in any longitudinal study; plan retention like you plan
  sampling.

## Appendix · the mathematics {.unnumbered}

::: {.math-appendix}
**Why a pre–post drop isn't an effect.** Write each observed score as true score plus error,
$O_t = T + e_t$, with errors independent across occasions and of $T$. Measurement **reliability**
is the share of observed variance that is true-score variance:
$$
\rho = \frac{\sigma_T^2}{\sigma_T^2 + \sigma_e^2}.
$$
With normal scores, the expected posttest given the pretest, with no treatment effect and a common
maturation shift $m$, is
$$
E[O_2 \mid O_1] = \mu + \rho\,(O_1 - \mu) - m .
$$
So for a group selected with mean pretest $\bar O_1$, the expected one-group pre–post change is
$$
\bar O_1 - E[\bar O_2] = \underbrace{(1-\rho)(\bar O_1 - \mu)}_{\text{statistical regression}}
+ \underbrace{m}_{\text{maturation}} + \underbrace{\tau}_{\text{treatment}} .
$$
Regression to the mean grows with how extreme the selection was ($\bar O_1 - \mu$) and how
unreliable the measure is ($1-\rho$). In the figure, $\rho = 100/136 \approx 0.74$, and selecting the
top 15% puts $\bar O_1$ about 18 points above $\mu$. That gives roughly $0.26 \times 18 \approx 4.8$
points of regression plus $3$ of maturation, about 7.8 points in all, with $\tau = 0$. The simulated
drop is 8.1 points, which is within sampling noise of that figure.

**Why the randomised control cancels it.** If the control arm is drawn by lot from the *same*
selected pool, its pretest mean matches the treated arm's in expectation. It also shares the same
$\rho$ and the same $m$, so the first two terms are identical in both arms. The treated-minus-control
difference in change then estimates $\tau$ alone. That is the
[difference-in-differences](../05-causal-inference/difference-in-differences.qmd) logic, with
randomisation guaranteeing that the trends are parallel.
:::
