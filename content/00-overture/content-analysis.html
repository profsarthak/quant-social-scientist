Quantitative Content Analysis: Turning Texts and Images into Counts

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source

Newspapers, speeches, tweets, websites and selfies can all be coded into variables. The coding scheme is your measuring instrument, and it needs the same care as a questionnaire.

Content analysis counts what documents contain, using categories fixed in advance and rules explicit enough that a stranger could apply them and get the same numbers. It reaches people you could never interview and periods you could never survey. But each count is only as good as the coding scheme behind it, so the scheme has to be designed, piloted and tested for reliability like any other measuring instrument.

Why it matters

Some of the most interesting social data were never collected by a researcher. You cannot interview a prime minister about the environment or get them to fill in a questionnaire, but you can analyse every speech they have given (Clark et al. 2021). Questions such as when did the press start covering this story, which papers covered it most, and did journalists’ stance change? are questions about documents, and content analysis is the method that answers them (Clark et al. 2021).

It is also where a great deal of student and professional research now starts, because the raw material is already sitting in newspaper archives, on websites and on social media platforms. That makes it look easy. It isn’t. Turning a newspaper article into a row of numbers takes many decisions (what counts as the unit, which categories exist, what to do with an ambiguous case), and a different coder may make each of them differently. Most of this entry is about making those decisions explicit enough to survive replication.

What content analysis is

The most quoted definition is Berelson’s (1952): content analysis is “a research technique for the objective, systematic and quantitative description of the manifest content of communication” (Clark et al. 2021). Holsti (1969) widens it to “any technique for making inferences by objectively and systematically identifying specified characteristics of messages” (Clark et al. 2021).

Both definitions stress two qualities. Being objective means the rules for assigning material to categories are fixed in advance and written down, so that personal bias intrudes as little as possible. Being systematic means applying those rules consistently. The rules themselves may reflect the researcher’s interests. What matters is that, once written, anyone could apply them and replicate the result. Krippendorff (2018) puts it as describing “how we derive our judgements, so that others—especially our critics—can replicate our results or build on them” (Clark et al. 2021).

The two definitions differ in two ways (Clark et al. 2021):

  • Manifest vs latent content. Berelson limits the method to manifest content, what an item is clearly about. Holsti’s “specified characteristics” allows latent content, the meanings beneath the surface, which have to be interpreted. Siegel et al. (2013) coded alcohol-brand references in US popular songs from 2009 to 2011. The brands and types of drink mentioned were manifest. Whether the song presented the drinking and its consequences positively, negatively or neutrally was latent. Some authors doubt that latent content can be measured quantitatively at all (Ahuvia 2001), and others point out that the line between the two is often fuzzy (Riffe et al. 2014).
  • Communication vs messages. Berelson wrote about mass media. Holsti’s “messages” opens the method to any unstructured text, including interview transcripts and open-ended survey answers.

Bryman’s own definition (Key concept 13.1) puts the quantitative version on the deductive side: predetermined categories, applied to test theories or hypotheses. Qualitative content analysis lets themes emerge from the data and pays more attention to context. The contrast is set out in qualitative evidence (Clark et al. 2021).

The range of material is wide. Bryman’s examples include newspaper coverage of the UK sugar tax (Buckton et al. 2018), online dog obituaries (MacKay et al. 2016), gender on primetime television (Sink and Mastro 2017), the Queen’s Speech (John and Jennings 2010), European Parliament speeches (Proksch and Slapin 2010), sex-discrimination case narratives (Bobbitt-Zeher 2011), Instagram selfies (Döring et al. 2016), meals in paintings (Wansink et al. 2016), conditions in IMF loan agreements (Kentikelenis et al. 2016) and populism in party press releases across 11 national elections (Bernhard and Kriesi 2019) (Clark et al. 2021).

Step 1: fix the research questions first

As with a survey, the questions come first. They decide which documents you sample and which dimensions the coding scheme must capture. Leave them vague and you risk analysing the wrong material or building a scheme that misses what you care about (Clark et al. 2021). Most content analyses revolve around who gets included, what gets included, where an issue appears, its location within the item, how much coverage it gets, and why (Clark et al. 2021).

Two further kinds of question are distinctive:

  • Omissions. What is not reported can matter as much as what is. If government reviews of community care say a lot about “costs” and “efficiencies” but almost nothing about “quality”, “choice” or “service user satisfaction”, that silence is a finding (Clark et al. 2021).
  • Change over time. Young and Dugas (2011) compared climate-change coverage in two national Canadian newspapers in 1988–9, 1998–9 and 2007–8, using the search terms “climate change”, “global warming”, “greenhouse effect” and “greenhouse gas”. Claims that climate change is caused by humans declined progressively across the three periods. University experts and government employees became less prominent as voices, while industry associations and environmental groups became more prominent. Stoddart et al. (2016) extended the series to 1997–2010 and found similar trends (Clark et al. 2021).

Step 2: sample the media, then the dates

Many studies take the form “the representation of X in Y”: trade unions, crime or social science research (X) in newspapers, television, podcasts, tweets or speeches (Y) (Clark et al. 2021). Once Y is chosen, the sample has to be narrowed. For newspapers: tabloids and/or broadsheets, weekday and/or weekend, national and/or local, paid-for and/or free. Then: all items, or news items only, with or without features and letters? Most studies pick one or two media, though Stroobant et al. (2018) analysed a month of Belgian health news across newspapers, magazines, radio, television and news websites (Clark et al. 2021).

Dates are the second stage.

  • Event-driven designs have one date fixed by the question. Bligh et al. (2004) tested Weber’s idea that charismatic leadership emerges in crises by comparing George W. Bush’s speeches before and after 11 September 2001. His rhetoric, and the media’s portrayal of him, became more charismatic. They still had to choose an end date; their last speech was on 11 March 2002, which raises the question of how long a crisis effect should be expected to last. Gunn and Naper (2016) analysed 3,420 tweets from the Obama and Romney accounts in 2012. Election day fixed the end date, and they chose 1 January 2012 as the start to cover the whole campaign (Clark et al. 2021).
  • Ongoing phenomena leave the dates open, and probability sampling can be adapted to them. Döring et al. (2016) drew 250 female and 250 male selfies from Instagram in April 2014, found through hashtags such as #selfie and #me, taking every tenth qualifying picture until they reached 500 (Clark et al. 2021). That is a systematic sample.

Watch for designs that over-represent some kinds of item. Beullens and Schepers (2013) coded the 20 most recent status updates on each of 160 Belgian students’ Facebook profiles, instead of all updates in a fixed period, “to avoid the overrepresentation of frequent and active Facebook users” (Clark et al. 2021). Sampling a time window would have given the heaviest posters the most weight. Taking a fixed number per person gives each person equal weight, so the sample describes users, not posts.

Large online populations usually need reducing. Humphreys et al. (2014) collected 101,069 tweets, then drew random samples of 1,050 sent online and 1,050 sent by text message, because the channel was one of their variables. Small populations can be coded in full: Ledford and Anderson (2013) coded all 49 company posts and 655 user posts in the ten days after Novartis used Facebook to announce a recall of Excedrin (Clark et al. 2021).

Step 3: decide what to count

What you count follows from the research questions. Bryman groups the usual units into four kinds (Clark et al. 2021).

Significant actors. Who wrote the item (a general or specialist reporter)? Who is its main focus (a politician, an expert, a spokesperson)? Who supplies alternative voices? What was the occasion (an interview, a report’s release, an event)? Coding these maps who gets to speak in the news.

Words. Word choice can reveal, for example, a tendency to sensationalise. Bailey et al. (2014) counted “epistemic markers”, words that signal uncertainty, in climate-change coverage in two US and two Spanish newspapers in 2001 and 2007, the years of IPCC reports. The markers included activities with uncertain outcomes (“predicting”), quantitative uncertainty (“likelihood”), challenges to the IPCC (“debate”), references to opponents (“deniers”) and modifiers (“controversial”). Context was checked before any term was marked. Markers were more frequent in the US papers in both years and rose between 2001 and 2007 in both countries, even though scientific understanding had strengthened in that period (Clark et al. 2021).

Word counting is where computer-assisted content analysis (CACA) comes in. Software removes human inconsistency and fatigue, produces key-word-in-context (KWIC) listings, finds co-occurrences and handles very large volumes. It cannot handle nuance, depends on a list of search terms that is hard to make complete, and invites a focus on frequency over meaning (Bligh and Kohles 2014) (Clark et al. 2021). Seale et al. (2006) used Wordsmith for a comparative keyword analysis of one day’s posts (20 April 2005) on the two largest UK online support forums for breast and prostate cancer. Men with prostate cancer used more words about research, treatment and diagnosis (“study”, “radical prostatectomy”, “biopsy”). Women with breast cancer used more words about feelings (“scared”) and people (“family”) and more superlatives (“amazing”). The counts were a starting point for a qualitative reading of the word clusters (Clark et al. 2021). Bligh et al.’s Bush study used DICTION, which works from ready-made or user-built dictionaries. Automated sentiment analysis, such as SentiStrength or the scoring in Greaves et al.’s (2014) study of tweets to NHS hospitals, is another form (Clark et al. 2021). Doing this at scale is the subject of text as data.

Subjects and themes. This means classifying the item. Fenton et al. (1998) sorted social science reported in the British media into seven disciplines. Buckton et al. (2018) read each sugar-tax article in full and recorded, for each theme, whether the article overtly contained it; a Daily Mail sentence about halving sugar intake “to tackle the growing obesity and diabetes crisis” was coded as linking sugar to both obesity and diabetes. Kentikelenis et al. (2016) searched 4,590 IMF loan documents from 1985 to 2014 and extracted 55,465 conditions across 131 countries. Conditions rose after 2008 even as the number of lending programmes fell (Clark et al. 2021). Thematic coding often moves from manifest to latent content.

Dispositions. This is the stance an item takes: for or against, positive or negative. Buckton et al. also coded whether each article favoured the sugar tax. Bobbitt-Zeher (2011) coded discrimination narratives for descriptive stereotyping (assumptions about women in general, or that women’s traits don’t fit a job) and prescriptive stereotyping (a particular woman violating gender expectations). Descriptive stereotyping appeared in 44% of narratives and prescriptive in 8%. Of the remaining 48%, most (37%) showed no stereotyping and the rest were coded “other”. Sink and Mastro (2017) coded 1,254 characters in 89 primetime programmes across nine US networks over ten weeks. Their coders were told to judge as “the average American television viewer” would, using the definitions in the codebook. Some stereotypes had declined, but dominant men and sexually provocative women persisted (Clark et al. 2021).

Step 4: the coding schedule and the coding manual

A coding scheme has two parts (Clark et al. 2021):

  • The coding schedule is the form on which each item’s codes are entered: one column per dimension, one form per item. Each completed form becomes one row of a data file for SPSS, R or Stata.
  • The coding manual is the instruction book. It lists every dimension, every category within each dimension, the number assigned to each category, and guidance on how to decide between them.

Bryman builds a toy example about crime reporting in UK national newspapers. It has nine dimensions: the nature of the offence, the gender, social class and age of the perpetrator and of the victim, the depiction of the victim, and the position of the item. The class categories follow Goldthorpe et al.’s scheme, with “unemployed”, “retired”, “housewife” and “other” added because newspapers use those words. The offence categories follow Home Office recording rules, which makes the results comparable with official crime data. Room is left for two offences per article, with the main one coded first. A Sunday Times murder-charge story and a Daily Star assault story each become a line of digits (Clark et al. 2021). That comparability point links directly to using existing data, where the official series has its own production process.

The scheme is a measuring instrument, and Bryman’s rules for it are the same as for a questionnaire or an observation schedule (Clark et al. 2021):

Rule What it guards against
Discrete dimensions two dimensions measuring overlapping things
Mutually exclusive categories an item that fits two categories, coded inconsistently
Exhaustive categories an item that fits no category
Clear instructions coder discretion; the manual may need to be long
A clear unit of analysis confusing the item (the article) with what is coded in it (each offence)

Pilot the scheme. A pilot shows where coders are unsure which category applies and which cases have no code. It also shows whether one category is swallowing most of the items. If so, split it, so that you can see detail inside it (Clark et al. 2021). The figure below shows a second reason a dominant category matters.

Reliability: would another coder get the same numbers?

Coding has to be consistent between coders (inter-rater reliability) and for each coder over time (intra-rater reliability). A lone student coding a dissertation escapes the first problem but not the second, which is why the manual matters even with one coder (Clark et al. 2021). Bryman notes that observers can be trained to use complex schedules with considerable reliability, and that reliability is a precondition for validity (Clark et al. 2021).

Albers explains how to put a number on it (Albers 2017):

  • Percentage agreement (“joint probability of agreement”) is the share of items both coders put in the same category: 73 of 100 is 73%. It is easy to compute and the least robust measure, because some agreement happens by chance. Two coders assigning one of five codes at random would agree 20% of the time. If near-misses are to count as agreement on an ordered scale, that tolerance has to be fixed before the analysis, not adjusted afterwards to reach an acceptable figure.
  • Cohen’s kappa corrects for chance agreement. It treats categories as nominal, runs from −1 to 1, and 0 means chance-level agreement. Albers gives the usual bands: 0–0.20 poor, 0.21–0.40 fair, 0.41–0.60 moderate, 0.61–0.80 substantial, 0.81–1 excellent. He suggests that a value above 0.5 is needed to support validity claims, and warns that kappa is higher with fewer codes and depends on how often each code is used.
  • Fleiss’ kappa extends this to three or more coders. Krippendorff’s alpha handles several coders and nominal, ordinal, interval or ratio codes, and has a long history in coding texts, open-ended answers and observations.

The chance correction matters most when one category dominates, which is common in content analysis. Beullens and Schepers found alcohol shown in a positive light in 72% of the Facebook photos they coded, neutral in 23%, and negative or unknown in the remaining 5% (Clark et al. 2021). The figure uses that split.

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(13)
K, N, R = 4, 500, 300                       # categories, items, replications
skew = np.array([0.72, 0.23, 0.03, 0.02])
flat = np.full(K, 0.25)
acc = np.linspace(0.5, 1.0, 11)

def code(truth, a):
    """Right with probability a, otherwise one of the other K-1 categories."""
    ok = rng.random(truth.shape) < a
    return np.where(ok, truth, (truth + rng.integers(1, K, truth.shape)) % K)

def agreement_and_kappa(c1, c2):
    po = (c1 == c2).mean(-1)
    p1 = np.stack([(c1 == k).mean(-1) for k in range(K)], -1)
    p2 = np.stack([(c2 == k).mean(-1) for k in range(K)], -1)
    pe = (p1 * p2).sum(-1)
    return po.mean(), ((po - pe) / (1 - pe)).mean()

res = {}
for name, pi in [("skew", skew), ("flat", flat)]:
    po_k, kap_k, big, rare = [], [], [], []
    for a in acc:
        truth = rng.choice(K, size=(R, N), p=pi)
        c1, c2 = code(truth, a), code(truth, a)
        po, kap = agreement_and_kappa(c1, c2)
        po_k.append(po); kap_k.append(kap)
        big.append((c1 == 0).mean()); rare.append((c1 >= 2).mean())
    res[name] = dict(po=np.array(po_k), kap=np.array(kap_k), big=np.array(big), rare=np.array(rare))

truth = rng.choice(K, size=(R, N), p=skew)
lazy_po, lazy_kap = agreement_and_kappa(truth, np.zeros_like(truth))

fig, (axL, axR) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(9.4, 3.9))

axL.plot(100 * acc, res["skew"]["po"], color="#161613", lw=2, label="raw agreement (either split)")
axL.plot(100 * acc, res["flat"]["kap"], color="#5e5e54", lw=1.8, ls="--", label="kappa, equal shares")
axL.plot(100 * acc, res["skew"]["kap"], color="#e8531d", lw=2.2, label="kappa, 72/23/3/2 split")
axL.scatter([100], [lazy_po], s=40, color="#8a8a7e", zorder=3)
axL.scatter([100], [lazy_kap], s=40, color="#8a8a7e", zorder=3)
axL.text(98, 0.03, f"grey dots: a lazy coder (always\n'positive') vs a perfect coder:\n"
         f"agreement {lazy_po:.2f}, kappa {lazy_kap:.2f}", fontsize=8, color="#5e5e54",
         ha="right", va="bottom")
axL.set_ylim(-0.05, 1.05)
axL.set_title("Agreement flatters; kappa corrects for chance", fontsize=10)
axL.set_xlabel("each coder's accuracy (%)")
axL.set_ylabel("agreement / kappa")

Read the left panel at 90% accuracy. Raw agreement is about 0.81 under either split. Kappa is about 0.75 when the four categories are equally common but about 0.63 under the 72/23/3/2 split, because two coders who both say “positive” most of the time agree a lot by chance. A coder who doesn’t read the photos at all and codes everything “positive” reaches 72% agreement with a perfect coder, close to two genuinely 85%-accurate coders, and kappa correctly scores this as zero. (Albers’ warning that kappa depends on code frequencies is the same point seen from the other side: equally good coders get a lower kappa when one code dominates, so kappas from studies with different category shares are not directly comparable (Albers 2017).)

The right panel is the reason reliability is not a formality. Random coding errors are not neutral in a count. They move items out of big categories and into small ones, so the coded share of the dominant category is too low and the rare categories are inflated. At 80% accuracy the “positive” share comes out near 60% and the “negative or unknown” share more than triples, from 5% to about 17% (our simulation; see the appendix). With rare categories, a small error rate can be most of what you count.

Online and visual material

Websites, blogs, forums and social media are documents in their own right, but who made them and why differs. Bryman’s four broad categories each bring their own problems (Clark et al. 2021).

Websites. Ask Scott’s (1990) questions of any document: why does it exist, and for whom? Kahan et al. (2019) analysed the websites of a representative sample of 759 US charter elementary schools in spring 2018. 97% had a working website, but 52% did not mention any of five common physical-activity programmes, physical education appeared on only 34%, and only 2% gave enough information to work out how much PE pupils received. The sites were too thin to support wide generalisations (Clark et al. 2021). Practical problems include search engines that cover only part of the web and rank results through advertising and optimisation, results that depend on your keywords, and sites that appear, disappear and change. Record the date you accessed each site. The Internet Archive’s Wayback Machine holds over 411 billion pages. Underneath all of this is the general problem of documentary research: it is hard to define the population you are sampling from, and online that population keeps changing (Clark et al. 2021).

Blogs share these problems. Boepple and Thompson (2014) coded 21 award-winning “healthy living” blogs with the most page views. Five bloggers used very negative language about being fat and four expressed admiration for thinness; the authors judged much of the content “potentially problematic” for readers (Clark et al. 2021).

Forums. Davis et al. (2015) coded the 1,094 comments posted under a blog about Amanda Todd’s cyberbullying suicide. 482 contained stories of being bullied: 12% about cyberbullying, 75% about traditional bullying, the rest a mixture. Physical appearance was the most common reason given, and the authors concluded that victims are often targeted for not conforming to mainstream norms (Clark et al. 2021).

Social media raise four questions before you code anything (Clark et al. 2021):

  • Public or private? Privacy settings vary by person. Material visible only to friends is not in the public domain and needs informed consent.
  • Anonymity. A quoted tweet can be traced back to its author by a simple search.
  • Exposure. Reproducing someone’s offensive post draws attention to them and creates a permanent record they can no longer delete.
  • Replicability. Platform terms of service limit what you can share, which makes it hard for others to reach the same data.

See research integrity & data ethics.

Visual material. The same principles apply to images and video: unit of analysis, sampling, reliability and validity, and what to count. Context matters more, because an image is hard to code without knowing how it was produced (Bock et al. 2011) (Clark et al. 2021). Wansink et al. (2016) screened 750 food paintings down to 140 showing small family meals. The most commonly eaten foods (chicken, eggs, squash) were painted least often, shellfish appeared most often in Germany, a country with little coastline, and 51.4% of the Dutch paintings showed tropical lemons. The paintings show aspiration, aesthetics or the painter’s skill, not what people ate (Clark et al. 2021). Kapidzic and Herring (2015) coded 400 teenage profile photos for distance, gaze and dress and found clear gender differences. Döring et al. coded their 500 selfies with Goffman’s (1979) and Kang’s (1997) gender-display categories plus three social-media ones (kissing pout, muscle presentation, faceless portrayal), and found traditional stereotypes reproduced (Clark et al. 2021).

Strengths and limits

Bryman’s balance sheet (Clark et al. 2021):

Advantages Disadvantages
Transparent: the scheme and sampling can be published and replicated Only as good as the documents: check authenticity, credibility and representativeness (Scott 1990)
Longitudinal: tracks change over long periods Coding manuals always need some interpretation from coders’ everyday knowledge
Flexible: almost any unstructured material Latent content is harder to code validly
Reaches the unreachable: leaders’ speeches and tweets Weak on “why?” questions
Unobtrusive and non-reactive (Webb et al. 1966) Can be atheoretical: counting what is measurable instead of what matters

Two of these need qualifying. Content analysis is non-reactive only if the documents were. Newspaper articles were not written for the analyst, but interview transcripts were shaped by the interview (Clark et al. 2021). And the “why?” problem is concrete. Beullens and Schepers found that photos showing alcohol positively and a brand logo got significantly more “likes”, but could only speculate about why. Seale et al.’s move from counts to qualitative reading is one remedy, and combining methods in a mixed-methods design is the general one (Clark et al. 2021). On the charge of being atheoretical, Döring et al.’s use of Goffman’s categories shows that a coding scheme can be built from theory (Clark et al. 2021).

Common pitfalls

WarningWhere this goes wrong
  • Building the coding scheme before the research questions. The scheme should follow from the questions. Otherwise it misses dimensions you need, or you sample the wrong documents. (Clark et al. 2021)
  • Sampling posts when you mean to describe people. A time window over-represents heavy posters. If the unit is the person, take a fixed number of items each, as Beullens and Schepers did. (Clark et al. 2021)
  • Confusing the unit of analysis with what is coded inside it. An article can contain several offences. Decide which one the data row describes, and be clear about which unit you are counting. (Clark et al. 2021)
  • Reporting percentage agreement alone. With one dominant category, high agreement is easy to get by chance. Report a chance-corrected statistic (kappa, or Krippendorff’s alpha) and the category shares. (Albers 2017)
  • Treating coder error as harmless noise in counts. Random misclassification moves items from big categories to small ones, so rare categories can be badly inflated. Pilot, train, and measure reliability before the main coding.
  • Reading representation as reality. Paintings show aspirational food; school websites under-report PE. A content analysis describes the documents, and claims about the world need an argument about how the documents were produced. (Clark et al. 2021)
  • Treating keyword counts as meaning. Software counts strings. Check words in context (KWIC) before you code them, as Bailey et al. did with epistemic markers. (Clark et al. 2021)

Appendix · the mathematics

Cohen’s kappa. Let p_o be the observed share of items on which two coders agree, and p_{1k}, p_{2k} the shares each coder assigns to category k. If the coders were independent, they would agree by chance with probability p_e = \sum_k p_{1k}\,p_{2k}. Then

\kappa = \frac{p_o - p_e}{1 - p_e},

the share of the possible above-chance agreement actually achieved. With K equally used categories, p_e = 1/K, which is Albers’ 20% for five codes.

The lazy coder. If coder 2 puts everything in category 1, then p_{2,1} = 1 and p_e = p_{1,1}. Agreement is p_o = p_{1,1} as well, so \kappa = 0 whatever the split. With a 72% category, that is 72% agreement and no information.

Agreement and kappa under random errors. Suppose each coder is right with probability a and otherwise picks one of the other K-1 categories at random, independently of the other coder. They agree if both are right, or both are wrong in the same way:

p_o = a^2 + \frac{(1-a)^2}{K-1}.

This does not depend on the category shares, which is why the black line in the figure is the same for both splits. With K = 4 and a = 0.9, p_o = 0.81 + 0.0033 \approx 0.813. But p_e does depend on the shares: it is 1/4 for equal categories and much larger when one category dominates, so the same p_o gives a smaller \kappa.

Misclassification bias in a count. Under the same error model, the expected coded share of category k with true share \pi_k is

\tilde\pi_k = a\,\pi_k + (1-a)\,\frac{1-\pi_k}{K-1}.

A category with \pi_k = 1/K is unchanged, and every other share is pulled towards 1/K. For the 72% category at a = 0.8: 0.8(0.72) + 0.2(0.28/3) \approx 0.595. For the 5% pooled “negative or unknown” group, each of its two categories gets 0.8\pi_k + 0.2(1-\pi_k)/3, which sums to 0.8(0.05) + 0.2(1.95)/3 = 0.17, more than three times the truth. If a is known from a reliability study, the relation can be inverted to correct the shares, \hat\pi_k = \big[(K-1)\tilde\pi_k - (1-a)\big]/(Ka - 1), but real coding errors are rarely this uniform, which is why the better remedy is a reliable scheme.

## Why it matters

Some of the most interesting social data were never collected by a researcher. You cannot interview a
prime minister about the environment or get them to fill in a questionnaire, but you can analyse every
speech they have given [@clark_bryman_2021]. Questions such as *when did the press start covering this
story, which papers covered it most, and did journalists' stance change?* are questions about documents,
and content analysis is the method that answers them [@clark_bryman_2021].

It is also where a great deal of student and professional research now starts, because the raw material
is already sitting in newspaper archives, on websites and on social media platforms. That makes it look
easy. It isn't. Turning a newspaper article into a row of numbers takes many decisions (what counts as
the unit, which categories exist, what to do with an ambiguous case), and a different coder may make
each of them differently. Most of this entry is about making those decisions explicit enough to
survive replication.

## What content analysis is

The most quoted definition is Berelson's (1952): content analysis is "a research technique for the
objective, systematic and quantitative description of the manifest content of communication"
[@clark_bryman_2021]. Holsti (1969) widens it to "any technique for making inferences by objectively
and systematically identifying specified characteristics of messages" [@clark_bryman_2021].

Both definitions stress two qualities. Being **objective** means the rules for assigning material to
categories are fixed in advance and written down, so that personal bias intrudes as little as possible.
Being **systematic** means applying those rules consistently. The rules themselves may reflect the
researcher's interests. What matters is that, once written, anyone could apply them and replicate the
result. Krippendorff (2018) puts it as describing "how we derive our judgements, so that
others—especially our critics—can replicate our results or build on them" [@clark_bryman_2021].

The two definitions differ in two ways [@clark_bryman_2021]:

- **Manifest vs latent content.** Berelson limits the method to *manifest* content, what an item is
  clearly about. Holsti's "specified characteristics" allows *latent* content, the meanings beneath the
  surface, which have to be interpreted. Siegel et al. (2013) coded alcohol-brand references in US
  popular songs from 2009 to 2011. The brands and types of drink mentioned were manifest. Whether the
  song presented the drinking and its consequences positively, negatively or neutrally was latent. Some
  authors doubt that latent content can be measured quantitatively at all (Ahuvia 2001), and others
  point out that the line between the two is often fuzzy (Riffe et al. 2014).
- **Communication vs messages.** Berelson wrote about mass media. Holsti's "messages" opens the method
  to any unstructured text, including interview transcripts and open-ended survey answers.

Bryman's own definition (Key concept 13.1) puts the quantitative version on the deductive side:
**predetermined categories**, applied to test theories or hypotheses. *Qualitative* content analysis
lets themes emerge from the data and pays more attention to context. The contrast is set out in
[qualitative evidence](qualitative-evidence.qmd) [@clark_bryman_2021].

The range of material is wide. Bryman's examples include newspaper coverage of the UK sugar tax
(Buckton et al. 2018), online dog obituaries (MacKay et al. 2016), gender on primetime television
(Sink and Mastro 2017), the Queen's Speech (John and Jennings 2010), European Parliament speeches
(Proksch and Slapin 2010), sex-discrimination case narratives (Bobbitt-Zeher 2011), Instagram selfies
(Döring et al. 2016), meals in paintings (Wansink et al. 2016), conditions in IMF loan agreements
(Kentikelenis et al. 2016) and populism in party press releases across 11 national elections (Bernhard
and Kriesi 2019) [@clark_bryman_2021].

## Step 1: fix the research questions first

As with a survey, the questions come first. They decide which documents you sample and which
dimensions the coding scheme must capture. Leave them vague and you risk analysing the wrong material
or building a scheme that misses what you care about [@clark_bryman_2021]. Most content analyses
revolve around **who** gets included, **what** gets included, **where** an issue appears, its
**location** within the item, **how much** coverage it gets, and **why** [@clark_bryman_2021].

Two further kinds of question are distinctive:

- **Omissions.** What is *not* reported can matter as much as what is. If government reviews of
  community care say a lot about "costs" and "efficiencies" but almost nothing about "quality",
  "choice" or "service user satisfaction", that silence is a finding [@clark_bryman_2021].
- **Change over time.** Young and Dugas (2011) compared climate-change coverage in two national
  Canadian newspapers in 1988–9, 1998–9 and 2007–8, using the search terms "climate change", "global
  warming", "greenhouse effect" and "greenhouse gas". Claims that climate change is caused by humans
  declined progressively across the three periods. University experts and government employees became
  less prominent as voices, while industry associations and environmental groups became more prominent.
  Stoddart et al. (2016) extended the series to 1997–2010 and found similar trends
  [@clark_bryman_2021].

## Step 2: sample the media, then the dates

Many studies take the form "the representation of **X** in **Y**": trade unions, crime or social
science research (X) in newspapers, television, podcasts, tweets or speeches (Y) [@clark_bryman_2021].
Once Y is chosen, the sample has to be narrowed. For newspapers: tabloids and/or broadsheets, weekday
and/or weekend, national and/or local, paid-for and/or free. Then: all items, or news items only, with
or without features and letters? Most studies pick one or two media, though Stroobant et al. (2018)
analysed a month of Belgian health news across newspapers, magazines, radio, television and news
websites [@clark_bryman_2021].

Dates are the second stage.

- **Event-driven designs** have one date fixed by the question. Bligh et al. (2004) tested Weber's idea
  that charismatic leadership emerges in crises by comparing George W. Bush's speeches before and after
  11 September 2001. His rhetoric, and the media's portrayal of him, became more charismatic. They still
  had to choose an end date; their last speech was on 11 March 2002, which raises the question of how
  long a crisis effect should be expected to last. Gunn and Naper (2016) analysed **3,420 tweets** from
  the Obama and Romney accounts in 2012. Election day fixed the end date, and they chose 1 January 2012
  as the start to cover the whole campaign [@clark_bryman_2021].
- **Ongoing phenomena** leave the dates open, and probability sampling can be adapted to them. Döring
  et al. (2016) drew **250 female and 250 male selfies** from Instagram in April 2014, found through
  hashtags such as #selfie and #me, taking every tenth qualifying picture until they reached 500
  [@clark_bryman_2021]. That is a [systematic sample](../02-inference/sampling-and-survey-design.qmd).

Watch for designs that over-represent some kinds of item. Beullens and Schepers (2013) coded the **20
most recent status updates** on each of 160 Belgian students' Facebook profiles, instead of all updates
in a fixed period, "to avoid the overrepresentation of frequent and active Facebook users"
[@clark_bryman_2021]. Sampling a time window would have given the heaviest posters the most weight.
Taking a fixed number per person gives each person equal weight, so the sample describes users, not
posts.

Large online populations usually need reducing. Humphreys et al. (2014) collected **101,069 tweets**,
then drew random samples of **1,050 sent online and 1,050 sent by text message**, because the channel
was one of their variables. Small populations can be coded in full: Ledford and Anderson (2013) coded
all **49 company posts and 655 user posts** in the ten days after Novartis used Facebook to announce a
recall of Excedrin [@clark_bryman_2021].

## Step 3: decide what to count

What you count follows from the research questions. Bryman groups the usual units into four kinds
[@clark_bryman_2021].

**Significant actors.** Who wrote the item (a general or specialist reporter)? Who is its main focus
(a politician, an expert, a spokesperson)? Who supplies alternative voices? What was the occasion (an
interview, a report's release, an event)? Coding these maps who gets to speak in the news.

**Words.** Word choice can reveal, for example, a tendency to sensationalise. Bailey et al. (2014)
counted "epistemic markers", words that signal uncertainty, in climate-change coverage in two US and
two Spanish newspapers in 2001 and 2007, the years of IPCC reports. The markers included activities
with uncertain outcomes ("predicting"), quantitative uncertainty ("likelihood"), challenges to the IPCC
("debate"), references to opponents ("deniers") and modifiers ("controversial"). Context was checked
before any term was marked. Markers were more frequent in the US papers in both years and rose between
2001 and 2007 in both countries, even though scientific understanding had strengthened in that period
[@clark_bryman_2021].

Word counting is where **computer-assisted content analysis** (CACA) comes in. Software removes human
inconsistency and fatigue, produces **key-word-in-context** (KWIC) listings, finds co-occurrences and
handles very large volumes. It cannot handle nuance, depends on a list of search terms that is hard to
make complete, and invites a focus on frequency over meaning (Bligh and Kohles 2014)
[@clark_bryman_2021]. Seale et al. (2006) used Wordsmith for a *comparative keyword analysis* of one
day's posts (20 April 2005) on the two largest UK online support forums for breast and prostate cancer.
Men with prostate cancer used more words about research, treatment and diagnosis ("study", "radical
prostatectomy", "biopsy"). Women with breast cancer used more words about feelings ("scared") and people
("family") and more superlatives ("amazing"). The counts were a starting point for a qualitative
reading of the word clusters [@clark_bryman_2021]. Bligh et al.'s Bush study used DICTION, which works
from ready-made or user-built dictionaries. Automated **sentiment analysis**, such as SentiStrength or
the scoring in Greaves et al.'s (2014) study of tweets to NHS hospitals, is another form
[@clark_bryman_2021]. Doing this at scale is the subject of [text as data](../04-predictive/text-as-data.qmd).

**Subjects and themes.** This means classifying the item. Fenton et al. (1998) sorted social science
reported in the British media into seven disciplines. Buckton et al. (2018) read each sugar-tax article
in full and recorded, for each theme, whether the article overtly contained it; a *Daily Mail*
sentence about halving sugar intake "to tackle the growing obesity and diabetes crisis" was coded as
linking sugar to both obesity and diabetes. Kentikelenis et al. (2016) searched **4,590 IMF loan
documents** from 1985 to 2014 and extracted **55,465 conditions** across **131 countries**. Conditions
rose after 2008 even as the number of lending programmes fell [@clark_bryman_2021]. Thematic coding
often moves from manifest to latent content.

**Dispositions.** This is the stance an item takes: for or against, positive or negative. Buckton et al.
also coded whether each article favoured the sugar tax. Bobbitt-Zeher (2011) coded discrimination
narratives for **descriptive** stereotyping (assumptions about women in general, or that women's traits
don't fit a job) and **prescriptive** stereotyping (a particular woman violating gender expectations).
Descriptive stereotyping appeared in **44%** of narratives and prescriptive in **8%**. Of the remaining
48%, most (37%) showed no stereotyping and the rest were coded "other". Sink and Mastro (2017) coded
**1,254 characters in 89 primetime programmes** across nine US networks over ten weeks. Their coders
were told to judge as "the average American television viewer" would, using the definitions in the
**codebook**. Some stereotypes had declined, but dominant men and sexually provocative women persisted
[@clark_bryman_2021].

## Step 4: the coding schedule and the coding manual

A coding scheme has two parts [@clark_bryman_2021]:

- The **coding schedule** is the form on which each item's codes are entered: one column per dimension,
  one form per item. Each completed form becomes one row of a data file for SPSS, R or Stata.
- The **coding manual** is the instruction book. It lists every dimension, every category within each
  dimension, the number assigned to each category, and guidance on how to decide between them.

Bryman builds a toy example about crime reporting in UK national newspapers. It has nine dimensions:
the nature of the offence, the gender, social class and age of the perpetrator and of the victim, the
depiction of the victim, and the position of the item. The class categories follow Goldthorpe et al.'s
scheme, with "unemployed", "retired", "housewife" and "other" added because newspapers use those words.
The offence categories follow Home Office recording rules, which makes the results comparable with
official crime data. Room is left for two offences per article, with the main one coded first. A
*Sunday Times* murder-charge story and a *Daily Star* assault story each become a line of
digits [@clark_bryman_2021]. That comparability point links directly to
[using existing data](using-existing-data.qmd), where the official series has its own production
process.

The scheme is a measuring instrument, and Bryman's rules for it are the same as for a questionnaire or
an observation schedule [@clark_bryman_2021]:

| Rule | What it guards against |
|---|---|
| **Discrete dimensions** | two dimensions measuring overlapping things |
| **Mutually exclusive categories** | an item that fits two categories, coded inconsistently |
| **Exhaustive categories** | an item that fits no category |
| **Clear instructions** | coder discretion; the manual may need to be long |
| **A clear unit of analysis** | confusing the item (the article) with what is coded in it (each offence) |

**Pilot the scheme.** A pilot shows where coders are unsure which category applies and which cases have
no code. It also shows whether one category is swallowing most of the items. If so, split it, so that
you can see detail inside it [@clark_bryman_2021]. The figure below shows a second reason a dominant
category matters.

## Reliability: would another coder get the same numbers?

Coding has to be consistent **between coders** (inter-rater reliability) and **for each coder over
time** (intra-rater reliability). A lone student coding a dissertation escapes the first problem but not
the second, which is why the manual matters even with one coder [@clark_bryman_2021]. Bryman notes that
observers *can* be trained to use complex schedules with considerable reliability, and that reliability
is a precondition for validity [@clark_bryman_2021].

Albers explains how to put a number on it [@albers_quant_data]:

- **Percentage agreement** ("joint probability of agreement") is the share of items both coders put in
  the same category: 73 of 100 is 73%. It is easy to compute and the least robust measure, because some
  agreement happens by chance. Two coders assigning one of five codes at random would agree 20% of the
  time. If near-misses are to count as agreement on an ordered scale, that tolerance has to be fixed
  before the analysis, not adjusted afterwards to reach an acceptable figure.
- **Cohen's kappa** corrects for chance agreement. It treats categories as nominal, runs from −1 to 1,
  and 0 means chance-level agreement. Albers gives the usual bands: 0–0.20 poor, 0.21–0.40 fair,
  0.41–0.60 moderate, 0.61–0.80 substantial, 0.81–1 excellent. He suggests that a value above 0.5 is
  needed to support validity claims, and warns that kappa is higher with fewer codes and depends on how
  often each code is used.
- **Fleiss' kappa** extends this to three or more coders. **Krippendorff's alpha** handles several
  coders and nominal, ordinal, interval or ratio codes, and has a long history in coding texts,
  open-ended answers and observations.

The chance correction matters most when one category dominates, which is common in content analysis.
Beullens and Schepers found alcohol shown in a positive light in **72%** of the Facebook photos they
coded, neutral in **23%**, and negative or unknown in the remaining **5%** [@clark_bryman_2021]. The
figure uses that split.

```{python}
#| label: fig-content-coding
#| fig-cap: "Two coders, simulated (our own illustration). 500 items fall into four categories in Beullens and Schepers' proportions (72% / 23% / 3% / 2%) or in equal shares. Each coder picks the right category with a given accuracy and otherwise picks one of the other three at random. Left: raw agreement is the same whatever the category shares, but kappa is lower when one category dominates. A 'lazy' coder who calls every item positive agrees with a perfect coder 72% of the time, with a kappa of zero. Right: coder error also biases the counts. The coded share of the big category shrinks towards one-quarter, and the coded share of the rare categories grows."
#| code-fold: true
#| code-summary: "Show the simulation code"

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(13)
K, N, R = 4, 500, 300                       # categories, items, replications
skew = np.array([0.72, 0.23, 0.03, 0.02])
flat = np.full(K, 0.25)
acc = np.linspace(0.5, 1.0, 11)

def code(truth, a):
    """Right with probability a, otherwise one of the other K-1 categories."""
    ok = rng.random(truth.shape) < a
    return np.where(ok, truth, (truth + rng.integers(1, K, truth.shape)) % K)

def agreement_and_kappa(c1, c2):
    po = (c1 == c2).mean(-1)
    p1 = np.stack([(c1 == k).mean(-1) for k in range(K)], -1)
    p2 = np.stack([(c2 == k).mean(-1) for k in range(K)], -1)
    pe = (p1 * p2).sum(-1)
    return po.mean(), ((po - pe) / (1 - pe)).mean()

res = {}
for name, pi in [("skew", skew), ("flat", flat)]:
    po_k, kap_k, big, rare = [], [], [], []
    for a in acc:
        truth = rng.choice(K, size=(R, N), p=pi)
        c1, c2 = code(truth, a), code(truth, a)
        po, kap = agreement_and_kappa(c1, c2)
        po_k.append(po); kap_k.append(kap)
        big.append((c1 == 0).mean()); rare.append((c1 >= 2).mean())
    res[name] = dict(po=np.array(po_k), kap=np.array(kap_k), big=np.array(big), rare=np.array(rare))

truth = rng.choice(K, size=(R, N), p=skew)
lazy_po, lazy_kap = agreement_and_kappa(truth, np.zeros_like(truth))

fig, (axL, axR) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(9.4, 3.9))

axL.plot(100 * acc, res["skew"]["po"], color="#161613", lw=2, label="raw agreement (either split)")
axL.plot(100 * acc, res["flat"]["kap"], color="#5e5e54", lw=1.8, ls="--", label="kappa, equal shares")
axL.plot(100 * acc, res["skew"]["kap"], color="#e8531d", lw=2.2, label="kappa, 72/23/3/2 split")
axL.scatter([100], [lazy_po], s=40, color="#8a8a7e", zorder=3)
axL.scatter([100], [lazy_kap], s=40, color="#8a8a7e", zorder=3)
axL.text(98, 0.03, f"grey dots: a lazy coder (always\n'positive') vs a perfect coder:\n"
         f"agreement {lazy_po:.2f}, kappa {lazy_kap:.2f}", fontsize=8, color="#5e5e54",
         ha="right", va="bottom")
axL.set_ylim(-0.05, 1.05)
axL.set_title("Agreement flatters; kappa corrects for chance", fontsize=10)
axL.set_xlabel("each coder's accuracy (%)")
axL.set_ylabel("agreement / kappa")
axL.legend(fontsize=8, frameon=False, loc="upper left", bbox_to_anchor=(0, 0.78))

axR.axhline(72, color="#b7b6a8", lw=1.2, ls=":")
axR.axhline(5, color="#b7b6a8", lw=1.2, ls=":")
axR.plot(100 * acc, 100 * res["skew"]["big"], color="#161613", lw=2, label="coded share 'positive' (true 72%)")
axR.plot(100 * acc, 100 * res["skew"]["rare"], color="#5e5e54", lw=1.8, ls="--",
         label="coded share 'negative/unknown' (true 5%)")
axR.set_ylim(0, 80)
axR.set_title("Unreliable coding biases the counts", fontsize=10)
axR.set_xlabel("coder's accuracy (%)")
axR.set_ylabel("share of items (%)")
axR.legend(fontsize=8, frameon=False, loc="center right")

fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

Read the left panel at 90% accuracy. Raw agreement is about 0.81 under either split. Kappa is about 0.75
when the four categories are equally common but about 0.63 under the 72/23/3/2 split, because two coders
who both say "positive" most of the time agree a lot by chance. A coder who doesn't read the photos at
all and codes everything "positive" reaches 72% agreement with a perfect coder, close to two genuinely
85%-accurate coders, and kappa correctly scores this as zero. (Albers' warning that kappa depends on code
frequencies is the same point seen from the other side: equally good coders get a lower kappa when one
code dominates, so kappas from studies with different category shares are not directly comparable
[@albers_quant_data].)

The right panel is the reason reliability is not a formality. Random coding errors are not neutral in
a count. They move items *out of* big categories and *into* small ones, so the coded share of the
dominant category is too low and the rare categories are inflated. At 80% accuracy the "positive" share
comes out near 60% and the "negative or unknown" share more than triples, from 5% to about 17% (our
simulation; see the appendix). With rare categories, a small error rate can be most of what you count.

## Online and visual material

Websites, blogs, forums and social media are documents in their own right, but who made them and why
differs. Bryman's four broad categories each bring their own problems [@clark_bryman_2021].

**Websites.** Ask Scott's (1990) questions of any document: why does it exist, and for whom? Kahan et al.
(2019) analysed the websites of a representative sample of **759 US charter elementary schools** in
spring 2018. **97%** had a working website, but **52%** did not mention any of five common
physical-activity programmes, physical education appeared on only **34%**, and only **2%** gave enough
information to work out how much PE pupils received. The sites were too thin to support wide
generalisations [@clark_bryman_2021]. Practical problems include search engines that cover only part of
the web and rank results through advertising and optimisation, results that depend on your keywords,
and sites that appear, disappear and change. Record the date you accessed each site. The Internet
Archive's Wayback Machine holds over 411 billion pages. Underneath all of this is the general problem of
documentary research: it is hard to define the population you are sampling from, and online that
population keeps changing [@clark_bryman_2021].

**Blogs** share these problems. Boepple and Thompson (2014) coded **21 award-winning "healthy living"
blogs** with the most page views. Five bloggers used very negative language about being fat and four
expressed admiration for thinness; the authors judged much of the content "potentially problematic"
for readers [@clark_bryman_2021].

**Forums.** Davis et al. (2015) coded the **1,094 comments** posted under a blog about Amanda Todd's
cyberbullying suicide. **482** contained stories of being bullied: 12% about cyberbullying, 75% about
traditional bullying, the rest a mixture. Physical appearance was the most common reason given, and the
authors concluded that victims are often targeted for not conforming to mainstream norms
[@clark_bryman_2021].

**Social media** raise four questions before you code anything [@clark_bryman_2021]:

- **Public or private?** Privacy settings vary by person. Material visible only to friends is not in
  the public domain and needs informed consent.
- **Anonymity.** A quoted tweet can be traced back to its author by a simple search.
- **Exposure.** Reproducing someone's offensive post draws attention to them and creates a permanent
  record they can no longer delete.
- **Replicability.** Platform terms of service limit what you can share, which makes it hard for
  others to reach the same data.

See [research integrity & data ethics](../06-field-realities/research-integrity-and-data-ethics.qmd).

**Visual material.** The same principles apply to images and video: unit of analysis, sampling,
reliability and validity, and what to count. Context matters more, because an image is hard to code
without knowing how it was produced (Bock et al. 2011) [@clark_bryman_2021]. Wansink et al. (2016)
screened **750 food paintings down to 140** showing small family meals. The most commonly eaten foods
(chicken, eggs, squash) were painted least often, shellfish appeared most often in Germany, a country
with little coastline, and **51.4%** of the Dutch paintings showed tropical lemons. The paintings show
aspiration, aesthetics or the painter's skill, not what people ate [@clark_bryman_2021]. Kapidzic and
Herring (2015) coded **400 teenage profile photos** for distance, gaze and dress and found clear gender
differences. Döring et al. coded their 500 selfies with Goffman's (1979) and Kang's (1997) gender-display
categories plus three social-media ones (kissing pout, muscle presentation, faceless portrayal), and
found traditional stereotypes reproduced [@clark_bryman_2021].

## Strengths and limits

Bryman's balance sheet [@clark_bryman_2021]:

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| **Transparent**: the scheme and sampling can be published and replicated | Only as good as the **documents**: check authenticity, credibility and representativeness (Scott 1990) |
| **Longitudinal**: tracks change over long periods | Coding manuals always need some **interpretation** from coders' everyday knowledge |
| **Flexible**: almost any unstructured material | **Latent content** is harder to code validly |
| **Reaches the unreachable**: leaders' speeches and tweets | Weak on **"why?"** questions |
| **Unobtrusive** and non-reactive (Webb et al. 1966) | Can be **atheoretical**: counting what is measurable instead of what matters |

Two of these need qualifying. Content analysis is non-reactive only if the documents were. Newspaper
articles were not written for the analyst, but interview transcripts were shaped by the interview
[@clark_bryman_2021]. And the "why?" problem is concrete. Beullens and Schepers found that photos showing
alcohol positively *and* a brand logo got significantly more "likes", but could only speculate about
why. Seale et al.'s move from counts to qualitative reading is one remedy, and combining methods in a
[mixed-methods design](mixed-methods-designs.qmd) is the general one [@clark_bryman_2021]. On the charge
of being atheoretical, Döring et al.'s use of Goffman's categories shows that a coding scheme can be
built from theory [@clark_bryman_2021].

## Common pitfalls

::: {.callout-warning title="Where this goes wrong"}
- **Building the coding scheme before the research questions.** The scheme should follow from the
  questions. Otherwise it misses dimensions you need, or you sample the wrong documents.
  [@clark_bryman_2021]
- **Sampling posts when you mean to describe people.** A time window over-represents heavy posters. If
  the unit is the person, take a fixed number of items each, as Beullens and Schepers did.
  [@clark_bryman_2021]
- **Confusing the unit of analysis with what is coded inside it.** An article can contain several
  offences. Decide which one the data row describes, and be clear about which unit you are counting.
  [@clark_bryman_2021]
- **Reporting percentage agreement alone.** With one dominant category, high agreement is easy to get
  by chance. Report a chance-corrected statistic (kappa, or Krippendorff's alpha) and the category
  shares. [@albers_quant_data]
- **Treating coder error as harmless noise in counts.** Random misclassification moves items from big
  categories to small ones, so rare categories can be badly inflated. Pilot, train, and measure
  reliability before the main coding.
- **Reading representation as reality.** Paintings show aspirational food; school websites under-report
  PE. A content analysis describes the documents, and claims about the world need an argument about how
  the documents were produced. [@clark_bryman_2021]
- **Treating keyword counts as meaning.** Software counts strings. Check words in context (KWIC)
  before you code them, as Bailey et al. did with epistemic markers. [@clark_bryman_2021]
:::

## Appendix · the mathematics {.unnumbered}

::: {.math-appendix}

**Cohen's kappa.** Let $p_o$ be the observed share of items on which two coders agree, and $p_{1k}$,
$p_{2k}$ the shares each coder assigns to category $k$. If the coders were independent, they would
agree by chance with probability $p_e = \sum_k p_{1k}\,p_{2k}$. Then

$$
\kappa = \frac{p_o - p_e}{1 - p_e},
$$

the share of the *possible* above-chance agreement actually achieved. With $K$ equally used categories,
$p_e = 1/K$, which is Albers' 20% for five codes.

**The lazy coder.** If coder 2 puts everything in category 1, then $p_{2,1} = 1$ and
$p_e = p_{1,1}$. Agreement is $p_o = p_{1,1}$ as well, so $\kappa = 0$ whatever the split. With a 72%
category, that is 72% agreement and no information.

**Agreement and kappa under random errors.** Suppose each coder is right with probability $a$ and
otherwise picks one of the other $K-1$ categories at random, independently of the other coder. They
agree if both are right, or both are wrong in the same way:

$$
p_o = a^2 + \frac{(1-a)^2}{K-1}.
$$

This does not depend on the category shares, which is why the black line in the figure is the same
for both splits. With $K = 4$ and $a = 0.9$, $p_o = 0.81 + 0.0033 \approx 0.813$. But $p_e$ does
depend on the shares: it is $1/4$ for equal categories and much larger when one category dominates, so
the same $p_o$ gives a smaller $\kappa$.

**Misclassification bias in a count.** Under the same error model, the expected coded share of category
$k$ with true share $\pi_k$ is

$$
\tilde\pi_k = a\,\pi_k + (1-a)\,\frac{1-\pi_k}{K-1}.
$$

A category with $\pi_k = 1/K$ is unchanged, and every other share is pulled towards $1/K$. For the 72% category at $a = 0.8$: $0.8(0.72) + 0.2(0.28/3) \approx 0.595$. For the 5% pooled
"negative or unknown" group, each of its two categories gets $0.8\pi_k + 0.2(1-\pi_k)/3$, which sums
to $0.8(0.05) + 0.2(1.95)/3 = 0.17$, more than three times the truth. If $a$ is known from a reliability
study, the relation can be inverted to correct the shares,
$\hat\pi_k = \big[(K-1)\tilde\pi_k - (1-a)\big]/(Ka - 1)$, but real coding errors are rarely this
uniform, which is why the better remedy is a reliable scheme.

:::
