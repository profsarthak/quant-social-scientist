Asking Questions: How the Instrument Shapes the Answer

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source

A survey answer is not a fact you collect. It is produced by a question, a format, an order, a mode, and sometimes a person asking. Design all five.

A careful sample can still produce wrong numbers if the questions are badly built. Wording, answer format, question order, who asks, and whether anyone asks at all (interview vs self-completion) all shape what people say. Most of these effects are systematic, so a larger sample does not remove them. Questionnaire design is measurement design.

Why it matters

Bryman lists three sources of error in social surveys: sampling error, data-collection error (poor question wording, poor interviewing, flawed instruments), and data-processing error (mistakes in coding and entering answers) (Clark et al. 2021). The sampling entry covers the first. This entry covers the other two. Bryman notes they relate “much more closely to concerns about the validity of measurement” (Clark et al. 2021).

A few findings show the size of the problem:

  • Order. In 1988 the Crime Survey for England and Wales accidentally asked “would you say the police in this area do a good job or a poor job?” twice for about half of respondents: once early on, and again after questions about contact with the police. Of those asked twice, 66% gave the same rating, 22% rated the police more positively the second time, and only 13% less positively (Mayhew 2000) (Clark et al. 2021). The respondents were the same people. Only the question’s position changed.
  • Mode. Among people known to have a criminal conviction, 67% admitted it in a postal questionnaire but only 58% in a face-to-face interview (Preisendörfer & Wolter 2014) (Clark et al. 2021).
  • Format. In Schuman and Presser’s study of what people want from a job, about 31% chose “a feeling of accomplishment” from a closed list, but only about 14.5% said something similar when the question was open (Clark et al. 2021).
  • Say vs do. In the 1930s LaPiere travelled across the United States with a Chinese couple. Only 1 of 66 hotels and none of 184 restaurants turned them away. When the same establishments were later sent a questionnaire asking whether they would accept Chinese guests, 92% of restaurants and 91% of hotels that replied said “No” (Clark et al. 2021).

None of these errors gets smaller with a larger n. They are bias, not noise.

Two ways to administer a survey

A survey is delivered either by an interviewer reading from an interview schedule (a structured or standardised interview, face-to-face, by video, or by phone as CAPI/CATI) or by a self-completion questionnaire (postal, email, online) (Clark et al. 2021). The structured interview’s central idea is standardisation. Each respondent should receive “exactly the same interview stimulus”: the same words, in the same order, with closed answer options where possible, so that differences in answers reflect differences between people and not differences in how they were asked (Clark et al. 2021).

Standardisation fails in two ways. Intra-interviewer variability is one interviewer being inconsistent. Inter-interviewer variability is interviewers being inconsistent with each other (Clark et al. 2021). Every departure adds variation that comes from error, not from the people being measured, and “error reduces the validity of a measure” (Clark et al. 2021). The effect can be measured. English experiments found that for about two-thirds of the questions studied, interviewers contributed less than 2% of the total variation (Collins 1997). That leaves a third of questions where they contributed more (Clark et al. 2021).

So interviewers are trained in controlled moves:

  • Probing asks for more when an answer is incomplete, with neutral probes such as “Could you say a little more about that?” Bryman calls probing “a highly problematic area” and says it should be kept to a minimum (Clark et al. 2021).
  • Prompting suggests possible answers. Every closed question is a standardised prompt. Offering options only to respondents who seem to be struggling is not acceptable (Clark et al. 2021).
  • Show cards present long lists, Likert batches, or sensitive bands (age, income) so the respondent can point instead of saying a number out loud (Clark et al. 2021).

Which mode? Neither mode wins on every criterion. Bryman’s comparison table rates in-person interviews best for response rates and self-completion modes best for minimising social desirability, with telephone in between (Clark et al. 2021). The research is consistent that people disclose more without an interviewer present. They report more drug use, alcohol consumption, sexual partners and abortions on self-completion forms than in interviews (Tourangeau & Smith 1996), and online more than on the phone (Tourangeau et al. 2013) (Clark et al. 2021). Self-completion has its own costs. Nobody can probe or clarify. The respondent can read the whole form, so question-order effects cannot be controlled. And the wrong person may fill it in: in one U.S. household survey 18% of respondents were not the intended person (Olson & Smyth 2014) (Clark et al. 2021).

Open or closed?

Open questions let respondents answer in their own terms, do not suggest answers, and are good for exploring new territory. But they must be post-coded, which is slow and unreliable. Bryman classes post-coding variability as data-processing error (Clark et al. 2021). Closed questions are quick, comparable, and easy to process. But they can omit what matters, force overlapping or non-exhaustive choices, and put words in people’s mouths (Clark et al. 2021).

Schuman and Presser’s work-values experiment shows the trade-off (Clark et al. 2021). In a first round, 60% of the categories people produced in the open format were not covered by the closed options. They rebuilt the closed list from the open answers and ran both again:

What people most want in a job Closed format Open format
Pay 13.2% 16.7%
Feeling of accomplishment 31.0% 14.5%
Control of work 11.7% 4.6%
Pleasant work 19.8% 14.5%
Security 20.3% 7.6%
Share covered by these five 96% 57.9%

Even after the redesign, only 58% of open answers fitted the closed categories, and “accomplishment” was chosen about twice as often when it was offered (Clark et al. 2021). A closed list is not a neutral container. It tells respondents which answers are expected. The practical lesson Bryman draws is to build the closed options from open answers, usually in a pilot (Clark et al. 2021).

When categories are coded, Bryman gives three rules: categories must not overlap, must be exhaustive (hence “other”), and must have clear assignment rules written in a coding frame (Clark et al. 2021).

Response sets: consistent, lawful, and irrelevant

Some bias comes from how people answer a series of questions, not from any single question. Webb et al. call these response sets, “irrelevant but lawful sources of variance”. They are consistent patterns of answering that have nothing to do with the concept being measured (Clark et al. 2021). The two main ones:

  • Acquiescence (“yea-saying” or “nay-saying”) is a tendency to agree, or disagree, with everything. It is a form of satisficing, where respondents “settle for merely satisfactory” answers instead of accurate ones (Krosnick 1999). Other forms include always taking the middle option or the last one listed (Clark et al. 2021).
  • Social desirability bias means answers shift towards what looks good. Charity is over-reported and heavy drinking under-reported, and sensitive questions also cause item non-response (Clark et al. 2021). Remedies include removing the interviewer (self-completion) and “forgiving” wording (“Everybody does it”). Minson et al. (2018) found that questions which presuppose a problem drew more disclosure of undesirable work behaviour than questions that presupposed there was none, or general questions (Clark et al. 2021).

The standard defence against acquiescence is a balanced scale. Some items are worded so that agreeing signals more of the trait, others so that agreeing signals less. The reversed items are then reverse-coded before summing. Bryman’s job-satisfaction example (from Brayfield & Rothe’s 18-item scale) includes “It seems that my friends are more interested in their jobs”, where disagreeing indicates satisfaction. Someone who agrees with all 18 items is showing a response set, and “their responses would not provide a valid assessment” (Clark et al. 2021). The figure below simulates what this buys you.

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(11)
n, k = 1000, 6
trait = rng.normal(0, 1, n)               # latent job satisfaction
yea = rng.random(n) < 0.15                # respondents who agree with everything

def administer(keys):
    """keys: +1 = agreeing signals satisfaction, -1 = reverse-worded item."""
    latent = trait[:, None] * keys[None, :] + rng.normal(0, 0.8, (n, k))
    raw = np.clip(np.round(3 + 1.1 * latent), 1, 5)
    raw[yea] = rng.choice([4, 5], size=(yea.sum(), k))
    scored = np.where(keys > 0, raw, 6 - raw)       # reverse-code before scoring
    return raw, scored

raw_same, score_same = administer(np.ones(k))
raw_bal, _ = administer(np.array([1, 1, 1, -1, -1, -1]))

Bryman notes a cost. Respondents are more likely to misread a question when its direction changes, so reversed items need careful wording (Clark et al. 2021).

Rules for writing a question

Bryman’s general test is to answer the question yourself. “Do you have a car?” is ambiguous: own one, or have access to one? “This week” is vague about which days count (Clark et al. 2021). Hard questions are costly as well as unclear, because they produce more non-substantive and middle answers (Lenzner 2012) (Clark et al. 2021). The specific rules:

Avoid Example Fix
Ambiguous terms “often”, “regularly”, “family”, “dinner” give concrete frequencies and definitions
Double-barrelled questions satisfaction with “pay and conditions” one question per idea
Leading or loaded wording “Do you agree with the view that…?” neutral, balanced phrasing
Negatives and double negatives “Would you rather not use non-perishable foods?” state it positively
Technical terms and acronyms “alienated”, “TUC” plain words
Stretching memory cinema visits in the last 12 months a shorter window (last month)
Presupposing behaviour “Which party did you vote for?” a filter question first
Overlapping or incomplete options age bands 30–40 / 40–50 mutually exclusive, exhaustive bands
Unbalanced scales Excellent / Good / Acceptable / Poor as many negative as positive points
“Tick all that apply” a checklist a yes/no for each item, which yields more selections (Smyth et al. 2006)

(Clark et al. 2021)

On the middle option and “don’t know”, Bryman’s reading of the evidence is to include both. Leaving out the midpoint increases item non-response. Midpoint answers mostly reflect genuinely having no opinion. And “don’t know” answers reflect less knowledge than even incorrect answers do (Jessee 2017) (Clark et al. 2021).

Question order. The evidence is inconsistent, but there are well-documented cases. Americans were less likely to call their taxes too high after being asked whether government spending should rise (Schuman & Presser) (Clark et al. 2021). The working rules: keep the order fixed across respondents, open with questions relevant to the topic (not demographics), put sensitive questions later, group related questions, go from general to specific, and ask attitudes before behaviour and knowledge (Clark et al. 2021).

Test it before you trust it

Bryman treats piloting as essential, especially for self-completion questionnaires, where no interviewer is present to repair confusion. A pilot shows which questions everyone answers the same way (which tell you nothing), which make people uncomfortable, and which are skipped or misunderstood. It can also turn open answers into closed options. Pilot on a group comparable to the sample, not on people who will be in it (Clark et al. 2021). Cognitive interviewing goes further. Respondents “think out loud” while answering, which shows how they interpret terms such as “working for pay” or “ill-health” (Clark et al. 2021). Where possible, reuse existing questions. They have in effect already been piloted, and they allow comparison with earlier studies (Clark et al. 2021).

Common pitfalls

WarningWhere this goes wrong
  • Treating a survey percentage as a fact about the world. It is a fact about the answers to that question, in that position, in that mode. The police-rating result shifted just because the question was asked twice. Report exact wording and mode alongside the number. (Clark et al. 2021)
  • Assuming a bigger sample fixes it. Wording, order and mode effects are bias. They do not average out. See the two kinds of error.
  • Writing closed options from the armchair. In Schuman and Presser’s first round, 60% of what people said in the open format had no closed box. Generate options from open answers in a pilot. (Clark et al. 2021)
  • Comparing results across modes as if they were the same. Interview vs self-completion changes how much people disclose, which matters most for sensitive items. A change of mode can look like a change in the world. (Clark et al. 2021)
  • Keying every item in the same direction. Acquiescence then inflates the scale and cannot be detected. Balance the scale, reverse-code before summing, and check for agreement with opposite statements. See also the Likert trap. (Clark et al. 2021)
  • Taking stated behaviour as actual behaviour. LaPiere’s hotels and restaurants said one thing and did another. When the question is about behaviour, consider diaries, observation, or records. (Clark et al. 2021)
  • Ignoring the “problem of meaning”. Surveys assume interviewer and respondent share the meaning of every term. Interpretivist critics argue that meaning is “worked at and achieved”, not given in advance, and Bryman notes the issue “tends to be ignored in structured interview research”. Cognitive interviews and qualitative evidence are how you check it. (Clark et al. 2021)

Appendix · the mathematics

Why interviewer variance does not shrink with the sample. Suppose m interviewers each complete b interviews, and each interviewer adds a shared shift u_j \sim (0, \sigma_u^2) to their respondents’ answers on top of individual variation \sigma_e^2. The within-interviewer correlation is \rho = \sigma_u^2/(\sigma_u^2 + \sigma_e^2), and the variance of the overall mean is

\mathrm{Var}(\bar y) = \frac{\sigma_u^2+\sigma_e^2}{mb}\,\big[1 + (b-1)\rho\big].

This is the cluster-sampling design effect 1+(m-1)\rho from complex survey estimation (Bui 2026), with interviewers playing the role of clusters. Even a small \rho is multiplied by the workload b. With \rho = 0.02, the edge of Collins’s “less than 2 per cent” range, and 50 interviews per interviewer, the variance nearly doubles (1 + 49 \times 0.02 = 1.98). Adding interviews per interviewer does not help. Only more interviewers, or better standardisation, reduces it.

Why acquiescence biases a same-keyed scale but not a balanced one. Model each raw answer as x_{ij} = \mu + \lambda_j \theta_i + a_i + e_{ij}, where \theta_i is the trait, \lambda_j = \pm 1 is the item’s direction, and a_i is respondent i’s agreement tendency. Reverse-coding multiplies an item by \lambda_j (up to a constant), so the scored mean is

\bar s_i = \text{const} + \theta_i + a_i\,\frac{1}{k}\sum_j \lambda_j + \bar e_i .

With all items keyed the same way, \frac1k\sum\lambda_j = 1 and a_i enters the score in full. With a balanced scale, \sum_j\lambda_j = 0 and a_i cancels. It then shows up in the sum of positive and reversed raw answers, the anti-diagonal in the figure, which is how it is detected.

subtitle: "A survey answer is not a fact you collect. It is produced by a question, a format, an order, a mode, and sometimes a person asking. Design all five."
---

::: {.lead-kernel}
A careful sample can still produce wrong numbers if the **questions** are badly built. Wording,
answer format, question order, who asks, and whether anyone asks at all (interview vs
self-completion) all shape what people say. Most of these effects are systematic, so a larger sample
does not remove them. **Questionnaire design is measurement design.**
:::

## Why it matters

Bryman lists **three sources of error** in social surveys: **sampling error**, **data-collection
error** (poor question wording, poor interviewing, flawed instruments), and **data-processing error**
(mistakes in coding and entering answers) [@clark_bryman_2021]. The [sampling entry](../02-inference/sampling-and-survey-design.qmd)
covers the first. This entry covers the other two. Bryman notes they relate "much more closely to
concerns about the validity of measurement" [@clark_bryman_2021].

A few findings show the size of the problem:

- **Order.** In 1988 the Crime Survey for England and Wales accidentally asked "would you say the police
  in this area do a good job or a poor job?" **twice** for about half of respondents: once early on, and
  again after questions about contact with the police. Of those asked twice, 66% gave the same rating,
  **22% rated the police more positively the second time, and only 13% less positively** (Mayhew 2000)
  [@clark_bryman_2021]. The respondents were the same people. Only the question's position changed.
- **Mode.** Among people *known* to have a criminal conviction, 67% admitted it in a postal
  questionnaire but only 58% in a face-to-face interview (Preisendörfer & Wolter 2014)
  [@clark_bryman_2021].
- **Format.** In Schuman and Presser's study of what people want from a job, about 31% chose "a feeling
  of accomplishment" from a closed list, but only about 14.5% said something similar when the question
  was open [@clark_bryman_2021].
- **Say vs do.** In the 1930s LaPiere travelled across the United States with a Chinese couple. Only 1 of
  66 hotels and none of 184 restaurants turned them away. When the same establishments were later sent a
  questionnaire asking whether they would accept Chinese guests, **92% of restaurants and 91% of hotels
  that replied said "No"** [@clark_bryman_2021].

None of these errors gets smaller with a larger $n$. They are **bias**, not noise.

## Two ways to administer a survey

A survey is delivered either by an interviewer reading from an **interview schedule** (a **structured**
or standardised interview, face-to-face, by video, or by phone as CAPI/CATI) or by a
**self-completion questionnaire** (postal, email, online) [@clark_bryman_2021]. The structured
interview's central idea is **standardisation**. Each respondent should receive "exactly the same
interview stimulus": the same words, in the same order, with closed answer options where possible, so
that differences in answers reflect differences between *people* and not differences in *how they
were asked* [@clark_bryman_2021].

Standardisation fails in two ways. **Intra-interviewer variability** is one interviewer being
inconsistent. **Inter-interviewer variability** is interviewers being inconsistent with each other
[@clark_bryman_2021]. Every departure adds variation that comes from error, not from the people being
measured, and "error reduces the validity of a measure" [@clark_bryman_2021]. The effect can be
measured. English experiments found that for about two-thirds of the questions studied, interviewers
contributed less than 2% of the total variation (Collins 1997). That leaves a third of questions where
they contributed more [@clark_bryman_2021].

So interviewers are trained in controlled moves:

- **Probing** asks for more when an answer is incomplete, with neutral probes such as "Could you say a
  little more about that?" Bryman calls probing "a highly problematic area" and says it should be kept
  to a minimum [@clark_bryman_2021].
- **Prompting** suggests possible answers. Every closed question is a *standardised* prompt. Offering
  options only to respondents who seem to be struggling is not acceptable [@clark_bryman_2021].
- **Show cards** present long lists, Likert batches, or sensitive bands (age, income) so the respondent
  can point instead of saying a number out loud [@clark_bryman_2021].

**Which mode?** Neither mode wins on every criterion. Bryman's comparison table rates in-person
interviews best for response rates and self-completion modes best for **minimising social
desirability**, with telephone in between [@clark_bryman_2021]. The research is consistent that people
disclose more without an interviewer present. They report more drug use, alcohol consumption, sexual
partners and abortions on self-completion forms than in interviews (Tourangeau & Smith 1996), and
online more than on the phone (Tourangeau et al. 2013) [@clark_bryman_2021]. Self-completion has its
own costs. Nobody can probe or clarify. The respondent can read the whole form, so question-order
effects cannot be controlled. And the wrong person may fill it in: in one U.S. household survey 18% of
respondents were not the intended person (Olson & Smyth 2014) [@clark_bryman_2021].

## Open or closed?

**Open questions** let respondents answer in their own terms, do not suggest answers, and are good for
exploring new territory. But they must be **post-coded**, which is slow and unreliable. Bryman classes
post-coding variability as data-processing error [@clark_bryman_2021]. **Closed questions** are quick,
comparable, and easy to process. But they can omit what matters, force overlapping or non-exhaustive
choices, and put words in people's mouths [@clark_bryman_2021].

Schuman and Presser's work-values experiment shows the trade-off [@clark_bryman_2021]. In a first
round, **60% of the categories people produced in the open format were not covered by the closed
options**. They rebuilt the closed list from the open answers and ran both again:

| What people most want in a job | Closed format | Open format |
|---|---|---|
| Pay | 13.2% | 16.7% |
| Feeling of accomplishment | 31.0% | 14.5% |
| Control of work | 11.7% | 4.6% |
| Pleasant work | 19.8% | 14.5% |
| Security | 20.3% | 7.6% |
| **Share covered by these five** | **96%** | **57.9%** |

Even after the redesign, only 58% of open answers fitted the closed categories, and "accomplishment"
was chosen about twice as often when it was offered [@clark_bryman_2021]. A closed list is not a neutral
container. It tells respondents which answers are expected. The practical lesson Bryman draws is to
**build the closed options from open answers**, usually in a pilot [@clark_bryman_2021].

When categories are coded, Bryman gives three rules: categories must **not overlap**, must be
**exhaustive** (hence "other"), and must have **clear assignment rules** written in a coding frame
[@clark_bryman_2021].

## Response sets: consistent, lawful, and irrelevant

Some bias comes from how people answer a *series* of questions, not from any single question. Webb et
al. call these **response sets**, "irrelevant but lawful sources of variance". They are consistent
patterns of answering that have nothing to do with the concept being measured
[@clark_bryman_2021]. The two main ones:

- **Acquiescence** ("yea-saying" or "nay-saying") is a tendency to agree, or disagree, with everything.
  It is a form of **satisficing**, where respondents "settle for merely satisfactory" answers instead of
  accurate ones (Krosnick 1999). Other forms include always taking the middle option or the last one
  listed [@clark_bryman_2021].
- **Social desirability bias** means answers shift towards what looks good. Charity is over-reported
  and heavy drinking under-reported, and sensitive questions also cause **item non-response**
  [@clark_bryman_2021]. Remedies include removing the interviewer (self-completion) and "forgiving"
  wording ("Everybody does it"). Minson et al. (2018) found that questions which **presuppose a
  problem** drew more disclosure of undesirable work behaviour than questions that presupposed there
  was none, or general questions [@clark_bryman_2021].

The standard defence against acquiescence is a **balanced scale**. Some items are worded so that
agreeing signals *more* of the trait, others so that agreeing signals *less*. The reversed items are
then reverse-coded before summing. Bryman's job-satisfaction example (from Brayfield & Rothe's 18-item
scale) includes "It seems that my friends are more interested in their jobs", where *disagreeing*
indicates satisfaction. Someone who agrees with all 18 items is showing a response set, and "their
responses would not provide a valid assessment" [@clark_bryman_2021]. The figure below simulates what
this buys you.

```{python}
#| label: fig-acquiescence
#| fig-cap: "A simulation of acquiescence on a six-item Likert scale: 1,000 respondents, 15% of whom answer 'agree' or 'strongly agree' to every item whatever it says (orange). Left: all items worded in the same direction. The yea-sayers blend in as apparently very satisfied people and push the average up. Right: three items reverse-worded. Consistent respondents fall near the diagonal, since agreeing with one set means disagreeing with the other. The yea-sayers sit in the top-right corner, agreeing with opposite statements, where they can be identified. Illustrative data, not from a real survey."
#| code-fold: true
#| code-summary: "Show the simulation code"

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(11)
n, k = 1000, 6
trait = rng.normal(0, 1, n)               # latent job satisfaction
yea = rng.random(n) < 0.15                # respondents who agree with everything

def administer(keys):
    """keys: +1 = agreeing signals satisfaction, -1 = reverse-worded item."""
    latent = trait[:, None] * keys[None, :] + rng.normal(0, 0.8, (n, k))
    raw = np.clip(np.round(3 + 1.1 * latent), 1, 5)
    raw[yea] = rng.choice([4, 5], size=(yea.sum(), k))
    scored = np.where(keys > 0, raw, 6 - raw)       # reverse-code before scoring
    return raw, scored

raw_same, score_same = administer(np.ones(k))
raw_bal, _ = administer(np.array([1, 1, 1, -1, -1, -1]))

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(8.8, 3.9))

ax = axes[0]
bins = np.linspace(1, 5, 17)
ax.hist([score_same.mean(1)[~yea], score_same.mean(1)[yea]], bins=bins, stacked=True,
        color=["#b7b6a8", "#e8531d"], edgecolor="#f4f3ec", linewidth=0.6,
        label=["other respondents", "yea-sayers (15%)"])
ax.set_title("All six items worded the same way", fontsize=10)
ax.set_xlabel("scale score (1–5)")
ax.set_ylabel("respondents")
ax.legend(fontsize=8.5, frameon=False, loc="upper left")

ax = axes[1]
x = raw_bal[:, :3].mean(1) + rng.uniform(-0.06, 0.06, n)
y = raw_bal[:, 3:].mean(1) + rng.uniform(-0.06, 0.06, n)
ax.scatter(x[~yea], y[~yea], s=7, color="#b7b6a8", lw=0)
ax.scatter(x[yea], y[yea], s=9, color="#e8531d", lw=0)
ax.plot([1, 5], [5, 1], color="#161613", lw=1, ls="--")
ax.text(1.1, 1.3, "consistent answers\nlie near this line", fontsize=8, color="#161613")
ax.text(3.75, 4.45, "agrees with\nopposite\nstatements", fontsize=8, color="#e8531d", ha="right")
ax.set_xlim(0.8, 5.2)
ax.set_ylim(0.8, 5.2)
ax.set_title("Three items reverse-worded", fontsize=10)
ax.set_xlabel("mean raw answer, positively worded items")
ax.set_ylabel("mean raw answer, reverse-worded items")

fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

Bryman notes a cost. Respondents are more likely to *misread* a question when its direction changes,
so reversed items need careful wording [@clark_bryman_2021].

## Rules for writing a question

Bryman's general test is to answer the question yourself. "Do you have a car?" is ambiguous: *own*
one, or have *access* to one? "This week" is vague about which days count [@clark_bryman_2021]. Hard
questions are costly as well as unclear, because they produce more non-substantive and middle answers
(Lenzner 2012) [@clark_bryman_2021]. The specific rules:

| Avoid | Example | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| **Ambiguous terms** | "often", "regularly", "family", "dinner" | give concrete frequencies and definitions |
| **Double-barrelled questions** | satisfaction with "pay and conditions" | one question per idea |
| **Leading or loaded wording** | "Do you agree with the view that…?" | neutral, balanced phrasing |
| **Negatives and double negatives** | "Would you rather not use non-perishable foods?" | state it positively |
| **Technical terms and acronyms** | "alienated", "TUC" | plain words |
| **Stretching memory** | cinema visits in the last 12 months | a shorter window (last month) |
| **Presupposing behaviour** | "Which party did you vote for?" | a **filter question** first |
| **Overlapping or incomplete options** | age bands 30–40 / 40–50 | mutually exclusive, exhaustive bands |
| **Unbalanced scales** | Excellent / Good / Acceptable / Poor | as many negative as positive points |
| **"Tick all that apply"** | a checklist | a yes/no for each item, which yields more selections (Smyth et al. 2006) |

[@clark_bryman_2021]

On the **middle option** and **"don't know"**, Bryman's reading of the evidence is to include both.
Leaving out the midpoint increases item non-response. Midpoint answers mostly reflect genuinely having
no opinion. And "don't know" answers reflect less knowledge than even incorrect answers do (Jessee
2017) [@clark_bryman_2021].

**Question order.** The evidence is inconsistent, but there are well-documented cases. Americans were
less likely to call their taxes too high after being asked whether government spending should rise
(Schuman & Presser) [@clark_bryman_2021]. The working rules: keep the order fixed across respondents,
open with questions relevant to the topic (not demographics), put sensitive questions later, group
related questions, go from general to specific, and ask attitudes before behaviour and knowledge
[@clark_bryman_2021].

## Test it before you trust it

Bryman treats **piloting** as essential, especially for self-completion questionnaires, where no
interviewer is present to repair confusion. A pilot shows which questions everyone answers the same
way (which tell you nothing), which make people uncomfortable, and which are skipped or misunderstood.
It can also turn open answers into closed options. Pilot on a group *comparable* to the sample, not on
people who will be in it [@clark_bryman_2021]. **Cognitive interviewing** goes further. Respondents
"think out loud" while answering, which shows how they interpret terms such as "working for pay" or
"ill-health" [@clark_bryman_2021]. Where possible, **reuse existing questions**. They have in effect
already been piloted, and they allow comparison with earlier studies [@clark_bryman_2021].

## Common pitfalls

::: {.callout-warning title="Where this goes wrong"}
- **Treating a survey percentage as a fact about the world.** It is a fact about the answers to *that
  question, in that position, in that mode*. The police-rating result shifted just because the question
  was asked twice. Report exact wording and mode alongside the number. [@clark_bryman_2021]
- **Assuming a bigger sample fixes it.** Wording, order and mode effects are bias. They do not average
  out. See [the two kinds of error](../02-inference/sampling-and-survey-design.qmd).
- **Writing closed options from the armchair.** In Schuman and Presser's first round, 60% of what people
  said in the open format had no closed box. Generate options from open answers in a pilot.
  [@clark_bryman_2021]
- **Comparing results across modes as if they were the same.** Interview vs self-completion changes how
  much people disclose, which matters most for sensitive items. A change of mode can look like a change
  in the world. [@clark_bryman_2021]
- **Keying every item in the same direction.** Acquiescence then inflates the scale and cannot be
  detected. Balance the scale, reverse-code before summing, and check for agreement with opposite
  statements. See also the [Likert trap](concepts-and-measurement.qmd). [@clark_bryman_2021]
- **Taking stated behaviour as actual behaviour.** LaPiere's hotels and restaurants said one thing and
  did another. When the question is about behaviour, consider diaries, observation, or records.
  [@clark_bryman_2021]
- **Ignoring the "problem of meaning".** Surveys assume interviewer and respondent share the meaning of
  every term. Interpretivist critics argue that meaning is "worked at and achieved", not given in
  advance, and Bryman notes the issue "tends to be ignored in structured interview research". Cognitive
  interviews and [qualitative evidence](qualitative-evidence.qmd) are how you check it.
  [@clark_bryman_2021]
:::

## Appendix · the mathematics {.unnumbered}

::: {.math-appendix}

**Why interviewer variance does not shrink with the sample.** Suppose $m$ interviewers each complete
$b$ interviews, and each interviewer adds a shared shift $u_j \sim (0, \sigma_u^2)$ to their
respondents' answers on top of individual variation $\sigma_e^2$. The within-interviewer correlation is
$\rho = \sigma_u^2/(\sigma_u^2 + \sigma_e^2)$, and the variance of the overall mean is

$$
\mathrm{Var}(\bar y) = \frac{\sigma_u^2+\sigma_e^2}{mb}\,\big[1 + (b-1)\rho\big].
$$

This is the cluster-sampling **design effect** $1+(m-1)\rho$ from
[complex survey estimation](../02-inference/complex-survey-estimation.qmd) [@bui_survey_sampling], with
interviewers playing the role of clusters. Even a small $\rho$ is multiplied by the workload $b$.
With $\rho = 0.02$, the edge of Collins's "less than 2 per cent" range, and 50 interviews per
interviewer, the variance nearly doubles ($1 + 49 \times 0.02 = 1.98$). Adding interviews *per
interviewer* does not help. Only more interviewers, or better standardisation, reduces it.

**Why acquiescence biases a same-keyed scale but not a balanced one.** Model each raw answer as
$x_{ij} = \mu + \lambda_j \theta_i + a_i + e_{ij}$, where $\theta_i$ is the trait, $\lambda_j = \pm 1$ is
the item's direction, and $a_i$ is respondent $i$'s agreement tendency. Reverse-coding multiplies an
item by $\lambda_j$ (up to a constant), so the scored mean is

$$
\bar s_i = \text{const} + \theta_i + a_i\,\frac{1}{k}\sum_j \lambda_j + \bar e_i .
$$

With all items keyed the same way, $\frac1k\sum\lambda_j = 1$ and $a_i$ enters the score in full. With a
balanced scale, $\sum_j\lambda_j = 0$ and $a_i$ cancels. It then shows up in the *sum* of positive and
reversed raw answers, the anti-diagonal in the figure, which is how it is detected.

:::
