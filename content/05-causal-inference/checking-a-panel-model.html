Checking a Panel Model

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source

Fitting fixed effects is where the work starts. A panel has two error terms, two directions in which it can be unstable, and its own ways of overstating how sure you should be.

A panel regression can fail diagnostics in more ways than a cross-section can. There are two error terms to inspect, not one. Errors can be correlated within a unit over time, which quietly shrinks your standard errors. And the relationship can drift across units as well as across years. Checking a panel model means asking each of those questions in turn and letting the answers change the model.

Why it matters

Fixed effects is often presented as a finished design: demean, regress, report. Ashley’s view is that an estimated panel model is a first draft, like any other regression. You check it, and what you find often changes what you conclude. (Ashley 2012) His running example shows how much can change. It is a growth regression on the Penn World Table: 187 countries, 8,180 country-years, real per-capita GDP growth on trade openness and the consumption, investment and government shares of output. Openness looks like it matters. After checking for nonlinearity and for differences between groups of countries, the evidence for openness is weak. What first looked like a nonlinear openness effect turns out to be an artefact of lumping together countries that entered the data set at different times. (Ashley 2012)

The checks themselves are the familiar ones from regression diagnostics and the craft of model building: normality and outliers, heteroskedasticity, serial correlation, nonlinearity, parameter stability. Panels add three twists. (Ashley 2012) There are two error components to check. Stability has to hold across units as well as across time. And the usual fix for serial correlation, adding lags of the outcome, breaks the fixed-effects estimator.

First, which panel model?

Ashley sets out five standard ways to fit a panel, and a diagnostic pass often sends you from one to another. (Ashley 2012)

Model What it uses Its bargain
Pooled OLS every row as if independent consistent only under the random-effects assumption, and then RE beats it anyway
Fixed effects (within) each unit’s deviations from its own mean robust to unit effects correlated with X; cannot estimate time-invariant regressors
Between effects one row per unit: the time-averages can estimate time-invariant regressors; discards all time variation
Random effects a weighted blend of within and between efficient and keeps time-invariant regressors, if the unit effect is uncorrelated with X
First differences period-to-period changes needed when regressors are not strictly exogenous (lags, feedback)

The between-effects model is the one most often left out of textbooks, and it is useful for diagnosis. It regresses each unit’s average outcome on its average regressors, so it shows what the data say across units once time is averaged away. In the Penn World Table it disagrees sharply with fixed effects. The within estimate of the openness coefficient is −0.031 (SE 0.017, p = 0.066). The between estimate is +0.206 (SE 0.061, p = 0.001). The between standard errors are two to four times larger, because averaging shrinks the effective sample from 8,180 to 187. (Ashley 2012) Ashley’s reading is that a big within/between gap is a symptom, not a contradiction. Fixed effects controls for year shocks; the between model averages the coefficient across 57 years in which it may not have been stable. This is the same disagreement the Hausman test and Mundlak’s device formalise.

Choosing, with tests you can trust. Ashley’s model choice is also a lesson in reading standard errors. Fit the growth panel as one pooled regression and openness looks significant with ordinary standard errors (p = 0.001). White heteroskedasticity-robust errors are about 2.4–2.7 times larger and put it at p = 0.21. Clustering by country makes them another 30–40% larger again. (Ashley 2012) Fixed and random effects then give almost identical coefficients (openness −0.031 against −0.029). The textbook Hausman test nevertheless rejects loudly (χ²(20) = 60.81, p < 0.0001), along with a warning that its variance matrix is not positive definite. Ashley calls the rejection meaningless. The standard test assumes homoskedastic errors, which these are not, and it often breaks down numerically even in large samples. (Ashley 2012) His generalised Hausman test estimates both models jointly with robust errors (Stata’s suest) and finds no significant difference, for any coefficient or for all four together (joint p = 0.41). A Lagrange-multiplier test (xttest0) then rejects the pooled model in favour of random effects (χ²(1) = 193.34). (Ashley 2012) So random effects is the model to check. Ashley’s point is that passing these tests makes it a candidate. It is not yet a model whose inferences you can trust.

Two error terms, two things to look at

A panel error splits into a unit component \nu_i (one value per country, fixed over time) and an idiosyncratic component \varepsilon_{it} (fresh each period). Software gives you estimates of both, and Ashley’s advice is to plot both, because they fail in different ways. (Ashley 2012)

In the growth panel, the estimated \varepsilon_{it} have thick tails. There are too many very small and very large values compared with a normal distribution, a shape called leptokurtic. Ashley does not treat the large values as outliers to delete. They show that the noise simply is not normal, which matters little in a sample this large. (Ashley 2012) The estimated \nu_i tell a more interesting story: their histogram has several peaks. Following that lead, Ashley plots the number of years each country is observed and finds four clusters. The Penn World Table grew in four “waves”, with countries joining in the early 1950s, around 1960, in 1970, and from 1987 to 1994. (Ashley 2012) A messy residual plot has become a hypothesis: maybe the relationship differs by wave. Diagnostic checking is meant to work like this, with each oddity pointing to a specific test. The wave hypothesis is tested below.

Heteroskedasticity: in the unit effect or in the noise?

In a panel, unequal variances can enter at two levels. Dong Li separates two textbook cases. (Li 2009)

  • Case 1: the unit effects have unequal variances: some countries’ permanent level is far less predictable than others’.
  • Case 2: the idiosyncratic noise has unequal variances: some countries are simply more volatile year to year.

Each case has a feasible-GLS recipe. Estimate a variance per unit from within and OLS residuals, build a unit-specific weight \hat\theta_i, quasi-demean, and re-run. (Li 2009) Dong Li then makes the practical point: in practice we do not like these FGLS estimators. They need many periods per unit to estimate each unit’s variance, which most labour panels do not have. Researchers prefer standard errors that are robust to heteroskedasticity of unknown form. (Li 2009)

Ashley adds that robust standard errors are no reason to skip the test. Regress the squared idiosyncratic residuals on plausible sources of heteroskedasticity. In the growth panel, the error variance moved mainly with the year dummies and the entry-wave dummies. That is a second clue pointing at the waves, and a test you should run because it is cheap. (Ashley 2012)

Serial correlation: three responses, and what each buys

Errors for the same country in neighbouring years are rarely independent. Note too that the within transformation creates some correlation by itself. Demeaning a unit’s errors with their own average makes the transformed errors correlated at -1/(T_i-1) even when the originals are independent. This is why ordinary and White heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors are both inconsistent for fixed effects. (Ashley 2012)

The check is easy. Regress the estimated \varepsilon_{it} on their own lags. In Ashley’s growth model, only the first lag is significant: 0.092 (t = 2.66), mild positive autocorrelation. It is mild because the outcome is a growth rate, a variable “in changes”. A model for the level of GDP would have strongly autocorrelated errors. (Ashley 2012) Once you know the errors are correlated, there are three responses.

1. Cluster-robust standard errors (the default). Treat each unit as a cluster. This allows any pattern of heteroskedasticity and any correlation among a unit’s errors across its own periods, not just AR(1). The correlation can also take a different form in every unit. (Ashley 2012) The one thing ruled out is correlation between different units. Clustering changes only the standard errors. It leaves the coefficients untouched, so whatever efficiency the serial correlation costs is still lost. (Ashley 2012) Ashley also notes simulation evidence (Stock & Watson) that under the within transformation, cluster-robust errors beat White errors even when T is large. (Ashley 2012)

2. Model the dynamics. Serial correlation often means the model is missing dynamics, and adding lagged variables can remove it and give a better model. Panels make this awkward. A lagged outcome inside a fixed-effects model is no longer strictly exogenous after demeaning, so the estimator becomes inconsistent. This is the Nickell bias, and the fix is first differences plus instruments. (Ashley 2012) Ashley’s rule of thumb: unless you need the dynamics, or have endogenous regressors, fixed effects with cluster-robust errors usually beats first differences, which give much less precise estimates. (Ashley 2012) The exception is strong serial correlation. Ashley cites simulations by Reed and Ye in which no fixed-effects standard-error estimator was reliably accurate once the error autocorrelation was well above about 0.3. (Ashley 2012) Random effects can tolerate a lagged outcome, but only if the idiosyncratic errors are homoskedastic and serially uncorrelated. (Ashley 2012)

3. Feasible GLS for AR(1) errors. If the errors look like \varepsilon_{it}=\rho\,\varepsilon_{i,t-1}+\eta_{it}, estimate \rho from the within residuals and quasi-difference each unit’s series. This is the panel version of Prais–Winsten, or of Cochrane–Orcutt if the first period is dropped. The Baltagi–Li estimator, xtregar in Stata, implements it. (Li 2009; Ashley 2012) This response actually recovers the lost efficiency, but it assumes AR(1) and, in that implementation, gives no cluster-robust errors. When heteroskedasticity is also present, or the correlation is not AR(1), GMM is the more general route. (Ashley 2012)

Show the figure code (within estimator, cluster-robust SEs and quasi-differencing, by hand) 
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(15)
N, T, R = 50, 20, 600
rhos = np.array([0.0, 0.15, 0.3, 0.45, 0.6, 0.75, 0.9])

def ar1(rho, shape):
    """Unit-variance AR(1) series, one per row, started from the stationary distribution."""
    e = rng.normal(size=shape)
    out = np.empty(shape)
    out[:, 0] = e[:, 0] / np.sqrt(1 - rho**2)
    for t in range(1, shape[1]):
        out[:, t] = rho * out[:, t - 1] + e[:, t]
    return out * np.sqrt(1 - rho**2)

def within(a):
    return a - a.mean(axis=1, keepdims=True)

rej_naive, rej_clust, sd_fe, sd_qd = [], [], [], []
for rho in rhos:
    rn = rc = 0
    b_fe, b_qd = [], []
    for _ in range(R):
        alpha = rng.normal(size=(N, 1))              # unit effects, correlated with x
        x = ar1(0.8, (N, T)) + 0.5 * alpha
        y = alpha + ar1(rho, (N, T))                 # true slope = 0
        xd, yd = within(x), within(y)
        sxx = (xd**2).sum()
        b = (xd * yd).sum() / sxx
        e = yd - b * xd
        se_naive = np.sqrt((e**2).sum() / (N * T - N - 1) / sxx)
        score = (xd * e).sum(axis=1)                 # one score per cluster (unit)
        se_clust = np.sqrt(N / (N - 1) * (score**2).sum()) / sxx
        rn += abs(b / se_naive) > 1.96
        rc += abs(b / se_clust) > 1.96
        b_fe.append(b)
        # quasi-difference with rho estimated from the within residuals (+1/T small-T correction)
        rh = (e[:, 1:] * e[:, :-1]).sum() / (e[:, :-1]**2).sum() + 1.0 / T
        xs, ys = within(x[:, 1:] - rh * x[:, :-1]), within(y[:, 1:] - rh * y[:, :-1])
        b_qd.append((xs * ys).sum() / (xs**2).sum())
    rej_naive.append(rn / R); rej_clust.append(rc / R)
    sd_fe.append(np.std(b_fe)); sd_qd.append(np.std(b_qd))

fig, (ax1, ax2) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(9.2, 3.9))
ax1.axhline(0.05, color="#b7b6a8", lw=1, ls=":")
ax1.plot(rhos, rej_naive, "o-", color="#e8531d", lw=2, label="ordinary FE SEs")
ax1.plot(rhos, rej_clust, "s-", color="#161613", lw=1.6, label="cluster-robust SEs")
ax1.set_xlabel("serial correlation of the errors, ρ")
ax1.set_ylabel("false-rejection rate (nominal 5%)")
ax1.set_title("Precision is overstated")
ax1.legend(frameon=False, fontsize=8, loc="upper left")

ax2.plot(rhos, sd_fe, "s-", color="#161613", lw=1.6, label="fixed effects (either SE)")
ax2.plot(rhos, sd_qd, "^-", color="#5e5e54", lw=1.6, label="quasi-differenced FE (FGLS)")
ax2.set_xlabel("serial correlation of the errors, ρ")
ax2.set_ylabel("sampling SD of the slope")
ax2.set_title("…and clustering doesn't recover efficiency")
ax2.set_ylim(0, None)
ax2.legend(frameon=False, fontsize=8, loc="lower left")
fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()

The left panel is why clustering became standard: the coefficient is fine, but the ordinary standard errors make it look far more precise than it is. The right panel is Ashley’s caveat: clustering corrects the reported precision and adds no actual precision. When the correlation is strong, modelling it is worth the trouble. (Ashley 2012)

Did you difference too much? Modelling “in changes” carries the opposite risk. If the level series was stationary all along, differencing it creates errors with negative autocorrelation at lag one. For an AR(1) level with persistence \phi, the lag-one autocorrelation of the change is -(1-\phi)/2. Ashley therefore reads the sign of the residual lag-one coefficient as an informal test. A significantly negative value suggests over-differencing. The growth model’s +0.092 makes over-differencing unlikely. (Ashley 2012) He prefers this to formal panel unit-root tests, which he regards as too low-powered to be very informative. (Ashley 2012)

When units are correlated with each other. Cluster-robust errors assume units are independent of each other. Countries hit by the same oil shock, or states sharing a labour market, break that. Year dummies absorb shocks common to everyone, but not shocks shared by a region. Dong Li’s software survey shows the wider menu. Errors can be modelled as groupwise heteroskedastic, as correlated across groups within a period, and as AR(1) within groups with a common or a unit-specific \rho. Stata’s xtgls covers the nine combinations, xtpcse gives panel-corrected standard errors, and generalised estimating equations (xtgee) choose a within-unit correlation structure (exchangeable, AR, unstructured) directly. (Li 2009)

Nonlinearity and stability: where the conclusion changed

The last two checks are the ones that changed Ashley’s substantive conclusion. (Ashley 2012)

Nonlinearity. In the linear random-effects model, the openness coefficient is not significant at 5% (p = 0.078). Adding squared terms changes that. Openness and its square are jointly significant at the 0.1% level (χ²(2) = 13.82, p = 0.001). Apparently a curved relationship was hiding behind a flat linear fit. (Ashley 2012)

Parameter stability across units. The earlier clues, the multi-peaked \nu_i and the heteroskedasticity that moved with entry wave, point to one more test. Does the slope differ by the wave in which a country joined the data? Interact each regressor with the wave dummies. Once the other shares may differ by wave, the squared openness terms are no longer significant. Testing every openness term together gives χ²(4) = 7.81, p = 0.099: not significant at 5%. (Ashley 2012) The investment and government-share effects, by contrast, really do differ across waves.

So the “nonlinear openness effect” was most likely produced by pooling heterogeneous groups of countries. Ashley’s closing judgement is honest. The final model may be an adequate statistical description of this data set. But its intercept moves around over time and its key slopes depend on when a country entered the sample, which shows it is describing rather than explaining. Its most useful output is a new question: why do the waves differ? (Ashley 2012) He also notes what the exercise never addressed: the shares are jointly determined with growth, so the regressors are probably endogenous. Instruments or lagged regressors are needed before any of it is read causally. (Ashley 2012)

Common pitfalls

WarningWhere this goes wrong

  • Reporting ordinary or White SEs for a fixed-effects model. The within transformation itself correlates each unit’s errors (-1/(T_i-1)), and real panels add serial correlation on top. Cluster by unit as the default. (Ashley 2012)

  • Thinking clustering fixes serial correlation. It fixes the standard errors. The coefficients, and their inefficiency, are unchanged. Persistent errors are often a sign of missing dynamics. (Ashley 2012)

  • Adding a lagged dependent variable to a fixed-effects model. After demeaning it is correlated with the error, so the estimates are inconsistent. Use first differences with instruments (dynamic panels). (Ashley 2012)

  • Clustering by unit when units share shocks. Clustering assumes no correlation between clusters. (Ashley 2012) Regional shocks that time dummies do not absorb call for an error structure that allows cross-unit correlation (Li 2009), or a coarser cluster, which is the difference-in-differences advice to cluster one level up. (Angrist and Pischke 2009)

  • Trusting a textbook Hausman test on heteroskedastic data. It assumes homoskedastic errors and can fail numerically. In the growth panel it “rejected” at χ²(20) = 60.81 while the coefficients were nearly identical. Use a robust (generalised) version. (Ashley 2012)

  • Checking only the idiosyncratic residuals. The unit-level residuals \hat\nu_i are where heterogeneity across units shows itself. In the Penn World Table, their several peaks were the first sign of the entry waves. (Ashley 2012)

  • Ignoring an unbalanced panel. When units enter at different times, the early sample may be a different population from the late one. Test whether slopes differ by entry cohort before trusting a pooled slope. (Ashley 2012)

  • Reaching for unit-specific FGLS on a short panel. Estimating a variance or \rho for every unit needs many periods per unit. With short T, robust standard errors are the safer choice. (Li 2009)

Appendix · the mathematics

Demeaning creates negative correlation. Let \varepsilon_{i1},\dots,\varepsilon_{iT} be independent with variance \sigma^2, and \ddot\varepsilon_{it}=\varepsilon_{it}-\bar\varepsilon_i. Then \operatorname{Var}(\ddot\varepsilon_{it})=\sigma^2(1-1/T) and, for t\neq s, \operatorname{Cov}(\ddot\varepsilon_{it},\ddot\varepsilon_{is})=-\sigma^2/T, so \operatorname{Corr}(\ddot\varepsilon_{it},\ddot\varepsilon_{is})=\frac{-1/T}{1-1/T}=-\frac{1}{T-1}. It vanishes as T grows, but the standard-error problem does not (see Stock & Watson, via Ashley).

The cluster-robust variance. For the within estimator \hat\beta=(\sum_i\ddot X_i'\ddot X_i)^{-1}\sum_i\ddot X_i'\ddot y_i, with \hat e_i the vector of unit i’s residuals, \widehat{\operatorname{Var}}_{\text{cluster}}(\hat\beta)=\Big(\sum_i\ddot X_i'\ddot X_i\Big)^{-1} \Big(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\ddot X_i'\,\hat e_i\hat e_i'\,\ddot X_i\Big) \Big(\sum_i\ddot X_i'\ddot X_i\Big)^{-1}. Expanding the middle term for one regressor gives \sum_i\sum_{t}\sum_{s}\ddot x_{it}\ddot x_{is}\hat e_{it}\hat e_{is}. The t=s terms are White’s heteroskedasticity-robust estimator, and the t\neq s terms allow arbitrary within-unit correlation. Cross-unit products (i\neq j) are absent, which is the assumption that units are independent. The figure multiplies by N/(N-1) as a small-sample correction.

The over-differencing signature. Let X_t=\phi X_{t-1}+U_t be stationary (|\phi|<1) with autocovariances \gamma_k=\phi^k\gamma_0, and let Z_t=X_t-X_{t-1}. Then \operatorname{Var}(Z_t)=2\gamma_0(1-\phi) and \operatorname{Cov}(Z_t,Z_{t-1})=2\gamma_1-\gamma_0-\gamma_2=-\gamma_0(1-\phi)^2, so \operatorname{Corr}(Z_t,Z_{t-1})=-\frac{1-\phi}{2}\in\left(-\tfrac12,\,0\right). A differenced stationary series therefore always has negative lag-one autocorrelation (−½ for white noise). A random walk (\phi=1) differences to white noise with zero autocorrelation. Residual lag-one autocorrelation that is significantly positive, as in Ashley’s growth model, points away from over-differencing.

Quasi-differencing under AR(1) errors. If \varepsilon_{it}=\rho\varepsilon_{i,t-1}+\eta_{it}, then y_{it}-\rho y_{i,t-1}=(x_{it}-\rho x_{i,t-1})\beta+(1-\rho)\alpha_i+\eta_{it},\qquad t\ge2. The unit effect becomes (1-\rho)\alpha_i, still constant within a unit, so demeaning the quasi-differenced data removes it and leaves serially uncorrelated \eta_{it}. Prais–Winsten keeps t=1 by scaling it by \sqrt{1-\rho^2}; Cochrane–Orcutt drops it. The figure estimates \rho from the within residuals and adds 1/T as a rough correction for the downward bias that demeaning induces in it (see the first result above).

::: {.lead-kernel}
A panel regression can fail diagnostics in more ways than a cross-section can. There are two error terms to
inspect, not one. Errors can be correlated within a unit over time, which quietly shrinks your standard errors.
And the relationship can drift across units as well as across years. Checking a panel model means asking each
of those questions in turn and letting the answers change the model.
:::

## Why it matters {#sec-why}

[Fixed effects](panel-and-fixed-effects.qmd) is often presented as a finished design: demean, regress, report.
Ashley's view is that an estimated panel model is a first draft, like any other regression. You check it, and
what you find often changes what you conclude. [@ashley_econometrics] His running example shows how much can change.
It is a growth regression on the Penn World Table: 187 countries, 8,180 country-years, real per-capita GDP
growth on trade openness and the consumption, investment and government shares of output. Openness looks
like it matters. After checking for nonlinearity and for differences between groups of countries, the evidence
for openness is weak. What first looked like a nonlinear openness effect turns out to be **an artefact of lumping
together countries that entered the data set at different times**. [@ashley_econometrics]

The checks themselves are the familiar ones from [regression diagnostics](../03-regression/regression-diagnostics.qmd)
and [the craft of model building](../03-regression/the-craft-of-model-building.qmd): normality and outliers,
heteroskedasticity, serial correlation, nonlinearity, parameter stability. Panels add three twists.
[@ashley_econometrics] There are **two** error components to check. Stability has to hold **across units as well
as across time**. And the usual fix for serial correlation, adding lags of the outcome, **breaks the fixed-effects
estimator**.

## First, which panel model? {#sec-menu}

Ashley sets out five standard ways to fit a panel, and a diagnostic pass often sends you from one to another.
[@ashley_econometrics]

| Model | What it uses | Its bargain |
|---|---|---|
| **Pooled OLS** | every row as if independent | consistent only under the random-effects assumption, and then RE beats it anyway |
| **Fixed effects (within)** | each unit's deviations from its own mean | robust to unit effects correlated with $X$; cannot estimate time-invariant regressors |
| **Between effects** | one row per unit: the time-averages | can estimate time-invariant regressors; discards all time variation |
| **Random effects** | a weighted blend of within and between | efficient and keeps time-invariant regressors, *if* the unit effect is uncorrelated with $X$ |
| **First differences** | period-to-period changes | needed when regressors are not strictly exogenous (lags, feedback) |

The **between-effects** model is the one most often left out of textbooks, and it is useful for diagnosis. It
regresses each unit's average outcome on its average regressors, so it shows what the data say *across* units
once time is averaged away. In the Penn World Table it disagrees sharply with fixed effects. The within estimate
of the openness coefficient is **−0.031** (SE 0.017, p = 0.066). The between estimate is **+0.206** (SE 0.061,
p = 0.001). The between standard errors are **two to four times larger**, because averaging shrinks the
effective sample from 8,180 to 187. [@ashley_econometrics] Ashley's reading is that a big within/between gap is
a symptom, not a contradiction. Fixed effects controls for year shocks; the between model averages the
coefficient across 57 years in which it may not have been stable. This is the same disagreement the
[Hausman test and Mundlak's device](correlated-random-effects.qmd) formalise.

**Choosing, with tests you can trust.** Ashley's model choice is also a lesson in reading standard errors. Fit
the growth panel as one pooled regression and openness looks significant with ordinary standard errors
(p = 0.001). White heteroskedasticity-robust errors are about 2.4–2.7 times larger and put it at p = 0.21. Clustering
by country makes them another 30–40% larger again. [@ashley_econometrics] Fixed and random effects then give
almost identical coefficients (openness −0.031 against −0.029). The textbook Hausman test nevertheless rejects
loudly (χ²(20) = 60.81, p < 0.0001), along with a warning that its variance matrix is not positive definite.
Ashley calls the rejection meaningless. The standard test assumes homoskedastic errors, which these are not, and
it often breaks down numerically even in large samples. [@ashley_econometrics] His **generalised Hausman test**
estimates both models jointly with robust errors (Stata's `suest`) and finds no significant difference, for any
coefficient or for all four together (joint p = 0.41). A Lagrange-multiplier test (`xttest0`) then rejects the
pooled model in favour of random effects (χ²(1) = 193.34). [@ashley_econometrics] So random effects is the model to
check. Ashley's point is that passing these tests makes it a candidate. It is not yet a model whose inferences you
can trust.

## Two error terms, two things to look at {#sec-two-errors}

A panel error splits into a **unit component** $\nu_i$ (one value per country, fixed over time) and an
**idiosyncratic component** $\varepsilon_{it}$ (fresh each period). Software gives you estimates of both, and
Ashley's advice is to plot both, because they fail in different ways. [@ashley_econometrics]

In the growth panel, the estimated $\varepsilon_{it}$ have **thick tails**. There are too many very small and very
large values compared with a normal distribution, a shape called *leptokurtic*. Ashley does not treat the large
values as outliers to delete. They show that the noise simply is not normal, which matters little in a sample
this large. [@ashley_econometrics] The estimated $\nu_i$ tell a more interesting story: their histogram has
**several peaks**. Following that lead, Ashley plots the number of years each country is observed and finds four
clusters. The Penn World Table grew in **four "waves"**, with countries joining in the early 1950s, around 1960,
in 1970, and from 1987 to 1994. [@ashley_econometrics] A messy residual plot has become a hypothesis: maybe the
relationship differs by wave. Diagnostic checking is meant to work like this, with each oddity pointing to a
specific test. The wave hypothesis is tested [below](#sec-stability).

## Heteroskedasticity: in the unit effect or in the noise? {#sec-het}

In a panel, unequal variances can enter at two levels. Dong Li separates two textbook cases.
[@dongli_panel]

- **Case 1:** the **unit effects** have unequal variances: some countries' permanent level is far less
  predictable than others'.
- **Case 2:** the **idiosyncratic noise** has unequal variances: some countries are simply more volatile year to
  year.

Each case has a feasible-GLS recipe. Estimate a variance per unit from within and OLS residuals, build a
unit-specific weight $\hat\theta_i$, quasi-demean, and re-run. [@dongli_panel] Dong Li then makes the practical
point: **in practice we do not like these FGLS estimators**. They need many periods per unit to estimate each
unit's variance, which most labour panels do not have. Researchers prefer standard errors that are robust to
heteroskedasticity *of unknown form*. [@dongli_panel]

Ashley adds that robust standard errors are no reason to skip the test. Regress the squared idiosyncratic
residuals on plausible sources of heteroskedasticity. In the growth panel, the error variance moved mainly with
the **year dummies and the entry-wave dummies**. That is a second clue pointing at the waves, and a test you
should run because it is cheap. [@ashley_econometrics]

## Serial correlation: three responses, and what each buys {#sec-serial}

Errors for the same country in neighbouring years are rarely independent. Note too that the within
transformation creates some correlation by itself. Demeaning a unit's errors with their own average makes the
transformed errors correlated at $-1/(T_i-1)$ even when the originals are independent. This is why ordinary and
White heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors are both inconsistent for fixed effects. [@ashley_econometrics]

The check is easy. Regress the estimated $\varepsilon_{it}$ on their own lags. In Ashley's growth model, only the
first lag is significant: **0.092** (t = 2.66), mild positive autocorrelation. It is mild because the outcome is a
growth rate, a variable "in changes". A model for the *level* of GDP would have strongly autocorrelated errors.
[@ashley_econometrics] Once you know the errors are correlated, there are three responses.

**1. Cluster-robust standard errors (the default).** Treat each unit as a cluster. This allows any pattern of
heteroskedasticity and any correlation among a unit's errors across its own periods, not just AR(1). The
correlation can also take a different form in every unit. [@ashley_econometrics] The one thing ruled out is
**correlation between different units**. Clustering changes only the *standard errors*. It leaves the
coefficients untouched, so whatever efficiency the serial correlation costs is still lost. [@ashley_econometrics]
Ashley also notes simulation evidence (Stock & Watson) that under the within transformation, cluster-robust
errors beat White errors even when $T$ is large. [@ashley_econometrics]

**2. Model the dynamics.** Serial correlation often means the model is missing dynamics, and adding lagged
variables can remove it and give a better model. Panels make this awkward. A lagged outcome inside a
fixed-effects model is no longer strictly exogenous after demeaning, so the estimator becomes inconsistent. This
is the [Nickell bias](dynamic-panels-gmm.qmd), and the fix is first differences plus instruments.
[@ashley_econometrics] Ashley's rule of thumb: unless you need the dynamics, or have endogenous regressors, fixed
effects with cluster-robust errors usually beats first differences, which give much less precise estimates.
[@ashley_econometrics] The exception is strong serial correlation. Ashley cites simulations by Reed and Ye in
which no fixed-effects standard-error estimator was reliably accurate once the error autocorrelation was well
above about 0.3. [@ashley_econometrics] Random effects can tolerate a lagged outcome, but only if the idiosyncratic
errors are homoskedastic and serially uncorrelated. [@ashley_econometrics]

**3. Feasible GLS for AR(1) errors.** If the errors look like $\varepsilon_{it}=\rho\,\varepsilon_{i,t-1}+\eta_{it}$,
estimate $\rho$ from the within residuals and **quasi-difference** each unit's series. This is the panel version
of Prais–Winsten, or of Cochrane–Orcutt if the first period is dropped. The Baltagi–Li estimator, `xtregar` in
Stata, implements it. [@dongli_panel; @ashley_econometrics] This response actually recovers the lost efficiency,
but it assumes AR(1) and, in that implementation, gives no cluster-robust errors. When heteroskedasticity is also
present, or the correlation is not AR(1), GMM is the more general route. [@ashley_econometrics]

```{python}
#| label: fig-panel-serial
#| fig-cap: "What serial correlation does to a fixed-effects regression, and what each response fixes. 50 units × 20 periods, true slope zero, a persistent regressor (AR(1), 0.8), and idiosyncratic errors that are AR(1) with the persistence ρ on the x-axis; 600 simulated panels per point. LEFT: how often a nominal 5% test of the true null rejects. Ordinary within-estimator SEs (orange) reject far too often as ρ rises; cluster-robust SEs (ink) stay near 5%. RIGHT: the spread of the slope estimate across simulations. Clustering does not change the estimate, so the fixed-effects spread (ink) is the same whichever SEs you use; quasi-differencing with an estimated ρ before demeaning (grey, a Cochrane–Orcutt-style FGLS) gives a tighter estimate once ρ is large."
#| code-fold: true
#| code-summary: "Show the figure code (within estimator, cluster-robust SEs and quasi-differencing, by hand)"

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(15)
N, T, R = 50, 20, 600
rhos = np.array([0.0, 0.15, 0.3, 0.45, 0.6, 0.75, 0.9])

def ar1(rho, shape):
    """Unit-variance AR(1) series, one per row, started from the stationary distribution."""
    e = rng.normal(size=shape)
    out = np.empty(shape)
    out[:, 0] = e[:, 0] / np.sqrt(1 - rho**2)
    for t in range(1, shape[1]):
        out[:, t] = rho * out[:, t - 1] + e[:, t]
    return out * np.sqrt(1 - rho**2)

def within(a):
    return a - a.mean(axis=1, keepdims=True)

rej_naive, rej_clust, sd_fe, sd_qd = [], [], [], []
for rho in rhos:
    rn = rc = 0
    b_fe, b_qd = [], []
    for _ in range(R):
        alpha = rng.normal(size=(N, 1))              # unit effects, correlated with x
        x = ar1(0.8, (N, T)) + 0.5 * alpha
        y = alpha + ar1(rho, (N, T))                 # true slope = 0
        xd, yd = within(x), within(y)
        sxx = (xd**2).sum()
        b = (xd * yd).sum() / sxx
        e = yd - b * xd
        se_naive = np.sqrt((e**2).sum() / (N * T - N - 1) / sxx)
        score = (xd * e).sum(axis=1)                 # one score per cluster (unit)
        se_clust = np.sqrt(N / (N - 1) * (score**2).sum()) / sxx
        rn += abs(b / se_naive) > 1.96
        rc += abs(b / se_clust) > 1.96
        b_fe.append(b)
        # quasi-difference with rho estimated from the within residuals (+1/T small-T correction)
        rh = (e[:, 1:] * e[:, :-1]).sum() / (e[:, :-1]**2).sum() + 1.0 / T
        xs, ys = within(x[:, 1:] - rh * x[:, :-1]), within(y[:, 1:] - rh * y[:, :-1])
        b_qd.append((xs * ys).sum() / (xs**2).sum())
    rej_naive.append(rn / R); rej_clust.append(rc / R)
    sd_fe.append(np.std(b_fe)); sd_qd.append(np.std(b_qd))

fig, (ax1, ax2) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(9.2, 3.9))
ax1.axhline(0.05, color="#b7b6a8", lw=1, ls=":")
ax1.plot(rhos, rej_naive, "o-", color="#e8531d", lw=2, label="ordinary FE SEs")
ax1.plot(rhos, rej_clust, "s-", color="#161613", lw=1.6, label="cluster-robust SEs")
ax1.set_xlabel("serial correlation of the errors, ρ")
ax1.set_ylabel("false-rejection rate (nominal 5%)")
ax1.set_title("Precision is overstated")
ax1.legend(frameon=False, fontsize=8, loc="upper left")

ax2.plot(rhos, sd_fe, "s-", color="#161613", lw=1.6, label="fixed effects (either SE)")
ax2.plot(rhos, sd_qd, "^-", color="#5e5e54", lw=1.6, label="quasi-differenced FE (FGLS)")
ax2.set_xlabel("serial correlation of the errors, ρ")
ax2.set_ylabel("sampling SD of the slope")
ax2.set_title("…and clustering doesn't recover efficiency")
ax2.set_ylim(0, None)
ax2.legend(frameon=False, fontsize=8, loc="lower left")
fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

The left panel is why clustering became standard: the coefficient is fine, but the ordinary standard errors
make it look far more precise than it is. The right panel is Ashley's caveat: clustering corrects the *reported*
precision and adds no *actual* precision. When the correlation is strong, modelling it is worth the trouble.
[@ashley_econometrics]

**Did you difference too much?** Modelling "in changes" carries the opposite risk. If the level series was
stationary all along, differencing it creates errors with **negative** autocorrelation at lag one. For an AR(1)
level with persistence $\phi$, the lag-one autocorrelation of the change is $-(1-\phi)/2$. Ashley therefore reads
the sign of the residual lag-one coefficient as an informal test. A significantly negative value suggests
over-differencing. The growth model's **+0.092** makes over-differencing unlikely. [@ashley_econometrics] He
prefers this to formal panel unit-root tests, which he regards as too low-powered to be very informative.
[@ashley_econometrics]

**When units are correlated with each other.** Cluster-robust errors assume units are independent of each other.
Countries hit by the same oil shock, or states sharing a labour market, break that. Year dummies absorb shocks
common to everyone, but not shocks shared by a region. Dong Li's software survey shows the wider menu. Errors can
be modelled as **groupwise heteroskedastic**, as **correlated across groups** within a period, and as **AR(1) within
groups** with a common or a unit-specific $\rho$. Stata's `xtgls` covers the nine combinations, `xtpcse` gives
panel-corrected standard errors, and generalised estimating equations (`xtgee`) choose a within-unit correlation
structure (exchangeable, AR, unstructured) directly. [@dongli_panel]

## Nonlinearity and stability: where the conclusion changed {#sec-stability}

The last two checks are the ones that changed Ashley's substantive conclusion. [@ashley_econometrics]

**Nonlinearity.** In the linear random-effects model, the openness coefficient is not significant at 5% (p = 0.078).
Adding squared terms changes that. Openness and its square are **jointly significant at the 0.1% level**
(χ²(2) = 13.82, p = 0.001). Apparently a curved relationship was hiding behind a flat linear fit.
[@ashley_econometrics]

**Parameter stability across units.** The earlier clues, the multi-peaked $\nu_i$ and the heteroskedasticity that
moved with entry wave, point to one more test. Does the slope differ by the wave in which a country joined the
data? Interact each regressor with the wave dummies. Once the other shares may differ by wave, the squared
openness terms are **no longer significant**. Testing every openness term together gives χ²(4) = 7.81,
p = 0.099: **not significant at 5%**. [@ashley_econometrics] The investment and government-share effects, by
contrast, really do differ across waves.

So the "nonlinear openness effect" was most likely produced by **pooling heterogeneous groups of countries**.
Ashley's closing judgement is honest. The final model may be an adequate *statistical* description of this data
set. But its intercept moves around over time and its key slopes depend on when a country entered the sample,
which shows it is **describing rather than explaining**. Its most useful output is a new question: why do the
waves differ? [@ashley_econometrics] He also notes what the exercise never addressed: the shares are jointly
determined with growth, so the regressors are probably endogenous. Instruments or lagged regressors are needed
before any of it is read causally. [@ashley_econometrics]

## Common pitfalls

::: {.callout-warning title="Where this goes wrong"}

- **Reporting ordinary or White SEs for a fixed-effects model.** The within transformation itself correlates
  each unit's errors ($-1/(T_i-1)$), and real panels add serial correlation on top. Cluster by unit as the
  default. [@ashley_econometrics]

- **Thinking clustering fixes serial correlation.** It fixes the *standard errors*. The coefficients, and their
  inefficiency, are unchanged. Persistent errors are often a sign of missing dynamics. [@ashley_econometrics]

- **Adding a lagged dependent variable to a fixed-effects model.** After demeaning it is correlated with the
  error, so the estimates are inconsistent. Use first differences with instruments
  ([dynamic panels](dynamic-panels-gmm.qmd)). [@ashley_econometrics]

- **Clustering by unit when units share shocks.** Clustering assumes no correlation *between* clusters.
  [@ashley_econometrics] Regional shocks that time dummies do not absorb call for an error structure that allows
  cross-unit correlation [@dongli_panel], or a coarser cluster, which is the
  [difference-in-differences](difference-in-differences.qmd) advice to cluster one level up.
  [@angrist_mostly_harmless]

- **Trusting a textbook Hausman test on heteroskedastic data.** It assumes homoskedastic errors and can fail
  numerically. In the growth panel it "rejected" at χ²(20) = 60.81 while the coefficients were nearly identical.
  Use a robust (generalised) version. [@ashley_econometrics]

- **Checking only the idiosyncratic residuals.** The unit-level residuals $\hat\nu_i$ are where heterogeneity
  across units shows itself. In the Penn World Table, their several peaks were the first sign of the entry
  waves. [@ashley_econometrics]

- **Ignoring an unbalanced panel.** When units enter at different times, the early sample may be a different
  population from the late one. Test whether slopes differ by entry cohort before trusting a pooled slope.
  [@ashley_econometrics]

- **Reaching for unit-specific FGLS on a short panel.** Estimating a variance or $\rho$ for every unit needs many
  periods per unit. With short $T$, robust standard errors are the safer choice. [@dongli_panel]

:::

## Appendix · the mathematics {.unnumbered}

::: {.math-appendix}

**Demeaning creates negative correlation.** Let $\varepsilon_{i1},\dots,\varepsilon_{iT}$ be independent with
variance $\sigma^2$, and $\ddot\varepsilon_{it}=\varepsilon_{it}-\bar\varepsilon_i$. Then
$\operatorname{Var}(\ddot\varepsilon_{it})=\sigma^2(1-1/T)$ and, for $t\neq s$,
$\operatorname{Cov}(\ddot\varepsilon_{it},\ddot\varepsilon_{is})=-\sigma^2/T$, so
$$
\operatorname{Corr}(\ddot\varepsilon_{it},\ddot\varepsilon_{is})=\frac{-1/T}{1-1/T}=-\frac{1}{T-1}.
$$
It vanishes as $T$ grows, but the standard-error problem does not (see Stock & Watson, via Ashley).

**The cluster-robust variance.** For the within estimator
$\hat\beta=(\sum_i\ddot X_i'\ddot X_i)^{-1}\sum_i\ddot X_i'\ddot y_i$, with $\hat e_i$ the vector of unit $i$'s
residuals,
$$
\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}_{\text{cluster}}(\hat\beta)=\Big(\sum_i\ddot X_i'\ddot X_i\Big)^{-1}
\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\ddot X_i'\,\hat e_i\hat e_i'\,\ddot X_i\Big)
\Big(\sum_i\ddot X_i'\ddot X_i\Big)^{-1}.
$$
Expanding the middle term for one regressor gives $\sum_i\sum_{t}\sum_{s}\ddot x_{it}\ddot x_{is}\hat e_{it}\hat e_{is}$.
The $t=s$ terms are White's heteroskedasticity-robust estimator, and the $t\neq s$ terms allow arbitrary
within-unit correlation. Cross-unit products ($i\neq j$) are absent, which is the assumption that units are
independent. The figure multiplies by $N/(N-1)$ as a small-sample correction.

**The over-differencing signature.** Let $X_t=\phi X_{t-1}+U_t$ be stationary ($|\phi|<1$) with autocovariances
$\gamma_k=\phi^k\gamma_0$, and let $Z_t=X_t-X_{t-1}$. Then
$\operatorname{Var}(Z_t)=2\gamma_0(1-\phi)$ and
$\operatorname{Cov}(Z_t,Z_{t-1})=2\gamma_1-\gamma_0-\gamma_2=-\gamma_0(1-\phi)^2$, so
$$
\operatorname{Corr}(Z_t,Z_{t-1})=-\frac{1-\phi}{2}\in\left(-\tfrac12,\,0\right).
$$
A differenced stationary series therefore always has negative lag-one autocorrelation (−½ for white noise). A
random walk ($\phi=1$) differences to white noise with zero autocorrelation. Residual lag-one autocorrelation that
is significantly *positive*, as in Ashley's growth model, points away from over-differencing.

**Quasi-differencing under AR(1) errors.** If $\varepsilon_{it}=\rho\varepsilon_{i,t-1}+\eta_{it}$, then
$$
y_{it}-\rho y_{i,t-1}=(x_{it}-\rho x_{i,t-1})\beta+(1-\rho)\alpha_i+\eta_{it},\qquad t\ge2.
$$
The unit effect becomes $(1-\rho)\alpha_i$, still constant within a unit, so demeaning the quasi-differenced data
removes it and leaves serially uncorrelated $\eta_{it}$. Prais–Winsten keeps $t=1$ by scaling it by
$\sqrt{1-\rho^2}$; Cochrane–Orcutt drops it. The figure estimates $\rho$ from the within residuals and adds $1/T$
as a rough correction for the downward bias that demeaning induces in it (see the first result above).

:::
