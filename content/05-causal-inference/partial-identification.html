Partial Identification & Bounds

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source

Every causal number is only as credible as the assumption that produced it. When the assumption you’d need for a single number isn’t believable, you can still say something honest: not a point, but a range the effect must lie in — and you can widen or narrow that range by deciding how much to assume.

A point estimate is a promise: the effect is exactly this. That promise is only kept if the identifying assumption behind it — parallel trends, no unmeasured confounding, no selective dropout — holds exactly. When it plainly doesn’t, the mature move is not to report the number anyway, nor to give up, but to bound the effect: state the smallest and largest values consistent with what you are willing to assume. Assume less and the range widens but grows more credible; assume more and it tightens but leans on a story you must defend. The band, not the point, is the honest object.

Why it matters

Almost every design in this Part earns its point estimate by asserting one strong, untestable claim: the experiment assumes no selective attrition, matching assumes selection on observables, difference-in-differences assumes parallel trends. When that claim is credible, a single number follows. But researchers routinely find themselves in the awkward middle: the assumption is not obviously true, yet abandoning the data entirely would be its own kind of dishonesty.

Charles Manski’s answer, which runs through the applied literature, is to decouple the two things a design does: describe what the data alone pin down, then add assumptions one at a time and watch the answer sharpen. (Gerber and Green 2012) With no assumptions you often get a wide range — but a range you can believe. Each extra assumption narrows it, at a stated price in credibility. The result is not a worse answer than a point estimate; it is a more truthful one, because it makes the trade between strength of assumption and strength of conclusion visible instead of hiding it inside a standard error. Gerber and Green put the tension plainly: assumption-free bounds have “the virtue of imposing few assumptions,” while the alternative — a statistical model of the missingness — “invoke[s] strong assumptions … in tension with the agnostic style of experimental investigation.” (Gerber and Green 2012)

The credibility–assumptions trade-off

Think of identification as a dial, not a switch. At one extreme the data are left to speak entirely for themselves; at the other, you impose enough structure to force out a single number. Every design sits somewhere on that dial, and where you sit determines two things that move in opposite directions:

  • Width of the conclusion. No assumptions → a wide identified set. Strong assumptions → a point.
  • Credibility of the conclusion. No assumptions → unassailable. Strong assumptions → only as good as the story.

Point identification — the single number — is the far end of the dial, bought with the strongest assumption. Partial identification lives everywhere short of it: you report the identified set, the collection of effect values consistent with your assumptions and the data. A wide set that surely contains the truth can be worth more than a precise number that rests on a fiction. The skill is choosing assumptions you can defend, then reporting exactly what they buy — no more.

No-assumption bounds: the worst case that still teaches

The cleanest illustration is attrition — outcomes going missing after random assignment. If the people who drop out differ systematically from those who stay (the most disgruntled controls vanish), the complete-case difference in means is biased, and no amount of randomization at baseline fixes it. (Gerber and Green 2012)

The assumption-free response, due to Manski, is the extreme-value bound (Gerber and Green also call it the “no-assumption bound”). (Gerber and Green 2012) Suppose the outcome Y can only lie in a known range, say [0,10]. For the missing subjects you don’t know the outcome — so imagine the worst and the best the missing values could possibly be:

  • Upper bound on the ATE: fill every missing treated outcome with the maximum (10) and every missing control outcome with the minimum (0). This makes treatment look as good as logically possible.
  • Lower bound: fill missing treated with 0 and missing control with 10 — treatment as bad as possible.

Gerber and Green’s worked case makes it tangible: eight subjects with a true ATE of 2, some outcomes lost to attrition. Substituting the extremes yields bounds of roughly [-2.75,\,4.75] — wide, but they contain the truth. (Gerber and Green 2012) Two lessons follow, and they are the whole philosophy in miniature: the bounds are guaranteed to bracket the real effect because they assumed nothing about who went missing; and they are wide for exactly the same reason. As the attrition rate climbs, or the outcome’s possible range widens, the bounds fan out and become less informative — and if Y has infinite range, they are undefined altogether (the repair is to coarsen Y to a binary event, e.g. “above threshold,” and bound that effect instead). (Gerber and Green 2012)

The figure traces this fan-out and, alongside it, the number a less careful analyst would have reported.

Show the figure code (extreme-value bounds built by hand) 
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
N, n_sim = 6000, 80
LO, HI = 0.0, 10.0
strengths = np.linspace(0.0, 1.4, 26)   # dial on how strongly outcome drives dropout

miss_frac, naive, lb, ub = [], [], [], []
for s in strengths:
    mf, nv, lo, up = [], [], [], []
    for _ in range(n_sim):
        Y0 = rng.uniform(0, 8, N)            # control potential outcome in [0,8]
        Y1 = Y0 + 2.0                         # true individual effect = 2  ->  Y1 in [2,10]
        Z  = rng.integers(0, 2, N)            # random assignment
        Yobs = np.where(Z == 1, Y1, Y0)       # observed outcome under assignment

        # selective attrition: higher observed outcomes are likelier to be missing
        p_miss = np.clip(s * (Yobs / HI), 0, 0.95)
        observed = rng.random(N) > p_miss

        t, c = (Z == 1), (Z == 0)
        to, co = t & observed, c & observed          # observed in each arm
        tm, cm = t & ~observed, c & ~observed        # missing in each arm
        nt, nc = t.sum(), c.sum()

        # naive complete-case difference in means
        nv.append(Yobs[to].mean() - Yobs[co].mean())

        # extreme-value bounds: fill missing with the range endpoints
        up.append((Yobs[to].sum() + HI*tm.sum())/nt - (Yobs[co].sum() + LO*cm.sum())/nc)
        lo.append((Yobs[to].sum() + LO*tm.sum())/nt - (Yobs[co].sum() + HI*cm.sum())/nc)
        mf.append((~observed).mean())
    miss_frac.append(np.mean(mf)); naive.append(np.mean(nv))
    lb.append(np.mean(lo)); ub.append(np.mean(up))

miss_frac = np.array(miss_frac); naive = np.array(naive)
lb = np.array(lb); ub = np.array(ub)

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7.4, 4.4))
ax.fill_between(miss_frac, lb, ub, color="#b7b6a8", alpha=0.55, label="identified set")
ax.plot(miss_frac, ub, color="#e8531d", lw=2.0)
ax.plot(miss_frac, lb, color="#e8531d", lw=2.0, label="extreme-value bounds")
ax.plot(miss_frac, naive, color="#8a8a7e", lw=1.8, ls="--", label="naive complete-case estimate")
ax.axhline(2.0, color="#161613", lw=1.6, ls="--")
ax.text(miss_frac[-1], 2.0, "  true ATE = 2", va="center", ha="left", fontsize=9, color="#161613")
ax.axhline(0.0, color="#5e5e54", lw=0.8)

# mark where the identified set first swallows zero
cross = np.argmax(lb <= 0)
if lb[cross] <= 0:
    ax.axvline(miss_frac[cross], color="#5e5e54", lw=0.9, ls=":")
    ax.text(miss_frac[cross], ax.get_ylim()[1], "bounds now\ninclude 0 ",
            va="top", ha="right", fontsize=8.5, color="#5e5e54", style="italic")

ax.set_xlabel("fraction of outcomes missing to attrition")
ax.set_ylabel("estimated average treatment effect")
ax.set_title("Assume nothing, and the bounds fan out — but never lie")
ax.legend(loc="lower left", frameon=False, fontsize=8.5)
fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()

Tightening the bounds: from no assumptions to a few

No-assumption bounds are the floor, not the ceiling. Their width is the invitation to add one mild, defensible assumption and watch the set shrink — the dial, turned one notch.

The standard first notch for attrition is monotonicity: assume that assignment never causes someone to report who otherwise wouldn’t (or vice versa). (Gerber and Green 2012) Under it, the population splits into just three observable types — Always-Reporters, If-Treated-Reporters, and Never-Reporters — because one logical combination is ruled out. That single restriction lets you trim the data (Lee’s approach) and bound the average effect specifically for the Always-Reporters, a far narrower set than the assumption-free one. (Gerber and Green 2012) The principle generalizes: monotone treatment response (“the effect can’t be negative”), a known sign of the selection bias, or a ceiling on how different the missing are — each is a small, arguable assumption that buys a tighter set. You are walking the dial from “unassailable but wide” toward “point but fragile,” stopping wherever your audience will still believe you.

There is even a bounding argument you may already be making without naming it. Angrist and Pischke note that fixed effects and a lagged dependent variable tend to bias a persistent treatment effect in opposite directions — so, under a mild story about the selection, the two estimates bracket the causal effect between them. (Angrist and Pischke 2009) Reporting both, and reading the truth as lying in between, is partial identification in everyday clothing.

A second example: bounding a difference-in-differences

The same logic rescues a difference-in-differences whose parallel-trends assumption is not believable. Manski and Pepper study the effect of right-to-carry gun laws on crime, comparing Virginia (which adopted a law in 1989) to Maryland (which did not). (Dayal 2020) The trouble is visible in the pre-period: from 1970 to 1988 the murder-rate gap between the two states is not flat but wanders between +1 and -2.7. Exact parallel trends — the DiD identifying assumption that this gap would have stayed constant — is simply not credible here. (Dayal 2020)

Rather than assert it anyway, Manski and Pepper relax it to a bound: instead of requiring the change-in-gap to be exactly zero, allow it to be no larger in magnitude than \delta, where \delta is read off the pre-period wandering (a natural choice is a high quantile of the observed year-to-year gap changes, here about 1.16). (Dayal 2020) That weaker, data-anchored assumption yields not a single treatment effect but a lower and an upper bound around the naive DiD estimate for each post-law year. (Dayal 2020) The message is identical to the attrition case: the DiD point estimate is one end of a dial; loosening its assumption to something the pre-trends actually support turns the number into an honest interval whose width is the price of that honesty.

Common pitfalls

WarningWhere this goes wrong

  • Reporting the point estimate when its assumption is visibly false. If the pre-trends wander or attrition is large and selective, a single DiD or complete-case number is a confident answer to a question the data can’t settle. Bounds are not a weaker result here — they are the correct one. (Dayal 2020)

  • Confusing bounds with sensitivity analysis. Rosenbaum \Gamma, Oster \delta, and the E-value ask “how strong a hidden confounder would it take to overturn my point estimate?” Partial identification asks “given only assumptions I believe, what is the whole range the effect could be?” The first defends a number; the second declines to commit to one. They are complementary, not the same.

  • Treating a wide identified set as failure. A set that spans zero is informative: it says, truthfully, that the credible assumptions cannot sign the effect. Narrowing it requires more assumptions, not more data — and those assumptions must be argued, not smuggled in. (Gerber and Green 2012)

  • Using extreme-value bounds when the outcome has no natural range. They are undefined for an unbounded Y. Coarsen to a binary event and bound that effect instead, or the “worst case” is literally infinite. (Gerber and Green 2012)

  • Forgetting the bounds inherit sampling error. The identified set is estimated, so its endpoints have confidence intervals of their own; a confidence interval on a set is wider than the set. Reporting the raw bounds as if they were exact understates uncertainty. (Gerber and Green 2012)

From the sources

NoteWho says what
  • Gerber & Green, Field Experiments — the two strategies for non-random attrition (assumption-free bounds vs. a model of the missingness, the latter “in tension with the agnostic style”); extreme-value / no-assumption bounds (Manski 1989) built by filling missing outcomes with the range endpoints, the eight-subject worked example where the bounds bracket the true ATE of 2 but are wide, the widening with attrition rate and outcome range, the infinite-range caveat and the coarsen-to-binary repair; and the monotonicity + trimming (Lee 2005) refinement with its three latent reporter types (Always-, If-Treated-, Never-Reporters).
  • Dayal, Quantitative Economics with R — the Manski & Pepper (2018) right-to-carry-and-crime example: reading implausible parallel trends off the Virginia–Maryland pre-period gap, relaxing the DiD assumption to |\text{change-in-gap}|\le\delta with \delta taken from a high quantile of the observed gap changes, and the resulting lower/upper bounds around the treatment effect — “bounded, not point, estimates.”
  • Angrist & Pischke, Mostly Harmless Econometrics — the observation that fixed effects and a lagged dependent variable bias a persistent effect in opposite directions and so bracket the causal effect between them (Guryan 2004) — a partial-identification argument in applied disguise.

Appendix · the mathematics

The identified set. Let \theta be the target (say the ATE). The data identify a distribution P of observables; a model/assumption set \mathcal{A} maps P to the collection of parameter values consistent with both: \Theta_I(\mathcal{A}) \;=\; \{\,\theta : \theta \text{ is consistent with } P \text{ and } \mathcal{A}\,\}. Point identification is the special case |\Theta_I|=1; partial identification is \Theta_I a nondegenerate set. Adding assumptions can only shrink it: \mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{A}'\Rightarrow\Theta_I(\mathcal{A}')\subseteq\Theta_I(\mathcal{A}). This monotonicity is the credibility–width trade-off, written as set inclusion.

Extreme-value bounds for attrition. Let Y\in[y_{\min},y_{\max}], and let R\in\{0,1\} mark whether the outcome is observed. For the treated arm, the law of total expectation gives E[Y(1)] = E[Y\mid R{=}1,Z{=}1]\,\Pr(R{=}1\mid Z{=}1) + E[Y(1)\mid R{=}0,Z{=}1]\,\Pr(R{=}0\mid Z{=}1), where only the unobserved second term is unknown. Replacing that term by y_{\max} and y_{\min} gives the sharp upper and lower values of E[Y(1)]; doing the mirror image for the control arm and differencing yields the ATE bounds \underbrace{\big(\bar y_{1}\pi_1 + y_{\min}(1-\pi_1)\big) - \big(\bar y_{0}\pi_0 + y_{\max}(1-\pi_0)\big)}_{\text{lower}} \ \le\ \text{ATE}\ \le\ \underbrace{\big(\bar y_{1}\pi_1 + y_{\max}(1-\pi_1)\big) - \big(\bar y_{0}\pi_0 + y_{\min}(1-\pi_0)\big)}_{\text{upper}}, with \bar y_z=E[Y\mid R{=}1,Z{=}z] the observed-arm mean and \pi_z=\Pr(R{=}1\mid Z{=}z) the observed fraction. The width is (y_{\max}-y_{\min})\big[(1-\pi_1)+(1-\pi_0)\big] — it grows linearly in the outcome range and in the missing fractions, and vanishes only when nothing is missing. This is exactly the fan-out the figure computes; the code just averages the finite-sample version over many draws.

The bounded DiD. With potential untreated outcomes Y_{gt}(0) for group g and time t, exact parallel trends is [Y_{T,1}(0)-Y_{T,0}(0)]-[Y_{C,1}(0)-Y_{C,0}(0)]=0, which point-identifies the treatment effect on the treated. Relaxing it to \big|[Y_{T,1}(0)-Y_{T,0}(0)]-[Y_{C,1}(0)-Y_{C,0}(0)]\big|\le\delta replaces the single counterfactual for Y_{T,1}(0) by an interval of width 2\delta, and the treatment effect inherits an interval of the same width centred on the usual DiD estimate. Taking \delta from the observed pre-period gap changes makes the assumption testable in spirit: it is chosen to be consistent with how far from parallel the groups already drift. (Dayal 2020)

Connections

  • Builds on: potential outcomes (the missing counterfactual is what bounds fill in with worst/best cases), randomized experiments (attrition and noncompliance are where bounds first earn their keep), and difference-in-differences (parallel trends relaxed to a bound).
  • Complements: sensitivity analysis & external validity — the two honest responses to an untestable assumption. Sensitivity analysis asks how much confounding would overturn a point estimate; partial identification asks what range is consistent with assuming less.
  • Relates to: instrumental variables (a violated exclusion restriction can be relaxed to a bound rather than assumed away) and combining & triangulating designs (bracketing an effect between two biased-in-opposite-directions estimators, as with fixed effects and a lagged dependent variable). (Angrist and Pischke 2009)

conclusion** visible instead of hiding it inside a standard error. Gerber and Green put the tension plainly:
assumption-free bounds have "the virtue of imposing few assumptions," while the alternative — a statistical
model of the missingness — "invoke[s] strong assumptions … in tension with the agnostic style of experimental
investigation." [@gerber_green]

## The credibility–assumptions trade-off

Think of identification as a dial, not a switch. At one extreme the data are left to speak entirely for
themselves; at the other, you impose enough structure to force out a single number. Every design sits somewhere
on that dial, and where you sit determines two things that move in opposite directions:

- **Width of the conclusion.** No assumptions → a wide identified set. Strong assumptions → a point.
- **Credibility of the conclusion.** No assumptions → unassailable. Strong assumptions → only as good as the story.

Point identification — the single number — is the far end of the dial, bought with the strongest assumption.
Partial identification lives everywhere short of it: you report the **identified set**, the collection of effect
values consistent with your assumptions and the data. A wide set that surely contains the truth can be worth more
than a precise number that rests on a fiction. The skill is choosing assumptions you can *defend*, then reporting
exactly what they buy — no more.

## No-assumption bounds: the worst case that still teaches

The cleanest illustration is **attrition** — outcomes going missing after random assignment. If the people who
drop out differ systematically from those who stay (the most disgruntled controls vanish), the
complete-case difference in means is biased, and no amount of randomization at baseline fixes it. [@gerber_green]

The assumption-free response, due to Manski, is the **extreme-value bound** (Gerber and Green also call it the
"no-assumption bound"). [@gerber_green] Suppose the outcome $Y$ can only lie in a known range, say $[0,10]$. For
the missing subjects you don't know the outcome — so imagine the *worst* and the *best* the missing values could
possibly be:

- **Upper bound** on the ATE: fill every missing **treated** outcome with the maximum (10) and every missing
  **control** outcome with the minimum (0). This makes treatment look as good as logically possible.
- **Lower bound**: fill missing treated with 0 and missing control with 10 — treatment as bad as possible.

Gerber and Green's worked case makes it tangible: eight subjects with a true ATE of 2, some outcomes lost to
attrition. Substituting the extremes yields bounds of roughly $[-2.75,\,4.75]$ — **wide, but they contain the
truth.** [@gerber_green] Two lessons follow, and they are the whole philosophy in miniature: the bounds are
*guaranteed* to bracket the real effect because they assumed nothing about who went missing; and they are *wide*
for exactly the same reason. As the attrition rate climbs, or the outcome's possible range widens, the bounds
fan out and become less informative — and if $Y$ has infinite range, they are undefined altogether (the repair
is to **coarsen** $Y$ to a binary event, e.g. "above threshold," and bound *that* effect instead). [@gerber_green]

The figure traces this fan-out and, alongside it, the number a less careful analyst would have reported.

```{python}
#| label: fig-bounds-fanout
#| fig-cap: "No-assumption (extreme-value) bounds on an average treatment effect as attrition worsens. Outcomes lie in [0,10]; the true ATE is 2 (ink dashed). Dropout is selective — subjects with higher outcomes are likelier to go missing — so the naive complete-case estimate (olive) drifts downward and, past heavy attrition, even flips sign: it confidently reports the wrong answer. The identified set (shaded) between the extreme-value lower and upper bounds (orange) is built by filling every missing outcome with the worst, then the best, value it could take. It always contains the truth — but it widens as more data go missing, and past the marked point it includes zero: the data can no longer even sign the effect. Wide honesty versus precise error is the trade this Part is about. Averaged over 80 simulated experiments of N=6000 at each attrition level."
#| code-fold: true
#| code-summary: "Show the figure code (extreme-value bounds built by hand)"

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
N, n_sim = 6000, 80
LO, HI = 0.0, 10.0
strengths = np.linspace(0.0, 1.4, 26)   # dial on how strongly outcome drives dropout

miss_frac, naive, lb, ub = [], [], [], []
for s in strengths:
    mf, nv, lo, up = [], [], [], []
    for _ in range(n_sim):
        Y0 = rng.uniform(0, 8, N)            # control potential outcome in [0,8]
        Y1 = Y0 + 2.0                         # true individual effect = 2  ->  Y1 in [2,10]
        Z  = rng.integers(0, 2, N)            # random assignment
        Yobs = np.where(Z == 1, Y1, Y0)       # observed outcome under assignment

        # selective attrition: higher observed outcomes are likelier to be missing
        p_miss = np.clip(s * (Yobs / HI), 0, 0.95)
        observed = rng.random(N) > p_miss

        t, c = (Z == 1), (Z == 0)
        to, co = t & observed, c & observed          # observed in each arm
        tm, cm = t & ~observed, c & ~observed        # missing in each arm
        nt, nc = t.sum(), c.sum()

        # naive complete-case difference in means
        nv.append(Yobs[to].mean() - Yobs[co].mean())

        # extreme-value bounds: fill missing with the range endpoints
        up.append((Yobs[to].sum() + HI*tm.sum())/nt - (Yobs[co].sum() + LO*cm.sum())/nc)
        lo.append((Yobs[to].sum() + LO*tm.sum())/nt - (Yobs[co].sum() + HI*cm.sum())/nc)
        mf.append((~observed).mean())
    miss_frac.append(np.mean(mf)); naive.append(np.mean(nv))
    lb.append(np.mean(lo)); ub.append(np.mean(up))

miss_frac = np.array(miss_frac); naive = np.array(naive)
lb = np.array(lb); ub = np.array(ub)

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7.4, 4.4))
ax.fill_between(miss_frac, lb, ub, color="#b7b6a8", alpha=0.55, label="identified set")
ax.plot(miss_frac, ub, color="#e8531d", lw=2.0)
ax.plot(miss_frac, lb, color="#e8531d", lw=2.0, label="extreme-value bounds")
ax.plot(miss_frac, naive, color="#8a8a7e", lw=1.8, ls="--", label="naive complete-case estimate")
ax.axhline(2.0, color="#161613", lw=1.6, ls="--")
ax.text(miss_frac[-1], 2.0, "  true ATE = 2", va="center", ha="left", fontsize=9, color="#161613")
ax.axhline(0.0, color="#5e5e54", lw=0.8)

# mark where the identified set first swallows zero
cross = np.argmax(lb <= 0)
if lb[cross] <= 0:
    ax.axvline(miss_frac[cross], color="#5e5e54", lw=0.9, ls=":")
    ax.text(miss_frac[cross], ax.get_ylim()[1], "bounds now\ninclude 0 ",
            va="top", ha="right", fontsize=8.5, color="#5e5e54", style="italic")

ax.set_xlabel("fraction of outcomes missing to attrition")
ax.set_ylabel("estimated average treatment effect")
ax.set_title("Assume nothing, and the bounds fan out — but never lie")
ax.legend(loc="lower left", frameon=False, fontsize=8.5)
fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

## Tightening the bounds: from no assumptions to a few

No-assumption bounds are the floor, not the ceiling. Their width is the invitation to add *one* mild, defensible
assumption and watch the set shrink — the dial, turned one notch.

The standard first notch for attrition is **monotonicity**: assume that assignment never *causes* someone to
report who otherwise wouldn't (or vice versa). [@gerber_green] Under it, the population splits into just three
observable types — Always-Reporters, If-Treated-Reporters, and Never-Reporters — because one logical combination
is ruled out. That single restriction lets you **trim** the data (Lee's approach) and bound the average effect
specifically for the Always-Reporters, a far narrower set than the assumption-free one. [@gerber_green] The
principle generalizes: **monotone treatment response** ("the effect can't be negative"), a known **sign** of the
selection bias, or a ceiling on how different the missing are — each is a small, arguable assumption that buys a
tighter set. You are walking the dial from "unassailable but wide" toward "point but fragile," stopping wherever
your audience will still believe you.

There is even a bounding argument you may already be making without naming it. Angrist and Pischke note that
[fixed effects](panel-and-fixed-effects.qmd) and a [lagged dependent variable](dynamic-panels-gmm.qmd) tend to
bias a persistent treatment effect in *opposite* directions — so, under a mild story about the selection, the two
estimates **bracket** the causal effect between them. [@angrist_mostly_harmless] Reporting both, and reading the
truth as lying in between, is partial identification in everyday clothing.

## A second example: bounding a difference-in-differences

The same logic rescues a [difference-in-differences](difference-in-differences.qmd) whose **parallel-trends**
assumption is not believable. Manski and Pepper study the effect of right-to-carry gun laws on crime, comparing
Virginia (which adopted a law in 1989) to Maryland (which did not). [@dayal_r_datascience] The trouble is visible
in the pre-period: from 1970 to 1988 the murder-rate *gap* between the two states is not flat but wanders between
$+1$ and $-2.7$. Exact parallel trends — the DiD identifying assumption that this gap would have stayed constant —
is simply not credible here. [@dayal_r_datascience]

Rather than assert it anyway, Manski and Pepper **relax it to a bound**: instead of requiring the change-in-gap
to be exactly zero, allow it to be *no larger in magnitude than $\delta$*, where $\delta$ is read off the
pre-period wandering (a natural choice is a high quantile of the observed year-to-year gap changes, here about
$1.16$). [@dayal_r_datascience] That weaker, data-anchored assumption yields not a single treatment effect but a
**lower and an upper bound** around the naive DiD estimate for each post-law year. [@dayal_r_datascience] The
message is identical to the attrition case: the DiD point estimate is one end of a dial; loosening its assumption
to something the pre-trends actually support turns the number into an honest interval whose width is the price of
that honesty.

## Common pitfalls

::: {.callout-warning title="Where this goes wrong"}

- **Reporting the point estimate when its assumption is visibly false.** If the pre-trends wander or attrition is
  large and selective, a single DiD or complete-case number is a confident answer to a question the data can't
  settle. Bounds are not a weaker result here — they are the *correct* one. [@dayal_r_datascience]

- **Confusing bounds with sensitivity analysis.** [Rosenbaum $\Gamma$, Oster $\delta$, and the E-value](sensitivity-and-external-validity.qmd)
  ask "how strong a hidden confounder would it take to *overturn my point estimate*?" Partial identification asks
  "given only assumptions I believe, what is the *whole range* the effect could be?" The first defends a number;
  the second declines to commit to one. They are complementary, not the same.

- **Treating a wide identified set as failure.** A set that spans zero is informative: it says, truthfully, that
  the credible assumptions cannot sign the effect. Narrowing it requires *more assumptions*, not more data — and
  those assumptions must be argued, not smuggled in. [@gerber_green]

- **Using extreme-value bounds when the outcome has no natural range.** They are undefined for an unbounded $Y$.
  Coarsen to a binary event and bound that effect instead, or the "worst case" is literally infinite. [@gerber_green]

- **Forgetting the bounds inherit sampling error.** The identified set is estimated, so its endpoints have
  confidence intervals of their own; a *confidence interval on a set* is wider than the set. Reporting the raw
  bounds as if they were exact understates uncertainty. [@gerber_green]

:::

## Appendix · the mathematics {.unnumbered}

::: {.math-appendix}

**The identified set.** Let $\theta$ be the target (say the ATE). The data identify a distribution $P$ of
observables; a model/assumption set $\mathcal{A}$ maps $P$ to the collection of parameter values consistent with
both:
$$
\Theta_I(\mathcal{A}) \;=\; \{\,\theta : \theta \text{ is consistent with } P \text{ and } \mathcal{A}\,\}.
$$
Point identification is the special case $|\Theta_I|=1$; partial identification is $\Theta_I$ a nondegenerate
set. Adding assumptions can only shrink it: $\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{A}'\Rightarrow\Theta_I(\mathcal{A}')\subseteq\Theta_I(\mathcal{A})$.
This monotonicity *is* the credibility–width trade-off, written as set inclusion.

**Extreme-value bounds for attrition.** Let $Y\in[y_{\min},y_{\max}]$, and let $R\in\{0,1\}$ mark whether the
outcome is observed. For the treated arm, the law of total expectation gives
$$
E[Y(1)] = E[Y\mid R{=}1,Z{=}1]\,\Pr(R{=}1\mid Z{=}1) + E[Y(1)\mid R{=}0,Z{=}1]\,\Pr(R{=}0\mid Z{=}1),
$$
where only the unobserved second term is unknown. Replacing that term by $y_{\max}$ and $y_{\min}$ gives the
sharp upper and lower values of $E[Y(1)]$; doing the mirror image for the control arm and differencing yields the
ATE bounds
$$
\underbrace{\big(\bar y_{1}\pi_1 + y_{\min}(1-\pi_1)\big) - \big(\bar y_{0}\pi_0 + y_{\max}(1-\pi_0)\big)}_{\text{lower}}
\ \le\ \text{ATE}\ \le\
\underbrace{\big(\bar y_{1}\pi_1 + y_{\max}(1-\pi_1)\big) - \big(\bar y_{0}\pi_0 + y_{\min}(1-\pi_0)\big)}_{\text{upper}},
$$
with $\bar y_z=E[Y\mid R{=}1,Z{=}z]$ the observed-arm mean and $\pi_z=\Pr(R{=}1\mid Z{=}z)$ the observed
fraction. The width is $(y_{\max}-y_{\min})\big[(1-\pi_1)+(1-\pi_0)\big]$ — it grows linearly in the outcome
range and in the missing fractions, and vanishes only when nothing is missing. This is exactly the fan-out the
figure computes; the code just averages the finite-sample version over many draws.

**The bounded DiD.** With potential untreated outcomes $Y_{gt}(0)$ for group $g$ and time $t$, exact parallel
trends is $[Y_{T,1}(0)-Y_{T,0}(0)]-[Y_{C,1}(0)-Y_{C,0}(0)]=0$, which point-identifies the treatment effect on
the treated. Relaxing it to $\big|[Y_{T,1}(0)-Y_{T,0}(0)]-[Y_{C,1}(0)-Y_{C,0}(0)]\big|\le\delta$ replaces the
single counterfactual for $Y_{T,1}(0)$ by an interval of width $2\delta$, and the treatment effect inherits an
interval of the same width centred on the usual DiD estimate. Taking $\delta$ from the observed pre-period gap
changes makes the assumption *testable in spirit*: it is chosen to be consistent with how far from parallel the
groups already drift. [@dayal_r_datascience]

:::
