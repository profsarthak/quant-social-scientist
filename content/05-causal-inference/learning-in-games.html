Learning Models: How Players Adapt

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source

Equilibrium models say where play should end up. Learning models say how people get there from where they start, by keeping a running score of how well each choice has paid and leaning toward the winners.

People in a repeated game do not jump straight to equilibrium. They keep a running score of how attractive each option has been and choose more often the ones that have scored well. Learning models differ in one question: does a choice’s score rise only when you picked it and it paid off, or also when you see it would have paid off? Moffatt’s experience-weighted attraction model nests both answers and lets the data choose.

Why it matters

The structural-estimation entry ends with noisy equilibrium: Quantal Response Equilibrium and level-k models let players miss the Nash prediction, but they are static. Each player’s strategy is the same in round 50 as in round 1. Real experimental subjects are not like that. They often start far from equilibrium and then adapt over the session, sometimes part of the way toward the equilibrium, sometimes all the way. Moffatt defines learning as an observed change in behaviour owing to experience, and the models in this entry are about why and how that movement happens, not just whether it does (Moffatt 2016).

There is a simpler tool, and it is often enough. Put the task number (round 1, 2, 3, …) into the model as an explanatory variable and see whether behaviour drifts toward the prediction. In individual decision tasks and in public-goods games without feedback about others, this works well. Contributions to the public fund fall with task number, and the “tremble” probability of a careless choice decays toward zero as subjects settle in (Moffatt 2016). In those settings people are learning about the task.

Task number stops being enough when players see what the other player did. Now each round brings news about the opponent’s behaviour and about which strategies would have paid. On top of that, the opponent is learning too, so the target keeps moving. A model for this needs to use a player’s own past payoffs and the other player’s past choices. That makes it a dynamic model, where current behaviour depends explicitly on past behaviour and outcomes (Moffatt 2016).

The simplest version: directional learning

Directional learning (Selten & Stoecker, 1986) is the most intuitive theory: after each episode, adjust your behaviour in the direction the last outcome points (Moffatt 2016).

The setting is a finitely repeated prisoner’s dilemma. Game theory predicts defection in every round, but subjects typically cooperate tacitly until near the end and then defect. The interesting variable is the round in which a player intends to defect. Defecting one round before your partner earns you the advantage, so after every supergame (ten rounds against one partner) each player has a reason to adjust:

  • If you defected after your partner, you were too slow, so next time defect earlier.
  • If you defected before your partner, you won, but you may have given up cooperative earnings by going early, so next time defect a little later.
  • If you defected in the same round, you both wish you had gone one round sooner.

Selten and Stoecker’s data have 35 subjects playing 25 supergames of ten rounds each, with partners rematched at random between supergames. The unit of analysis is the supergame, not the single round. Moffatt regresses the change in a subject’s intended defection round on whether, last supergame, they defected before, at the same time as, or after their partner, with no constant, so each coefficient is simply the average adjustment in that case (Moffatt 2016):

Last supergame you defected… Average change in intended defection round t
after your partner −0.61 (defect earlier) −7.74
in the same round −0.16 (defect slightly earlier) −2.03
before your partner +0.36 (defect later) 4.90

528 supergame transitions; R² = 0.14. Every sign matches the theory, and the “same round” effect is the smallest, as expected. Using the change as the outcome is what makes plain OLS acceptable here. Each subject’s personal tendency to defect early or late is a fixed amount, and differencing removes it, just as first differencing removes a fixed effect (Moffatt 2016).

The common machinery: attractions and a logit choice rule

The three main learning models share one structure (Moffatt 2016):

  1. Attractions. Each player keeps a number for each strategy, its attraction, which is updated after every round. Players begin with initial attractions that stand in for whatever experience they bring into the lab. These are estimated, with one strategy’s attraction fixed at zero so the rest can be identified.
  2. A logit choice rule. The chance of choosing a strategy rises with its attraction from the previous round, through the same logistic formula as a logit model. A sensitivity parameter λ says how much attractions matter. At λ = 0 they are ignored and the player flips a coin; when λ is large, the most attractive strategy is chosen almost every time.

The models differ only in how the attractions are updated.

Reinforcement learning (RL; Erev & Roth, 1998). Only what you did, and only what you got. After each round, the strategy you actually played has the payoff you received added to its attraction. Strategies you did not play gain nothing, however well they would have done. Old attractions are first multiplied by a recency parameter φ between 0 and 1. At φ = 0 only the latest payoff counts; at φ = 1 every past payoff counts equally (Moffatt 2016). It is the logic of a pigeon pecking a lever: do more of what has paid.

Belief learning (BL), also called “weighted fictitious play”. What every option would have earned. Here you update every strategy’s attraction by the payoff it would have earned against what the opponent actually did. Which payoff you actually received does not matter. Moffatt builds it up in three steps (Moffatt 2016):

  • Cournot learning. Best-respond to the opponent’s play in the last round only.
  • Fictitious play. Each strategy’s attraction is its average payoff against everything the opponent has done so far, with all past rounds weighted equally.
  • Weighted fictitious play. As fictitious play, but rounds further back are down-weighted by the recency parameter φ. At φ = 0 this collapses to Cournot learning; at φ = 1 it becomes standard fictitious play.

To write the weighted average round by round, BL tracks an experience count N(t), measured in “observation equivalents”: how many rounds’ worth of evidence the current attractions summarise.

Experience-weighted attraction (EWA; Camerer & Ho, 1999). Both, with a dial between them. EWA uses BL’s experience-weighted averaging, but weights the payoff of a strategy you did not play by a parameter δ (Moffatt 2016):

  • δ = 0: forgone payoffs are ignored, which is reinforcement learning.
  • δ = 1: forgone payoffs count fully, which is belief learning.
  • In between: the estimate of δ says how far toward belief learning people actually are.

A second parameter, ρ, controls how quickly the experience count itself depreciates. With seven parameters in all, RL and BL are both special cases of EWA, reached by fixing some of them. That makes the comparison between them a test of restrictions, not a contest between unrelated models.

Worked example: Moffatt’s pursue–evade simulation

The game. Moffatt’s running example is a pursue–evade game, analysed in the lab by Rosenthal et al. (2003). The everyday version is the football penalty: the goalkeeper (pursuer) wants to dive the same way as the kick, and the kicker (evader) wants the opposite. Each player picks Left or Right. If they differ, nothing changes hands. If both pick Left, the evader pays the pursuer 1; if both pick Right, the evader pays 2 (Moffatt 2016). In the mixed-strategy Nash equilibrium both players choose Left two-thirds of the time. Each player has to make the other indifferent, and the bigger stake on the Right pushes both of them toward the Left (derivation in the appendix).

The test. Because EWA is heavily parameterised, Moffatt demonstrates the estimation on simulated data. He simulates 100 pairs playing 50 rounds, with known true values (δ = 0.60, φ = 0.94, ρ = 0.97, λ = 0.80, initial attractions to Left of +1 for the pursuer and −2 for the evader, and N(0) = 1). He then estimates RL, BL and full EWA by maximum likelihood (Moffatt 2016):

Four lessons come out of this table:

  1. The right model recovers the truth. All seven EWA confidence intervals contain the true value. That is what tells you the estimation code is correct, and it is the reason for running it on simulated data at all. The estimate δ̂ = 0.54 (95% CI 0.32 to 0.76) says that both actual and forgone payoffs matter, with forgone ones slightly more.
  2. Both special cases are rejected. Likelihood-ratio tests against EWA give 124.34 on 3 df for RL and 14.76 on 2 df for BL (p = 0.0006). RL is rejected far more strongly. That is no surprise, since the truth was built halfway between the two.
  3. The wrong model distorts every parameter, not just the fit. Under RL the evader’s initial preference for Right is exaggerated fourfold (−8.31 against a true −2). Sensitivity λ is badly underestimated by both restricted models (0.11 and 0.43 against 0.80). Recency φ collapses under RL (0.77 against 0.94) but not under BL. Moffatt’s conclusion: identify the best-fitting model before you interpret its parameters.
  4. Some parameters are not comparable across models. BL’s λ is much larger than RL’s only because the attractions are on different scales (a running sum of received payoffs versus an average of hypothetical ones). A λ is only meaningful within its own model.

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# Pursue–evade payoffs. Player 1 = pursuer, player 2 = evader; strategy 0 = Left, 1 = Right.
def pay1(j, y2):                     # pursuer's payoff from strategy j, given the evader's choice
    return np.where(j == 1, 2.0 * (y2 == 1), 1.0 * (y2 == 0))
def pay2(j, y1):                     # evader's payoff from strategy j, given the pursuer's choice
    return np.where(j == 1, -2.0 * (y1 == 1), -1.0 * (y1 == 0))

def ewa(rho, delta, phi, lam, A10, A20, N0, y1=None, y2=None, n=100, T=50, rng=None):
    """Simulate choices (if y1, y2 are None) or return the log-likelihood of given choices."""
    simulate = y1 is None
    if simulate:
        y1 = np.zeros((n, T), int); y2 = np.zeros((n, T), int)
    A1 = np.zeros((n, 2)); A1[:, 0] = A10          # attraction to Right normalised to 0
    A2 = np.zeros((n, 2)); A2[:, 0] = A20
    N, ll = np.full(n, N0), 0.0
    for t in range(T):
        p1 = 1 / (1 + np.exp(lam * (A1[:, 0] - A1[:, 1])))   # P(Right), logit in last round's attractions
        p2 = 1 / (1 + np.exp(lam * (A2[:, 0] - A2[:, 1])))
        if simulate:
            y1[:, t] = rng.random(n) < p1
            y2[:, t] = rng.random(n) < p2
        a, b = y1[:, t], y2[:, t]
        ll += np.sum(np.log(np.where(a == 1, p1, 1 - p1)) + np.log(np.where(b == 1, p2, 1 - p2)))
        N_new = rho * N + 1                                   # experience
        for j in (0, 1):                                      # chosen payoff weight 1, forgone weight delta
            A1[:, j] = (phi * N * A1[:, j] + (delta + (1 - delta) * (a == j)) * pay1(j, b)) / N_new
            A2[:, j] = (phi * N * A2[:, j] + (delta + (1 - delta) * (b == j)) * pay2(j, a)) / N_new
        N = N_new
    return (y1, y2) if simulate else ll

truth = dict(rho=0.97, delta=0.60, phi=0.94, lam=0.80, A10=1.0, A20=-2.0, N0=1.0)

rng = np.random.default_rng(56734512)                         # Moffatt's Stata seed, reused for flavour
left1, left2 = [], []
for _ in range(20):
    y1, y2 = ewa(**truth, rng=rng)
    left1.append(1 - y1.mean(0)); left2.append(1 - y2.mean(0))
left1, left2 = np.mean(left1, 0), np.mean(left2, 0)

y1, y2 = ewa(**truth, rng=np.random.default_rng(2016))        # one experiment for the likelihood slice
deltas = np.linspace(0, 1, 41)
ll = np.array([ewa(**{**truth, "delta": d}, y1=y1, y2=y2) for d in deltas])
d_hat = deltas[np.argmax(ll)]

fig, (axL, axR) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(10, 4.2))
rounds = np.arange(1, 51)
axL.plot(rounds, left1, color="#161613", lw=2.0, label="pursuer")
axL.plot(rounds, left2, color="#b7b6a8", lw=2.0, label="evader")
axL.axhline(2 / 3, color="#8a8a7e", lw=1.1, ls=":")
axL.text(50, 2 / 3 + 0.02, "Nash: Left 2/3 of the time", ha="right", fontsize=8.5, color="#5e5e54")
axL.set_ylim(0, 1)
axL.set_xlabel("round")
axL.set_ylabel("share choosing Left")
axL.set_title("Players adapt fast, then settle short of Nash")
axL.legend(frameon=False, loc="lower right")

axR.plot(deltas, ll, color="#161613", lw=2.0)
axR.axvline(d_hat, color="#e8531d", lw=2.0)
axR.text(d_hat + 0.02, ll.min() + 1.0, f"peak δ = {d_hat:.2f}", fontsize=9, color="#e8531d")
axR.annotate("δ = 0\nreinforcement-like", (0, ll[0]), textcoords="offset points", xytext=(10, 6),
             ha="left", fontsize=8.5, color="#5e5e54")
axR.annotate("δ = 1\nbelief-like", (1, ll[-1]), textcoords="offset points", xytext=(0, -10),
             ha="right", va="top", fontsize=8.5, color="#5e5e54")
axR.set_xlabel("δ, the weight on forgone payoffs")
axR.set_ylabel("log-likelihood (other parameters at truth)")
axR.set_title("The data prefer a mix of both kinds of learning")
fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()

Our re-simulation agrees with the book where they can be compared. The log-likelihood at the true parameters lies between about −6,780 and −6,795 across the draws we tried, the same scale as Moffatt’s −6,801 for his own draw. The δ-slice peaks at 0.62 for the plotted draw, and other draws peaked between 0.50 and 0.65, well inside the width of Moffatt’s confidence interval (0.32 to 0.76). The left panel adds something the book does not discuss. At λ = 0.8, learning carries players a long way from their starting preferences, but not to Nash. They settle in the same neighbourhood as a noisy (quantal-response) equilibrium at that sensitivity, which our calculation puts at about 47% Left for the pursuer and 62% for the evader. Learning explains movement; it does not guarantee arrival.

Heterogeneity: everyone learns the same way?

The EWA model above is a representative-agent model: every subject learns by the same weighted mix of RL and BL. Moffatt flags two problems (Moffatt 2016):

  • Ignored heterogeneity biases δ. In a Monte Carlo study (Wilcox, 2006), when some learning parameters vary across subjects but estimation assumes they do not, δ is severely biased in a direction that favours reinforcement learning over belief learning. A finding that “people are mostly reinforcement learners” may partly reflect a representative-agent model rather than how people learn.
  • A mixture is a different claim from a blend. A natural alternative is a finite mixture with two types of learner, some pure RL and some pure BL, estimating the share of each. That is not the same as EWA’s δ. EWA says everyone blends the two in the same proportion; the mixture says people differ, and each is one or the other. Both can fit the same data, and they describe different worlds (compare the type-mixture logic in structural estimation).

Common pitfalls

WarningWhere this goes wrong
  • Interpreting parameters from the wrong model. Fit RL to data generated by a blend and you get a fourfold exaggerated initial preference, a sensitivity λ that is a seventh of the truth, and a recency parameter that is far too low. Choose the model (likelihood-ratio tests of the nested restrictions) before reading its numbers. (Moffatt 2016)
  • Comparing λ across models. Attractions are on different scales in RL (sums of received payoffs) and BL (averages of hypothetical ones), so λ cannot be compared between them. (Moffatt 2016)
  • Reaching for a learning model when task number will do. Without feedback about others, learning is about the task, and a task-number term (for example, a tremble that decays) is usually the best way to capture it. (Moffatt 2016)
  • Using the wrong unit of observation. In directional learning the decision happens once per supergame, so the supergame is the unit, not each of its ten rounds. (Moffatt 2016)
  • Treating a recovered simulation as validation of the theory. Recovering known parameters from simulated data shows that the code is right. It says nothing about whether real people learn this way. Moffatt estimates EWA only on simulated data and leaves the real pursue–evade data as an exercise. (Moffatt 2016)
  • Over-parameterisation. Seven parameters, several of them weakly separated (ρ and φ, N(0) and the initial attractions), stretch what 5,000 pair-rounds (10,000 binary choices) can pin down. A mixture of types or subject-level heterogeneity would add more still. (Moffatt 2016)
  • Assuming learning ends at equilibrium. Players can adapt quickly and still settle somewhere else, as in the figure. Movement toward a prediction is not evidence of arrival.

Appendix · the mathematics

Notation. Players i=1,2; rounds t=1,\dots,T; strategies s_i^j, j\in\{0,1\} (Left, Right). Player i’s payoff is \pi_i(s_i(t),s_{-i}(t)). A_i^j(t) is i’s attraction to strategy j after round t, and N(t) is the experience count.

Choice rule (Moffatt eq. 18.1). P_i^j(t)=\frac{\exp\{\lambda A_i^j(t-1)\}}{\exp\{\lambda A_i^0(t-1)\}+\exp\{\lambda A_i^1(t-1)\}}.

In plain words: a logit in last round’s attractions; λ = 0 is a coin flip, large λ always picks the favourite.

Reinforcement learning (18.2). A_i^j(t)=\phi\,A_i^j(t-1)+I\{s_i(t)=s_i^j\}\,\pi_i\big(s_i^j,s_{-i}(t)\big).

In plain words: fade the old score by φ, then add today’s payoff, but only to the strategy you played.

Weighted fictitious play (18.6 ⇔ 18.7). Writing attractions as recency-weighted averages, A_i^j(t)=\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{t}\phi^{\,t-k}\,\pi_i(s_i^j,s_{-i}(k))}{\sum_{k=1}^{t}\phi^{\,t-k}}. Let N(t)=\phi N(t-1)+1 with N(0)=0, so N(t)=\sum_{k=1}^t\phi^{t-k}. Multiply the numerator of A(t-1) by \phi and add the newest term: A_i^j(t)=\frac{\phi N(t-1)A_i^j(t-1)+\pi_i\big(s_i^j,s_{-i}(t)\big)}{N(t)}. \phi=0 gives N\equiv1 and A_i^j(t)=\pi_i(s_i^j,s_{-i}(t)), which is Cournot learning. \phi=1 gives N(t)=t, a plain running average, which is fictitious play.

EWA (18.8–18.9). N(t)=\rho N(t-1)+1,\qquad A_i^j(t)=\frac{\phi N(t-1)A_i^j(t-1)+\big[\delta+(1-\delta)I\{s_i(t)=s_i^j\}\big]\pi_i\big(s_i^j,s_{-i}(t)\big)}{N(t)}. Nesting: RL is \delta=0,\ \rho=0,\ N(0)=1. Then N(t)\equiv1 and the update is exactly 18.2. Taking N(0)=0 instead would zero out the first-round \phi N(0)A(0) term and leave the initial attractions unidentified. BL is \delta=1,\ \rho=\phi, which is exactly 18.7 with free N(0).

Likelihood. With y_{it}=1 for Right, pair p’s round-t contribution is the product of the two players’ choice probabilities, and \log L=\sum_{p}\sum_{t}\Big[\log\big(P_1^{y_{1t}}(t)\big)+\log\big(P_2^{y_{2t}}(t)\big)\Big]. The attractions make each term depend on the whole history, so the likelihood is evaluated by running the updating recursion forward for each candidate parameter vector.

Likelihood-ratio tests. LR=2(\ell_{\text{EWA}}-\ell_{\text{restricted}})\sim\chi^2_{r} under the restriction, with r the number of restrictions: 2(6863.09-6800.92)=124.34 with r=3 for RL, and 2(6808.30-6800.92)=14.76 with r=2 for BL.

Pursue–evade Nash equilibrium (Moffatt §16.2.1). If the evader plays Left with probability q, the pursuer gets q from Left and 2(1-q) from Right. Indifference requires q=2(1-q), so q=2/3. Symmetrically, the evader is indifferent when the pursuer’s p satisfies -p=-2(1-p), so p=2/3. Logit QRE replaces exact indifference with p=\Lambda\big(\lambda[q-2(1-q)]\big) and q=\Lambda\big(\lambda[2(1-p)-p]\big), where \Lambda is the logistic function. At \lambda=0.8 the fixed point is p\approx0.47, q\approx0.62.

Directional-learning regression. With D_{i,s} the intended defection round in supergame s, D_{i,s}-D_{i,s-1}=\beta_b\,\text{before}_{i,s-1}+\beta_=\,\text{same}_{i,s-1}+\beta_a\,\text{after}_{i,s-1}+u_{i,s}, with no constant (the three indicators sum to one). Any subject constant \alpha_i in D_{i,s} cancels in the difference.

subtitle: "Equilibrium models say where play should end up. Learning models say how people get there from where they start, by keeping a running score of how well each choice has paid and leaning toward the winners."
---

::: {.lead-kernel}
People in a repeated game do not jump straight to equilibrium. They keep a running score of how attractive
each option has been and choose more often the ones that have scored well. Learning models differ in one
question: does a choice's score rise only when you *picked* it and it paid off, or also when you see it *would
have* paid off? Moffatt's experience-weighted attraction model nests both answers and lets the data choose.
:::

## Why it matters

The [structural-estimation](structural-estimation.qmd) entry ends with noisy equilibrium: Quantal Response
Equilibrium and level-$k$ models let players miss the Nash prediction, but they are **static**. Each player's
strategy is the same in round 50 as in round 1. Real experimental subjects are not like that. They often start
far from equilibrium and then **adapt** over the session, sometimes part of the way toward the equilibrium,
sometimes all the way. Moffatt defines **learning** as an *observed change in behaviour owing to experience*, and
the models in this entry are about *why and how* that movement happens, not just whether it does
[@moffatt_experimetrics].

There is a simpler tool, and it is often enough. Put the **task number** (round 1, 2, 3, …) into the model as an
explanatory variable and see whether behaviour drifts toward the prediction. In individual decision tasks and in
public-goods games without feedback about others, this works well. Contributions to the public fund fall with
task number, and the "tremble" probability of a careless choice decays toward zero as subjects settle in
[@moffatt_experimetrics]. In those settings people are learning about the *task*.

Task number stops being enough when players **see what the other player did**. Now each round brings news about
the opponent's behaviour and about which strategies would have paid. On top of that, the opponent is learning
too, so the target keeps moving. A model for this needs to use a player's own past payoffs *and* the other
player's past choices. That makes it a **dynamic** model, where current behaviour depends explicitly on past
behaviour and outcomes [@moffatt_experimetrics].

## The simplest version: directional learning

**Directional learning** (Selten & Stoecker, 1986) is the most intuitive theory: after each episode, adjust your
behaviour *in the direction the last outcome points* [@moffatt_experimetrics].

The setting is a finitely repeated prisoner's dilemma. Game theory predicts defection in every round, but
subjects typically cooperate tacitly until near the end and then defect. The interesting variable is the
**round in which a player intends to defect**. Defecting one round before your partner earns you the advantage,
so after every **supergame** (ten rounds against one partner) each player has a reason to adjust:

- If you defected **after** your partner, you were too slow, so next time defect earlier.
- If you defected **before** your partner, you won, but you may have given up cooperative earnings by going
  early, so next time defect a little later.
- If you defected **in the same round**, you both wish you had gone one round sooner.

Selten and Stoecker's data have **35 subjects** playing **25 supergames** of ten rounds each, with partners
rematched at random between supergames. The unit of analysis is the **supergame**, not the single round.
Moffatt regresses the *change* in a subject's intended defection round on whether, last supergame, they defected
before, at the same time as, or after their partner, with no constant, so each coefficient is simply the average
adjustment in that case [@moffatt_experimetrics]:

| Last supergame you defected… | Average change in intended defection round | t |
|---|---|---|
| **after** your partner | **−0.61** (defect earlier) | −7.74 |
| **in the same round** | **−0.16** (defect slightly earlier) | −2.03 |
| **before** your partner | **+0.36** (defect later) | 4.90 |

*528 supergame transitions; R² = 0.14.* Every sign matches the theory, and the "same round" effect is the smallest,
as expected. Using the *change* as the outcome is what makes plain OLS acceptable here. Each subject's personal
tendency to defect early or late is a fixed amount, and differencing removes it, just as
[first differencing](panel-and-fixed-effects.qmd) removes a fixed effect [@moffatt_experimetrics].

## The common machinery: attractions and a logit choice rule

The three main learning models share one structure [@moffatt_experimetrics]:

1. **Attractions.** Each player keeps a number for each strategy, its *attraction*, which is updated after every
   round. Players begin with **initial attractions** that stand in for whatever experience they bring into the
   lab. These are estimated, with one strategy's attraction fixed at zero so the rest can be identified.
2. **A logit choice rule.** The chance of choosing a strategy rises with its attraction from the *previous*
   round, through the same logistic formula as a [logit model](../03-regression/limited-dependent-variables.qmd).
   A **sensitivity parameter λ** says how much attractions matter. At λ = 0 they are ignored and the player flips
   a coin; when λ is large, the most attractive strategy is chosen almost every time.

The models differ only in **how the attractions are updated**.

**Reinforcement learning (RL; Erev & Roth, 1998).** *Only what you did, and only what you got.* After each round,
the strategy you actually played has the payoff you received added to its attraction. Strategies you did not
play gain nothing, however well they would have done. Old attractions are first multiplied by a **recency
parameter φ** between 0 and 1. At φ = 0 only the latest payoff counts; at φ = 1 every past payoff counts equally
[@moffatt_experimetrics]. It is the logic of a pigeon pecking a lever: do more of what has paid.

**Belief learning (BL), also called "weighted fictitious play".** *What every option would have earned.* Here you
update *every* strategy's attraction by the payoff it **would have** earned against what the opponent actually
did. Which payoff you actually received does not matter. Moffatt builds it up in three steps
[@moffatt_experimetrics]:

- **Cournot learning.** Best-respond to the opponent's play in the last round only.
- **Fictitious play.** Each strategy's attraction is its *average* payoff against everything the opponent has done
  so far, with all past rounds weighted equally.
- **Weighted fictitious play.** As fictitious play, but rounds further back are down-weighted by the recency
  parameter φ. At φ = 0 this collapses to Cournot learning; at φ = 1 it becomes standard fictitious play.

To write the weighted average round by round, BL tracks an **experience** count $N(t)$, measured in
"observation equivalents": how many rounds' worth of evidence the current attractions summarise.

**Experience-weighted attraction (EWA; Camerer & Ho, 1999).** *Both, with a dial between them.* EWA uses BL's
experience-weighted averaging, but weights the payoff of a strategy you **did not** play by a parameter **δ**
[@moffatt_experimetrics]:

- **δ = 0**: forgone payoffs are ignored, which is reinforcement learning.
- **δ = 1**: forgone payoffs count fully, which is belief learning.
- **In between**: the estimate of δ says *how far toward belief learning* people actually are.

A second parameter, **ρ**, controls how quickly the experience count itself depreciates. With seven parameters in
all, RL and BL are both **special cases** of EWA, reached by fixing some of them. That makes the comparison
between them a test of restrictions, not a contest between unrelated models.

## Worked example: Moffatt's pursue–evade simulation {#sec-pursue-evade}

**The game.** Moffatt's running example is a **pursue–evade** game, analysed in the lab by Rosenthal et al.
(2003). The everyday version is the football penalty: the goalkeeper (pursuer) wants to dive the same way as
the kick, and the kicker (evader) wants the opposite. Each player picks Left or Right. If they differ, nothing
changes hands. If both pick Left, the evader pays the pursuer **1**; if both pick Right, the evader pays **2**
[@moffatt_experimetrics]. In the mixed-strategy Nash equilibrium **both players choose Left two-thirds of the
time**. Each player has to make the other indifferent, and the bigger stake on the Right pushes both of them
toward the Left (derivation in the appendix).

**The test.** Because EWA is heavily parameterised, Moffatt demonstrates the estimation on **simulated** data. He
simulates **100 pairs** playing **50 rounds**, with known true values (δ = 0.60, φ = 0.94, ρ = 0.97, λ = 0.80,
initial attractions to Left of +1 for the pursuer and −2 for the evader, and N(0) = 1). He then estimates RL, BL
and full EWA by [maximum likelihood](../02-inference/maximum-likelihood.qmd) [@moffatt_experimetrics]:

| Parameter | True | RL | BL | **EWA** |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| δ (weight on forgone payoffs) | 0.60 | 0 (fixed) | 1 (fixed) | **0.541** (0.112) |
| φ (recency) | 0.94 | 0.768 (0.085) | 0.953 (0.019) | **0.943** (0.021) |
| ρ (experience decay) | 0.97 | 0 (fixed) | = φ | **0.983** (0.019) |
| λ (sensitivity) | 0.80 | 0.110 (0.016) | 0.425 (0.036) | **0.821** (0.150) |
| pursuer's initial attraction to Left | 1.0 | 1.390 (1.457) | 1.684 (0.469) | **0.926** (0.273) |
| evader's initial attraction to Left | −2.0 | −8.311 (2.286) | −4.255 (0.770) | **−2.108** (0.561) |
| N(0) (initial experience) | 1.0 | 1 (fixed) | 0.472 (0.150) | **0.884** (0.307) |
| log-likelihood | | −6863.09 | −6808.30 | **−6800.92** |

: Moffatt's Table 18.2 (standard errors in brackets), with the true simulation values added. {.striped}

Four lessons come out of this table:

1. **The right model recovers the truth.** All seven EWA confidence intervals contain the true value. That is what
   tells you the estimation code is correct, and it is the reason for running it on simulated data at all. The
   estimate δ̂ = 0.54 (95% CI 0.32 to 0.76) says that both actual and forgone payoffs matter, with forgone ones
   slightly more.
2. **Both special cases are rejected.** Likelihood-ratio tests against EWA give **124.34 on 3 df** for RL and
   **14.76 on 2 df** for BL (p = 0.0006). RL is rejected far more strongly. That is no surprise, since the truth
   was built halfway between the two.
3. **The wrong model distorts every parameter, not just the fit.** Under RL the evader's initial preference for
   Right is exaggerated fourfold (−8.31 against a true −2). Sensitivity λ is badly underestimated by both
   restricted models (0.11 and 0.43 against 0.80). Recency φ collapses under RL (0.77 against 0.94) but not under
   BL. Moffatt's conclusion: **identify the best-fitting model before you interpret its parameters**.
4. **Some parameters are not comparable across models.** BL's λ is much larger than RL's only because the
   attractions are on different scales (a running *sum* of received payoffs versus an *average* of hypothetical
   ones). A λ is only meaningful within its own model.

The figure re-runs Moffatt's simulation from scratch (our code, his parameter values) and shows two things the
tables cannot:

```{python}
#| label: fig-ewa-learning
#| fig-cap: "EWA learning in the pursue–evade game, re-simulated with Moffatt's true parameter values (our own numpy code, not his data). Left: the share of players choosing Left in each round, averaged over 20 simulated experiments of 100 pairs. Players start where their initial attractions put them (pursuer ≈70% Left, evader ≈19%) and move within a few rounds, but they settle near 46% and 55%, well short of the Nash mix of two-thirds (dotted). With sensitivity λ = 0.8, choices stay noisy. Right: the log-likelihood of one simulated experiment as δ varies, with every other parameter held at its true value. It peaks (orange) at 0.62, next to the true 0.60, and both ends, δ = 0 (only actual payoffs count) and δ = 1 (all forgone payoffs count fully), fit visibly worse. This is a one-dimensional slice, not Moffatt's full seven-parameter estimate."
#| code-fold: true
#| code-summary: "Show the figure code (EWA simulation and likelihood by hand)"

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# Pursue–evade payoffs. Player 1 = pursuer, player 2 = evader; strategy 0 = Left, 1 = Right.
def pay1(j, y2):                     # pursuer's payoff from strategy j, given the evader's choice
    return np.where(j == 1, 2.0 * (y2 == 1), 1.0 * (y2 == 0))
def pay2(j, y1):                     # evader's payoff from strategy j, given the pursuer's choice
    return np.where(j == 1, -2.0 * (y1 == 1), -1.0 * (y1 == 0))

def ewa(rho, delta, phi, lam, A10, A20, N0, y1=None, y2=None, n=100, T=50, rng=None):
    """Simulate choices (if y1, y2 are None) or return the log-likelihood of given choices."""
    simulate = y1 is None
    if simulate:
        y1 = np.zeros((n, T), int); y2 = np.zeros((n, T), int)
    A1 = np.zeros((n, 2)); A1[:, 0] = A10          # attraction to Right normalised to 0
    A2 = np.zeros((n, 2)); A2[:, 0] = A20
    N, ll = np.full(n, N0), 0.0
    for t in range(T):
        p1 = 1 / (1 + np.exp(lam * (A1[:, 0] - A1[:, 1])))   # P(Right), logit in last round's attractions
        p2 = 1 / (1 + np.exp(lam * (A2[:, 0] - A2[:, 1])))
        if simulate:
            y1[:, t] = rng.random(n) < p1
            y2[:, t] = rng.random(n) < p2
        a, b = y1[:, t], y2[:, t]
        ll += np.sum(np.log(np.where(a == 1, p1, 1 - p1)) + np.log(np.where(b == 1, p2, 1 - p2)))
        N_new = rho * N + 1                                   # experience
        for j in (0, 1):                                      # chosen payoff weight 1, forgone weight delta
            A1[:, j] = (phi * N * A1[:, j] + (delta + (1 - delta) * (a == j)) * pay1(j, b)) / N_new
            A2[:, j] = (phi * N * A2[:, j] + (delta + (1 - delta) * (b == j)) * pay2(j, a)) / N_new
        N = N_new
    return (y1, y2) if simulate else ll

truth = dict(rho=0.97, delta=0.60, phi=0.94, lam=0.80, A10=1.0, A20=-2.0, N0=1.0)

rng = np.random.default_rng(56734512)                         # Moffatt's Stata seed, reused for flavour
left1, left2 = [], []
for _ in range(20):
    y1, y2 = ewa(**truth, rng=rng)
    left1.append(1 - y1.mean(0)); left2.append(1 - y2.mean(0))
left1, left2 = np.mean(left1, 0), np.mean(left2, 0)

y1, y2 = ewa(**truth, rng=np.random.default_rng(2016))        # one experiment for the likelihood slice
deltas = np.linspace(0, 1, 41)
ll = np.array([ewa(**{**truth, "delta": d}, y1=y1, y2=y2) for d in deltas])
d_hat = deltas[np.argmax(ll)]

fig, (axL, axR) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(10, 4.2))
rounds = np.arange(1, 51)
axL.plot(rounds, left1, color="#161613", lw=2.0, label="pursuer")
axL.plot(rounds, left2, color="#b7b6a8", lw=2.0, label="evader")
axL.axhline(2 / 3, color="#8a8a7e", lw=1.1, ls=":")
axL.text(50, 2 / 3 + 0.02, "Nash: Left 2/3 of the time", ha="right", fontsize=8.5, color="#5e5e54")
axL.set_ylim(0, 1)
axL.set_xlabel("round")
axL.set_ylabel("share choosing Left")
axL.set_title("Players adapt fast, then settle short of Nash")
axL.legend(frameon=False, loc="lower right")

axR.plot(deltas, ll, color="#161613", lw=2.0)
axR.axvline(d_hat, color="#e8531d", lw=2.0)
axR.text(d_hat + 0.02, ll.min() + 1.0, f"peak δ = {d_hat:.2f}", fontsize=9, color="#e8531d")
axR.annotate("δ = 0\nreinforcement-like", (0, ll[0]), textcoords="offset points", xytext=(10, 6),
             ha="left", fontsize=8.5, color="#5e5e54")
axR.annotate("δ = 1\nbelief-like", (1, ll[-1]), textcoords="offset points", xytext=(0, -10),
             ha="right", va="top", fontsize=8.5, color="#5e5e54")
axR.set_xlabel("δ, the weight on forgone payoffs")
axR.set_ylabel("log-likelihood (other parameters at truth)")
axR.set_title("The data prefer a mix of both kinds of learning")
fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

Our re-simulation agrees with the book where they can be compared. The log-likelihood at the true parameters
lies between about −6,780 and −6,795 across the draws we tried, the same scale as Moffatt's −6,801 for his own
draw. The δ-slice peaks at 0.62 for the plotted draw, and other draws peaked between 0.50 and 0.65, well inside
the width of Moffatt's confidence interval (0.32 to 0.76). The left panel adds something the book does not discuss. At λ = 0.8, learning carries players a long
way from their starting preferences, but not to Nash. They settle in the same neighbourhood as a noisy
(quantal-response) equilibrium at that sensitivity, which our calculation puts at about 47% Left for the
pursuer and 62% for the evader. Learning explains *movement*; it does not guarantee *arrival*.

## Heterogeneity: everyone learns the same way?

The EWA model above is a **representative-agent** model: every subject learns by the *same* weighted mix of RL
and BL. Moffatt flags two problems [@moffatt_experimetrics]:

- **Ignored heterogeneity biases δ.** In a Monte Carlo study (Wilcox, 2006), when some learning parameters vary
  across subjects but estimation assumes they do not, δ is severely biased **in a direction that favours
  reinforcement learning over belief learning**. A finding that "people are mostly reinforcement learners" may
  partly reflect a representative-agent model rather than how people learn.
- **A mixture is a different claim from a blend.** A natural alternative is a
  [finite mixture](../ml-track/mixture-models-and-em.qmd) with two **types** of learner, some pure RL and some pure
  BL, estimating the share of each. That is not the same as EWA's δ. EWA says *everyone* blends the two in the
  same proportion; the mixture says *people differ*, and each is one or the other. Both can fit the same data,
  and they describe different worlds (compare the type-mixture logic in
  [structural estimation](structural-estimation.qmd)).

## Common pitfalls

::: {.callout-warning title="Where this goes wrong"}
- **Interpreting parameters from the wrong model.** Fit RL to data generated by a blend and you get a fourfold
  exaggerated initial preference, a sensitivity λ that is a seventh of the truth, and a recency parameter that is
  far too low. Choose the model (likelihood-ratio tests of the nested restrictions) **before** reading its
  numbers. [@moffatt_experimetrics]
- **Comparing λ across models.** Attractions are on different scales in RL (sums of received payoffs) and BL
  (averages of hypothetical ones), so λ cannot be compared between them. [@moffatt_experimetrics]
- **Reaching for a learning model when task number will do.** Without feedback about others, learning is about
  the task, and a task-number term (for example, a tremble that decays) is usually the best way to capture it.
  [@moffatt_experimetrics]
- **Using the wrong unit of observation.** In directional learning the decision happens once per *supergame*, so
  the supergame is the unit, not each of its ten rounds. [@moffatt_experimetrics]
- **Treating a recovered simulation as validation of the theory.** Recovering known parameters from simulated
  data shows that the *code* is right. It says nothing about whether real people learn this way. Moffatt
  estimates EWA only on simulated data and leaves the real pursue–evade data as an exercise.
  [@moffatt_experimetrics]
- **Over-parameterisation.** Seven parameters, several of them weakly separated (ρ and φ, N(0) and the initial
  attractions), stretch what 5,000 pair-rounds (10,000 binary choices) can pin down. A mixture of types or subject-level heterogeneity
  would add more still. [@moffatt_experimetrics]
- **Assuming learning ends at equilibrium.** Players can adapt quickly and still settle somewhere else, as in the
  figure. Movement toward a prediction is not evidence of arrival.
:::

## Appendix · the mathematics {.unnumbered}

::: {.math-appendix}

**Notation.** Players $i=1,2$; rounds $t=1,\dots,T$; strategies $s_i^j$, $j\in\{0,1\}$ (Left, Right). Player
$i$'s payoff is $\pi_i(s_i(t),s_{-i}(t))$. $A_i^j(t)$ is $i$'s attraction to strategy $j$ after round $t$, and
$N(t)$ is the experience count.

**Choice rule (Moffatt eq. 18.1).**
$$
P_i^j(t)=\frac{\exp\{\lambda A_i^j(t-1)\}}{\exp\{\lambda A_i^0(t-1)\}+\exp\{\lambda A_i^1(t-1)\}}.
$$

> **In plain words:** a logit in last round's attractions; λ = 0 is a coin flip, large λ always picks the favourite.

**Reinforcement learning (18.2).**
$$
A_i^j(t)=\phi\,A_i^j(t-1)+I\{s_i(t)=s_i^j\}\,\pi_i\big(s_i^j,s_{-i}(t)\big).
$$

> **In plain words:** fade the old score by φ, then add today's payoff, but only to the strategy you played.

**Weighted fictitious play (18.6 ⇔ 18.7).** Writing attractions as recency-weighted averages,
$$
A_i^j(t)=\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{t}\phi^{\,t-k}\,\pi_i(s_i^j,s_{-i}(k))}{\sum_{k=1}^{t}\phi^{\,t-k}}.
$$
Let $N(t)=\phi N(t-1)+1$ with $N(0)=0$, so $N(t)=\sum_{k=1}^t\phi^{t-k}$. Multiply the numerator of $A(t-1)$ by
$\phi$ and add the newest term:
$$
A_i^j(t)=\frac{\phi N(t-1)A_i^j(t-1)+\pi_i\big(s_i^j,s_{-i}(t)\big)}{N(t)}.
$$
$\phi=0$ gives $N\equiv1$ and $A_i^j(t)=\pi_i(s_i^j,s_{-i}(t))$, which is Cournot learning. $\phi=1$ gives $N(t)=t$, a
plain running average, which is fictitious play.

**EWA (18.8–18.9).**
$$
N(t)=\rho N(t-1)+1,\qquad
A_i^j(t)=\frac{\phi N(t-1)A_i^j(t-1)+\big[\delta+(1-\delta)I\{s_i(t)=s_i^j\}\big]\pi_i\big(s_i^j,s_{-i}(t)\big)}{N(t)}.
$$
Nesting: **RL** is $\delta=0,\ \rho=0,\ N(0)=1$. Then $N(t)\equiv1$ and the update is exactly 18.2. Taking
$N(0)=0$ instead would zero out the first-round $\phi N(0)A(0)$ term and leave the initial attractions
unidentified. **BL** is $\delta=1,\ \rho=\phi$, which is exactly 18.7 with free $N(0)$.

**Likelihood.** With $y_{it}=1$ for Right, pair $p$'s round-$t$ contribution is the product of the two players'
choice probabilities, and
$$
\log L=\sum_{p}\sum_{t}\Big[\log\big(P_1^{y_{1t}}(t)\big)+\log\big(P_2^{y_{2t}}(t)\big)\Big].
$$
The attractions make each term depend on the whole history, so the likelihood is evaluated by running the
updating recursion forward for each candidate parameter vector.

**Likelihood-ratio tests.** $LR=2(\ell_{\text{EWA}}-\ell_{\text{restricted}})\sim\chi^2_{r}$ under the
restriction, with $r$ the number of restrictions: $2(6863.09-6800.92)=124.34$ with $r=3$ for RL, and
$2(6808.30-6800.92)=14.76$ with $r=2$ for BL.

**Pursue–evade Nash equilibrium (Moffatt §16.2.1).** If the evader plays Left with probability $q$, the pursuer
gets $q$ from Left and $2(1-q)$ from Right. Indifference requires $q=2(1-q)$, so $q=2/3$. Symmetrically, the
evader is indifferent when the pursuer's $p$ satisfies $-p=-2(1-p)$, so $p=2/3$. **Logit QRE** replaces exact
indifference with $p=\Lambda\big(\lambda[q-2(1-q)]\big)$ and $q=\Lambda\big(\lambda[2(1-p)-p]\big)$, where
$\Lambda$ is the logistic function. At $\lambda=0.8$ the fixed point is $p\approx0.47$, $q\approx0.62$.

**Directional-learning regression.** With $D_{i,s}$ the intended defection round in supergame $s$,
$$
D_{i,s}-D_{i,s-1}=\beta_b\,\text{before}_{i,s-1}+\beta_=\,\text{same}_{i,s-1}+\beta_a\,\text{after}_{i,s-1}+u_{i,s},
$$
with no constant (the three indicators sum to one). Any subject constant $\alpha_i$ in $D_{i,s}$ cancels in the
difference.

:::
