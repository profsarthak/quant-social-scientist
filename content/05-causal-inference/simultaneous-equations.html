Simultaneous Equations: When the Outcome Pushes Back

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source

Price and quantity, police and murder, openness and inflation. When two variables settle each other, a regression of one on the other estimates neither relationship, unless something outside the system shifts one of them on its own.

In a market you only ever see where supply and demand cross. A cloud of such crossing points is not a demand curve, not a supply curve, and not any mixture of the two you can name. The way out is the oldest idea in econometrics: find something that shifts one curve and leaves the other alone. As it moves, the crossing point slides along the curve that stayed put and traces it out. That is identification, and the simultaneous equations model is where the word comes from.

Why it matters

A city council wants to know whether hiring more police lowers the murder rate. The obvious regression compares cities with more and fewer officers per head. But cities do not choose their police forces at random: a city that expects more murders hires more police. (Wooldridge 2019) The data mix two behavioural relationships, one describing potential murderers and one describing city officials, and the regression cannot tell them apart. It will tend to find that police “cause” murder.

This is simultaneity, the third classic source of endogeneity alongside omitted variables and measurement error (Gauss–Markov lists all three). (Wooldridge 2019) The simultaneous equations model (SEM) is the framework that makes the problem precise. It also gave us the vocabulary used by every instrumental-variables study since: structural versus reduced form, exclusion restrictions, identified, overidentified. (Wooldridge 2019)

Each equation must stand on its own

Wooldridge’s first and most important rule is that every equation in an SEM should have a ceteris paribus, causal interpretation on its own. (Wooldridge 2019) Because we only observe outcomes in equilibrium, writing the equations means thinking counterfactually: how many hours would workers offer if the wage were different from the one we observe?

The classic case is a county-level market for agricultural labour: (Wooldridge 2019)

  • Labour supply (a behavioural equation for workers): hours offered rise with the farm wage, and fall with the manufacturing wage, which is the opportunity cost of working on a farm. The manufacturing wage is an observed supply shifter.
  • Labour demand (a behavioural equation for farmers): hours wanted fall with the wage, and rise with agricultural land area, since land and labour are complements. Land area is an observed demand shifter.

The two equations describe different people making different decisions. They are linked only because the market clears: in each county we see a single wage and a single number of hours, where the curves cross. Hours and the wage are the endogenous variables. The two shifters, determined outside the model, are exogenous, which statistically means uncorrelated with the unobserved parts of supply and demand. (Wooldridge 2019)

Notice what the shifters do. Without them the two equations look identical: hours on one side, the wage and an error on the other. Nothing in the data would say which one is supply. If the only extra variable were average adult education, which plausibly affects both sides, the equations would still be indistinguishable and neither could be estimated. (Wooldridge 2019)

When an SEM is the wrong tool

Wooldridge is blunt that “all too many SEM applications” fail the stand-alone test. (Wooldridge 2019) His example is a household choosing housing expenditure and saving, written as two equations each containing the other. Ask what the housing equation means: if income rises by $10,000, how does housing spending change, holding saving fixed? That is not an interesting question, because the same household chooses both, jointly, as functions of income, prices and preferences. Neither equation has a meaning in isolation, and with the same controls in both, they could not be estimated anyway. The same applies to a student’s weekly hours of study and hours of paid work. (Wooldridge 2019)

The lesson: two variables being determined at the same time does not make an SEM appropriate. The model fits when the equations describe different agents or different sides of a market: supply and demand, murderers and city councils. (Wooldridge 2019)

Why OLS fails: simultaneity bias

Take two structural equations, each containing the other’s outcome:

y_1 = \alpha_1 y_2 + \beta_1 z_1 + u_1, \qquad y_2 = \alpha_2 y_1 + \beta_2 z_2 + u_2 .

Substitute the first into the second and solve for y_2. Provided \alpha_1\alpha_2 \ne 1 (the curves are not parallel), you get the reduced form: y_2 written purely in terms of the exogenous variables and the errors. (Wooldridge 2019) Its error term is (\alpha_2 u_1 + u_2)/(1-\alpha_1\alpha_2), which contains u_1. So y_2 is correlated with the very error in the equation we want to estimate. That is the whole problem in one line: a shock to y_1 moves y_2 through the second equation, and feeds back into the regressor. (Wooldridge 2019)

The only escape is the special case where \alpha_2 = 0 (so y_1 does not affect y_2 at all) and the two errors are uncorrelated. In other words, OLS works when there is no simultaneity to begin with. (Wooldridge 2019)

The direction of the bias can be worked out in the simplest version. With u_1 and u_2 uncorrelated, \operatorname{Cov}(y_2, u_1) has the sign of \alpha_2/(1-\alpha_1\alpha_2). (Wooldridge 2019) For police and murder, we expect more murder to bring more police (\alpha_2 > 0) and more police to bring less murder (\alpha_1 < 0), so \alpha_1\alpha_2 < 0 and the bias is upward. OLS will understate how effective police are, and could even show a positive “effect” of police on murder. (Wooldridge 2019)

Identification: shift one curve, trace the other

Wooldridge’s milk market makes the idea visual. (Wooldridge 2019) Let quantity and price of milk be determined by

  • supply, which depends on price and on the price of cattle feed (a cost shifter), and
  • demand, which depends on price and nothing else that we observe.

Which equation can be estimated? Demand. The feed price moves the supply curve up and down while, by assumption, leaving demand untouched. Each movement slides the equilibrium along the fixed demand curve. With enough variation in feed prices, the equilibria trace out demand, and feed price serves as the instrument for price in the demand equation. (Wooldridge 2019)

Supply, by contrast, cannot be estimated: nothing observed shifts demand, and the unobserved demand shocks do not help, because “we need something observed.” (Wooldridge 2019) Add an observed demand shifter (income, say) and supply becomes identified too. The summary is worth memorising: it is an exogenous variable in the supply equation that lets us estimate the demand equation. (Wooldridge 2019)

Show the figure code 
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(16)
n = 600
a_d, b_d = -1.0, 20.0          # demand:  q = 20 - 1.0 p + u_d
a_s, c_s = 1.5, -4.0           # supply:  q = -4 + 1.5 p - 2.0 z + u_s

def equilibria(z, sd_d, sd_s):
    u_d = rng.normal(0, sd_d, n)
    u_s = rng.normal(0, sd_s, n)
    # solve b_d + a_d p + u_d = c_s + a_s p - 2 z + u_s for p
    p = (b_d - c_s + u_d - u_s + 2.0 * z) / (a_s - a_d)
    q = b_d + a_d * p + u_d
    return p, q

def ols_slope(x, y):
    xc = x - x.mean()
    return (xc * (y - y.mean())).sum() / (xc ** 2).sum()

# Left: no observed shifter, demand shocks larger than supply shocks
p0, q0 = equilibria(np.zeros(n), sd_d=2.0, sd_s=1.0)
b_ols0 = ols_slope(p0, q0)

# Right: an observed cost shifter z (feed price) moves supply
z = rng.uniform(0, 4, n)
p1, q1 = equilibria(z, sd_d=1.0, sd_s=1.0)
zc = z - z.mean()
b_iv = (zc * (q1 - q1.mean())).sum() / (zc * (p1 - p1.mean())).sum()   # Wald/IV slope
b_ols1 = ols_slope(p1, q1)

fig, (ax1, ax2) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(9.8, 4.3), sharey=True)
grid = np.linspace(min(p0.min(), p1.min()) - 0.3, max(p0.max(), p1.max()) + 0.3, 50)

ax1.scatter(p0, q0, s=9, color="#b7b6a8", alpha=0.7, label="observed equilibria")
ax1.plot(grid, b_d + a_d * grid, color="#161613", lw=2.2, label=f"true demand (slope {a_d:.1f})")
ax1.plot(grid, q0.mean() + b_ols0 * (grid - p0.mean()), color="#5e5e54", lw=1.8, ls="--",
         label=f"OLS of q on p (slope {b_ols0:+.2f})")
ax1.set_xlabel("price")
ax1.set_ylabel("quantity")
ax1.set_title("Only unobserved shocks: OLS estimates neither curve")
ax1.legend(fontsize=8, frameon=False, loc="lower left")

shades = ["#b7b6a8", "#8a8a7e", "#5e5e54"]
bands = np.digitize(z, [4 / 3, 8 / 3])
for k in range(3):
    m = bands == k
    ax2.scatter(p1[m], q1[m], s=9, color=shades[k], alpha=0.75)
    ax2.plot(p1[m].mean(), q1[m].mean(), "D", color="#161613", ms=7, zorder=5)
ax2.plot(grid, b_d + a_d * grid, color="#161613", lw=2.2, label="true demand")
ax2.plot(grid, q1.mean() + b_iv * (grid - p1.mean()), color="#e8531d", lw=2.4,
         label=f"IV using feed price (slope {b_iv:+.2f})")
ax2.plot(grid, q1.mean() + b_ols1 * (grid - p1.mean()), color="#5e5e54", lw=1.6, ls="--",
         label=f"OLS (slope {b_ols1:+.2f})")
ax2.set_xlabel("price")
ax2.set_title("A supply shifter traces out demand")
ax2.legend(fontsize=8, frameon=False, loc="lower left")
ax2.annotate("band means\n(low → high feed price)", (p1[bands == 2].mean(), q1[bands == 2].mean()),

In the left panel the OLS line is not a biased estimate of demand in any useful sense. It is a blend of demand slope and supply slope, weighted by which curve happened to be shaken harder. Make demand shocks smaller than supply shocks and the OLS line tilts towards demand; make them larger and it tilts towards supply. Only the right panel, where an observed variable moves just one curve, gives an answer that does not depend on the relative size of the shocks.

The rank and order conditions

For a general two-equation system, the rules are: (Wooldridge 2019)

  • Rank condition (necessary and sufficient). The first equation is identified if and only if the second equation contains at least one exogenous variable, with a nonzero coefficient, that is excluded from the first.
  • Order condition (necessary only). At least one exogenous variable is excluded from the equation. This is a matter of counting and is trivial to check once both equations are written down.

The difference matters. The order condition only asks whether you wrote an excluded variable into the other equation. The rank condition asks whether it really belongs there: whether it actually moves the endogenous regressor. That part is testable, with a t or F test on the excluded variables in the reduced form, exactly the first-stage check of IV. (Wooldridge 2019)

The exclusion restrictions are what make identification possible, and they are assumptions about the world. Saying that the cattle-feed price does not enter milk demand, or that past experience does not affect how many hours a woman wants to work once her wage is known, is a substantive claim. You defend it with economic reasoning; you cannot test it. (Wooldridge 2019)

Worked example 1: labour supply of married women

Wooldridge’s Example 16.3/16.5 uses the MROZ data on 428 married women in the workforce. (Wooldridge 2019)

  • Labour supply: hours depend on log wage, education, age, number of children under six, and non-wage income (which includes the husband’s earnings).
  • Wage offer: log wage depends on hours, education, experience and experience squared.

The supply equation is identified because experience and its square are excluded from it but appear in the wage equation. That is an assumption: once the wage is controlled for, past experience is taken to have no direct effect on how much a woman wants to work. The wage equation in turn is identified by age, young children and non-wage income, which are assumed not to affect the wage offered. Wooldridge notes that this would be wrong if employers discriminated by age or by number of small children. (Wooldridge 2019)

Estimated by 2SLS with all six exogenous variables as instruments: (Wooldridge 2019)

Estimate of the wage effect on hours Coefficient on log(wage) SE
OLS −2.05 54.88
2SLS 1,639.56 470.58

OLS says wages have no effect on hours. 2SLS says the supply curve slopes clearly upward. Because hours are in levels and the wage in logs, the elasticity is 1{,}640/\text{hours}: about 1.26 at the average of 1,303 hours, and over 2 for someone working 800 hours. That is a large elasticity. (Wooldridge 2019) A regression-based endogeneity test (add the reduced-form residual to the OLS equation) gives a t statistic of 6.61 in absolute value, so log(wage) does appear endogenous. (Wooldridge 2019) In the other direction, the 2SLS wage-offer equation finds no effect of hours on the offered wage (.00013, SE .00025). (Wooldridge 2019)

Wooldridge also flags the limits. The population is women who already work, because a wage offer is unobserved for those who do not; including them is the sample-selection problem of Chapter 17. And education appears in both equations: if omitted ability sits in the error, education is endogenous too. “The distinction between omitted variables and simultaneity in applications is not always sharp.” (Wooldridge 2019)

Worked example 2: is openness good for inflation?

Romer (1993) argued that more open economies should have lower inflation. Wooldridge sets out the system Romer has in mind: (Wooldridge 2019)

  • Inflation depends on openness (import share of GDP) and log per-capita income.
  • Openness depends on inflation, log per-capita income and log land area: smaller countries tend to trade more.

Land area is excluded from the inflation equation, so the inflation equation is identified provided land area really matters for openness. The openness equation is not identified, since it contains both exogenous variables. We do not need it. (Wooldridge 2019)

Check the rank condition first. In the reduced form for openness (114 countries, R^2 = .449), log land area has a t statistic over nine in absolute value: smaller countries are indeed more open. Log income is insignificant there, and Wooldridge stresses that this is “irrelevant.” (Wooldridge 2019) Then the IV estimate of openness on inflation is −.337 (SE .144) against an OLS estimate of −.215 (SE .095). Each extra percentage point of import share goes with about a third of a percentage point lower annual inflation. (Wooldridge 2019)

Bigger systems: counting is not enough

With three or more equations, proving identification needs matrix algebra, but failure is often easy to spot. (Wooldridge 2019) Wooldridge’s three-equation example has an equation that contains every exogenous variable in the system, which leaves no instruments for its endogenous regressor. That equation is unidentified. The general order condition is: (Wooldridge 2019)

the number of exogenous variables excluded from the equation must be at least the number of endogenous variables on its right-hand side.

The terms just identified and overidentified, familiar from IV, were born here. An equation with two endogenous regressors and three excluded exogenous variables has one overidentifying restriction, which can be tested. (Wooldridge 2019)

The subtle point is that the order condition can be satisfied on paper and still fail in fact. In Wooldridge’s system, the second equation’s only excluded variable, z_4, appears solely in the third equation. If its coefficient there is actually zero, z_4 is unrelated to every endogenous variable, and the second equation is not identified after all. Identification of one equation depends on the parameter values in other equations, which we can never know for sure. (Wooldridge 2019)

Any identified equation, in any size of system, can be estimated by 2SLS, using as instruments all the exogenous variables that appear anywhere in the system. System methods such as three-stage least squares are more efficient when the whole system is correctly specified, but Wooldridge leaves them beyond the scope of the book. (Wooldridge 2019)

SEMs with time series: the rise and fall of macro models

Large simultaneous systems were among the first econometric models, built to describe whole economies. Wooldridge’s Keynesian example has a consumption function, an investment function and the national-income identity Y = C + I + G. The identity holds by definition, so it is never estimated, but it makes income endogenous. (Wooldridge 2019) He gives three reasons such models are “seldom estimated now”: (Wooldridge 2019)

  1. Exogeneity is hard to believe at the aggregate level. Taxes depend on income; government spending responds to the economy; interest rates are set with an eye on output. Add a money market to make interest rates endogenous, and there are no longer enough exogenous variables to identify the equations.
  2. They were static. Adding lags helps. A lagged endogenous variable is called predetermined, and if the errors are uncorrelated with the past it can act as its own instrument.
  3. The series are persistent. Aggregate consumption, income and investment look like they have unit roots, which breaks the weak-dependence assumptions standard OLS and 2SLS inference relies on. Specifying the system in growth rates helps, but that is a different model from one in levels.

Worked example 3: the permanent income hypothesis. Campbell and Mankiw (1990) tested whether consumption growth responds to predictable income growth, which the pure PIH says it should not. (Wooldridge 2019) Under the PIH the error is unpredictable from anything known last year, so lagged consumption growth, income growth and real interest rates are valid instruments. Using annual US data for 1959–1995 (n = 35), Wooldridge’s 2SLS estimate of the income-growth coefficient is .586 (SE .135, t = 4.34): a 1% rise in disposable income raises consumption by over half a percent. The real-interest coefficient is tiny and insignificant. The pure PIH is strongly rejected, as Campbell and Mankiw found. (Wooldridge 2019)

SEMs with panel data: differencing first, then instruments

Panel data let you remove a fixed unobserved effect from each equation, a taste for leisure, say, while still dealing with simultaneity. The recipe is two steps: difference or time-demean to remove the fixed effect, then find instruments for the endogenous variable in the transformed equation. (Wooldridge 2019)

The catch is that the instruments must change over time, and differently for different units. Wooldridge’s warning case: in a differenced labour-supply equation for people who work every year, experience rises by exactly one for everyone, every year. Its change is a constant and useless as an instrument. (Wooldridge 2019)

Worked example 4: prisons and violent crime. Levitt (1996) wanted the effect of prison population on crime, but crime rates and prison populations drive each other. His instrument was prison-overcrowding litigation: whether a final court decision on overcrowding had been reached in the current year or the previous two. Such rulings force prison populations down for reasons unrelated to that year’s crime. (Wooldridge 2019) On Wooldridge’s subset of Levitt’s data (51 units × 14 years, 1980–1993, 714 observations) for the change in log violent crime: (Wooldridge 2019)

Estimator Effect of log prison population SE
Pooled OLS, first differences −.181 .048
Pooled 2SLS, litigation instruments −1.032 .370

The 2SLS effect is much larger and, as usual, much less precise. Levitt found similar results with a longer period and more instruments. (Wooldridge 2019) The direction of the gap is what the simultaneity story predicts: rising crime pushes prison populations up, which masks the crime-reducing effect of incarceration in OLS.

Common pitfalls

WarningWhere this goes wrong

  • Writing an SEM for choices made by one agent. Housing and saving, study hours and work hours: if the same person chooses both, neither equation has a ceteris paribus meaning. Model each outcome as a function of the exogenous variables instead. (Wooldridge 2019)

  • Reading a regression through equilibrium points as a demand (or supply) curve. It is neither. Its slope depends on which curve was shocked harder, and the sign can be wrong, as with police “raising” murder. (Wooldridge 2019)

  • Satisfying the order condition and calling it identification. Counting excluded variables is necessary, not sufficient. Check in the reduced form that the excluded variables actually move the endogenous regressor. Land area’s t > 9 is the good case. (Wooldridge 2019)

  • Treating exclusion restrictions as facts. “Experience does not affect desired hours” and “age does not affect the wage offer” are assumptions, and Wooldridge points out how each could fail. Defend them with economics; no test will certify them. (Wooldridge 2019)

  • Assuming macro aggregates are exogenous. Taxes, government spending and interest rates respond to the economy. Old-style aggregate SEMs fail here first. (Wooldridge 2019)

  • Using an instrument that does not vary after differencing. In panel SEMs, an instrument whose change is the same for everyone (like a year of experience) carries no information. (Wooldridge 2019)

Appendix · the mathematics

The reduced form. With intercepts suppressed, take y_1 = \alpha_1 y_2 + \beta_1 z_1 + u_1 and y_2 = \alpha_2 y_1 + \beta_2 z_2 + u_2. Substituting the first into the second gives (1-\alpha_2\alpha_1)\,y_2 = \alpha_2\beta_1 z_1 + \beta_2 z_2 + \alpha_2 u_1 + u_2. If \alpha_1\alpha_2 \ne 1:

y_2 = \pi_{21} z_1 + \pi_{22} z_2 + v_2, \qquad \pi_{21} = \frac{\alpha_2\beta_1}{1-\alpha_1\alpha_2},\; \pi_{22} = \frac{\beta_2}{1-\alpha_1\alpha_2},\; v_2 = \frac{\alpha_2 u_1 + u_2}{1-\alpha_1\alpha_2}.

The reduced-form parameters are nonlinear functions of the structural ones. Because v_2 is a combination of the structural errors, it is uncorrelated with z_1, z_2, so the reduced form can be estimated consistently by OLS. That is the first stage of 2SLS. (Wooldridge 2019)

The sign of the bias. Drop z_1 and assume \operatorname{Cov}(u_1,u_2)=0. Then

\operatorname{Cov}(y_2,u_1) = \operatorname{Cov}(v_2,u_1) = \frac{\alpha_2}{1-\alpha_1\alpha_2}\,\sigma_1^2 ,

so the inconsistency in the OLS estimate of \alpha_1 has the sign of \alpha_2/(1-\alpha_1\alpha_2). (Wooldridge 2019) (Our addition: in this one-regressor case, \operatorname{plim}\hat\alpha_1^{\text{OLS}} = \alpha_1 + \operatorname{Cov}(y_2,u_1)/\operatorname{Var}(y_2), the same omitted-variable-style formula as in regression and causality.)

Why the IV slope works in the figure. In the milk market, demand is q = \beta_d + \alpha_d p + u_d and the feed price z enters only supply. If \operatorname{Cov}(z,u_d)=0,

\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(z,q)}{\operatorname{Cov}(z,p)} = \frac{\alpha_d\operatorname{Cov}(z,p) + \operatorname{Cov}(z,u_d)}{\operatorname{Cov}(z,p)} = \alpha_d ,

provided \operatorname{Cov}(z,p)\ne 0, which is the rank condition in this setting. The supply slope never appears, which is why the right panel’s orange line lands on demand whatever the supply curve looks like.

Counting restrictions. For an equation with G_1 right-hand-side endogenous variables and K total exogenous variables in the system, of which K_1 appear in the equation, the order condition is K - K_1 \ge G_1. The number of overidentifying restrictions is the total number of exogenous variables in the system minus the total number of explanatory variables in the equation, K - (K_1 + G_1). (Wooldridge 2019) The rank condition for a general system requires matrix algebra (Wooldridge points to his graduate text, Chapter 9).

Panel SEMs. With unobserved effects a_{i1}, the equation y_{it1} = \alpha_1 y_{it2} + z_{it1}\beta_1 + a_{i1} + u_{it1} is first-differenced to \Delta y_{it1} = \alpha_1\Delta y_{it2} + \Delta z_{it1}\beta_1 + \Delta u_{it1} and estimated by pooled 2SLS with the time-varying elements of z_{it2} excluded from z_{it1} as instruments. Time-demeaning plus pooled 2SLS is equivalent to 2SLS with unit dummies acting as their own instruments; if the demeaned equation is estimated directly, the degrees of freedom must be corrected to N(T-1) - k_1. (Wooldridge 2019)

::: {.lead-kernel}
In a market you only ever see **where supply and demand cross**. A cloud of such crossing points is not a
demand curve, not a supply curve, and not any mixture of the two you can name. The way out is the oldest
idea in econometrics: find something that **shifts one curve and leaves the other alone**. As it moves, the
crossing point slides along the curve that stayed put and traces it out. That is identification, and the
simultaneous equations model is where the word comes from.
:::

## Why it matters

A city council wants to know whether hiring more police lowers the murder rate. The obvious regression
compares cities with more and fewer officers per head. But cities do not choose their police forces at
random: a city that *expects* more murders hires more police. [@wooldridge_intro] The data mix two
behavioural relationships, one describing potential murderers and one describing city officials, and the
regression cannot tell them apart. It will tend to find that police "cause" murder.

This is **simultaneity**, the third classic source of endogeneity alongside omitted variables and
measurement error ([Gauss–Markov](../03-regression/gauss-markov-assumptions.qmd) lists all three).
[@wooldridge_intro] The simultaneous equations model (SEM) is the framework that makes the problem
precise. It also gave us the vocabulary used by every
[instrumental-variables](instrumental-variables.qmd) study since: *structural* versus *reduced form*,
*exclusion restrictions*, *identified*, *overidentified*. [@wooldridge_intro]

## Each equation must stand on its own

Wooldridge's first and most important rule is that **every equation in an SEM should have a ceteris
paribus, causal interpretation on its own.** [@wooldridge_intro] Because we only observe outcomes in
equilibrium, writing the equations means thinking counterfactually: *how many hours would workers offer if
the wage were different from the one we observe?*

The classic case is a county-level market for agricultural labour: [@wooldridge_intro]

- **Labour supply** (a behavioural equation for *workers*): hours offered rise with the farm wage, and fall
  with the manufacturing wage, which is the opportunity cost of working on a farm. The manufacturing wage
  is an **observed supply shifter**.
- **Labour demand** (a behavioural equation for *farmers*): hours wanted fall with the wage, and rise with
  agricultural land area, since land and labour are complements. Land area is an **observed demand
  shifter**.

The two equations describe different people making different decisions. They are linked only because the
market clears: in each county we see a single wage and a single number of hours, where the curves cross.
Hours and the wage are the **endogenous** variables. The two shifters, determined outside the model, are
**exogenous**, which statistically means uncorrelated with the unobserved parts of supply and demand.
[@wooldridge_intro]

Notice what the shifters do. Without them the two equations look identical: hours on one side, the wage
and an error on the other. **Nothing in the data would say which one is supply.** If the only extra
variable were average adult education, which plausibly affects both sides, the equations would still be
indistinguishable and neither could be estimated. [@wooldridge_intro]

### When an SEM is the wrong tool

Wooldridge is blunt that *"all too many SEM applications"* fail the stand-alone test. [@wooldridge_intro]
His example is a household choosing **housing expenditure and saving**, written as two equations each
containing the other. Ask what the housing equation means: *if income rises by \$10,000, how does housing
spending change, holding saving fixed?* That is not an interesting question, because the same household
chooses both, jointly, as functions of income, prices and preferences. Neither equation has a meaning in
isolation, and with the same controls in both, they could not be estimated anyway. The same applies to a
student's weekly hours of study and hours of paid work. [@wooldridge_intro]

The lesson: **two variables being determined at the same time does not make an SEM appropriate.** The
model fits when the equations describe *different agents* or *different sides of a market*: supply and
demand, murderers and city councils. [@wooldridge_intro]

## Why OLS fails: simultaneity bias

Take two structural equations, each containing the other's outcome:

$$
y_1 = \alpha_1 y_2 + \beta_1 z_1 + u_1, \qquad y_2 = \alpha_2 y_1 + \beta_2 z_2 + u_2 .
$$

Substitute the first into the second and solve for $y_2$. Provided $\alpha_1\alpha_2 \ne 1$ (the curves
are not parallel), you get the **reduced form**: $y_2$ written purely in terms of the exogenous variables
and the errors. [@wooldridge_intro] Its error term is $(\alpha_2 u_1 + u_2)/(1-\alpha_1\alpha_2)$, which
contains $u_1$. So $y_2$ is correlated with the very error in the equation we want to estimate. That is
the whole problem in one line: **a shock to $y_1$ moves $y_2$ through the second equation, and feeds back
into the regressor.** [@wooldridge_intro]

The only escape is the special case where $\alpha_2 = 0$ (so $y_1$ does not affect $y_2$ at all) *and*
the two errors are uncorrelated. In other words, OLS works when there is no simultaneity to begin with.
[@wooldridge_intro]

The direction of the bias can be worked out in the simplest version. With $u_1$ and $u_2$ uncorrelated,
$\operatorname{Cov}(y_2, u_1)$ has the sign of $\alpha_2/(1-\alpha_1\alpha_2)$. [@wooldridge_intro]
For police and murder, we expect more murder to bring more police ($\alpha_2 > 0$) and more police to
bring less murder ($\alpha_1 < 0$), so $\alpha_1\alpha_2 < 0$ and the bias is **upward**. OLS will
**understate how effective police are**, and could even show a positive "effect" of police on murder.
[@wooldridge_intro]

## Identification: shift one curve, trace the other

Wooldridge's milk market makes the idea visual. [@wooldridge_intro] Let quantity and price of milk be
determined by

- **supply**, which depends on price and on the **price of cattle feed** (a cost shifter), and
- **demand**, which depends on price and nothing else that we observe.

Which equation can be estimated? **Demand.** The feed price moves the supply curve up and down while,
by assumption, leaving demand untouched. Each movement slides the equilibrium along the fixed demand
curve. With enough variation in feed prices, the equilibria trace out demand, and feed price serves as the
instrument for price in the demand equation. [@wooldridge_intro]

Supply, by contrast, **cannot** be estimated: nothing observed shifts demand, and the unobserved demand
shocks do not help, because *"we need something observed."* [@wooldridge_intro] Add an observed demand
shifter (income, say) and supply becomes identified too. The summary is worth memorising:
**it is an exogenous variable in the supply equation that lets us estimate the demand equation.**
[@wooldridge_intro]

```{python}
#| label: fig-sem-identification
#| fig-cap: "A simulated milk market (our own illustration of Wooldridge's Figure 16.1). Left: equilibria when only unobserved shocks move the two curves. The true demand curve (black) slopes down, but OLS through the cloud (grey) slopes up, because supply shocks and demand shocks are both in the data. Right: the same market with an observed cost shifter. Each band of feed prices (shades of grey) moves supply, so the band averages slide along the fixed demand curve. The IV estimate (orange) that uses only feed-price variation recovers the demand slope."
#| code-fold: true
#| code-summary: "Show the figure code"

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(16)
n = 600
a_d, b_d = -1.0, 20.0          # demand:  q = 20 - 1.0 p + u_d
a_s, c_s = 1.5, -4.0           # supply:  q = -4 + 1.5 p - 2.0 z + u_s

def equilibria(z, sd_d, sd_s):
    u_d = rng.normal(0, sd_d, n)
    u_s = rng.normal(0, sd_s, n)
    # solve b_d + a_d p + u_d = c_s + a_s p - 2 z + u_s for p
    p = (b_d - c_s + u_d - u_s + 2.0 * z) / (a_s - a_d)
    q = b_d + a_d * p + u_d
    return p, q

def ols_slope(x, y):
    xc = x - x.mean()
    return (xc * (y - y.mean())).sum() / (xc ** 2).sum()

# Left: no observed shifter, demand shocks larger than supply shocks
p0, q0 = equilibria(np.zeros(n), sd_d=2.0, sd_s=1.0)
b_ols0 = ols_slope(p0, q0)

# Right: an observed cost shifter z (feed price) moves supply
z = rng.uniform(0, 4, n)
p1, q1 = equilibria(z, sd_d=1.0, sd_s=1.0)
zc = z - z.mean()
b_iv = (zc * (q1 - q1.mean())).sum() / (zc * (p1 - p1.mean())).sum()   # Wald/IV slope
b_ols1 = ols_slope(p1, q1)

fig, (ax1, ax2) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(9.8, 4.3), sharey=True)
grid = np.linspace(min(p0.min(), p1.min()) - 0.3, max(p0.max(), p1.max()) + 0.3, 50)

ax1.scatter(p0, q0, s=9, color="#b7b6a8", alpha=0.7, label="observed equilibria")
ax1.plot(grid, b_d + a_d * grid, color="#161613", lw=2.2, label=f"true demand (slope {a_d:.1f})")
ax1.plot(grid, q0.mean() + b_ols0 * (grid - p0.mean()), color="#5e5e54", lw=1.8, ls="--",
         label=f"OLS of q on p (slope {b_ols0:+.2f})")
ax1.set_xlabel("price")
ax1.set_ylabel("quantity")
ax1.set_title("Only unobserved shocks: OLS estimates neither curve")
ax1.legend(fontsize=8, frameon=False, loc="lower left")

shades = ["#b7b6a8", "#8a8a7e", "#5e5e54"]
bands = np.digitize(z, [4 / 3, 8 / 3])
for k in range(3):
    m = bands == k
    ax2.scatter(p1[m], q1[m], s=9, color=shades[k], alpha=0.75)
    ax2.plot(p1[m].mean(), q1[m].mean(), "D", color="#161613", ms=7, zorder=5)
ax2.plot(grid, b_d + a_d * grid, color="#161613", lw=2.2, label="true demand")
ax2.plot(grid, q1.mean() + b_iv * (grid - p1.mean()), color="#e8531d", lw=2.4,
         label=f"IV using feed price (slope {b_iv:+.2f})")
ax2.plot(grid, q1.mean() + b_ols1 * (grid - p1.mean()), color="#5e5e54", lw=1.6, ls="--",
         label=f"OLS (slope {b_ols1:+.2f})")
ax2.set_xlabel("price")
ax2.set_title("A supply shifter traces out demand")
ax2.legend(fontsize=8, frameon=False, loc="lower left")
ax2.annotate("band means\n(low → high feed price)", (p1[bands == 2].mean(), q1[bands == 2].mean()),
             xytext=(p1.max() - 2.2, q1.max() - 0.6), fontsize=8, color="#5e5e54")

fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

In the left panel the OLS line is not a biased estimate of demand in any useful sense. It is a blend of
demand slope and supply slope, weighted by which curve happened to be shaken harder. Make demand shocks
smaller than supply shocks and the OLS line tilts towards demand; make them larger and it tilts towards
supply. Only the right panel, where an observed variable moves just one curve, gives an answer that does
not depend on the relative size of the shocks.

### The rank and order conditions

For a general two-equation system, the rules are: [@wooldridge_intro]

- **Rank condition (necessary and sufficient).** The first equation is identified *if and only if* the
  second equation contains at least one exogenous variable, with a **nonzero coefficient**, that is
  excluded from the first.
- **Order condition (necessary only).** At least one exogenous variable is excluded from the equation.
  This is a matter of counting and is trivial to check once both equations are written down.

The difference matters. The order condition only asks whether you *wrote* an excluded variable into the
other equation. The rank condition asks whether it *really* belongs there: whether it actually moves the
endogenous regressor. That part is testable, with a $t$ or $F$ test on the excluded variables in the
reduced form, exactly the [first-stage](instrumental-variables.qmd) check of IV. [@wooldridge_intro]

The **exclusion restrictions** are what make identification possible, and they are assumptions about the
world. Saying that the cattle-feed price does not enter milk demand, or that past experience does not affect
how many hours a woman wants to work once her wage is known, is a substantive claim. You defend it with
economic reasoning; you cannot test it. [@wooldridge_intro]

## Worked example 1: labour supply of married women

Wooldridge's Example 16.3/16.5 uses the MROZ data on 428 married women in the workforce. [@wooldridge_intro]

- **Labour supply:** hours depend on log wage, education, age, number of children under six, and non-wage
  income (which includes the husband's earnings).
- **Wage offer:** log wage depends on hours, education, experience and experience squared.

The supply equation is identified because **experience and its square are excluded** from it but appear in
the wage equation. That is an assumption: once the wage is controlled for, past experience is taken to have
no direct effect on how much a woman wants to work. The wage equation in turn is identified by **age, young
children and non-wage income**, which are assumed not to affect the wage offered. Wooldridge notes that
this would be wrong if employers discriminated by age or by number of small children. [@wooldridge_intro]

Estimated by 2SLS with all six exogenous variables as instruments: [@wooldridge_intro]

| Estimate of the wage effect on hours | Coefficient on log(wage) | SE |
|---|---|---|
| OLS | −2.05 | 54.88 |
| **2SLS** | **1,639.56** | **470.58** |

OLS says wages have **no effect** on hours. 2SLS says the supply curve slopes clearly upward. Because hours
are in levels and the wage in logs, the elasticity is $1{,}640/\text{hours}$: about **1.26** at the average
of 1,303 hours, and over 2 for someone working 800 hours. That is a large elasticity. [@wooldridge_intro]
A regression-based endogeneity test (add the reduced-form residual to the OLS equation) gives a $t$
statistic of 6.61 in absolute value, so log(wage) does appear endogenous. [@wooldridge_intro] In the
other direction, the 2SLS wage-offer equation finds **no** effect of hours on the offered wage
(.00013, SE .00025). [@wooldridge_intro]

Wooldridge also flags the limits. The population is women who already work, because a wage offer is
unobserved for those who do not; including them is the [sample-selection
problem](../03-regression/censoring-truncation-and-selection.qmd) of Chapter 17. And education appears in
*both* equations: if omitted ability sits in the error, education is endogenous too. *"The distinction
between omitted variables and simultaneity in applications is not always sharp."* [@wooldridge_intro]

## Worked example 2: is openness good for inflation?

Romer (1993) argued that more open economies should have lower inflation. Wooldridge sets out the
system Romer has in mind: [@wooldridge_intro]

- **Inflation** depends on openness (import share of GDP) and log per-capita income.
- **Openness** depends on inflation, log per-capita income and **log land area**: smaller countries tend
  to trade more.

Land area is excluded from the inflation equation, so the inflation equation is identified *provided land
area really matters for openness*. The openness equation is **not** identified, since it contains both
exogenous variables. We do not need it. [@wooldridge_intro]

Check the rank condition first. In the reduced form for openness (114 countries, $R^2 = .449$), log land
area has a $t$ statistic **over nine** in absolute value: smaller countries are indeed more open. Log
income is insignificant there, and Wooldridge stresses that this is *"irrelevant."* [@wooldridge_intro]
Then the IV estimate of openness on inflation is **−.337** (SE .144) against an OLS estimate of
**−.215** (SE .095). Each extra percentage point of import share goes with about **a third of a
percentage point** lower annual inflation. [@wooldridge_intro]

## Bigger systems: counting is not enough

With three or more equations, proving identification needs matrix algebra, but *failure* is often easy to
spot. [@wooldridge_intro] Wooldridge's three-equation example has an equation that contains **every**
exogenous variable in the system, which leaves no instruments for its endogenous regressor. That equation
is **unidentified**. The general **order condition** is: [@wooldridge_intro]

> the number of exogenous variables excluded from the equation must be at least the number of endogenous
> variables on its right-hand side.

The terms *just identified* and *overidentified*, familiar from [IV](instrumental-variables.qmd),
were born here. An equation with two endogenous regressors and three excluded exogenous variables has one
**overidentifying restriction**, which can be tested. [@wooldridge_intro]

The subtle point is that the order condition can be satisfied on paper and still fail in fact. In
Wooldridge's system, the second equation's only excluded variable, $z_4$, appears solely in the third
equation. If its coefficient there is actually zero, $z_4$ is unrelated to every endogenous variable, and
the second equation is not identified after all. **Identification of one equation depends on the
parameter values in other equations**, which we can never know for sure. [@wooldridge_intro]

Any identified equation, in any size of system, can be estimated by 2SLS, using as instruments all the
exogenous variables that appear anywhere in the system. System methods such as **three-stage least
squares** are more efficient when the whole system is correctly specified, but Wooldridge leaves them
beyond the scope of the book. [@wooldridge_intro]

## SEMs with time series: the rise and fall of macro models

Large simultaneous systems were among the first econometric models, built to describe whole economies.
Wooldridge's Keynesian example has a consumption function, an investment function and the national-income
identity $Y = C + I + G$. The identity holds by definition, so it is never estimated, but it makes income
endogenous. [@wooldridge_intro] He gives three reasons such models are *"seldom estimated now"*:
[@wooldridge_intro]

1. **Exogeneity is hard to believe at the aggregate level.** Taxes depend on income; government spending
   responds to the economy; interest rates are set with an eye on output. Add a money market to make
   interest rates endogenous, and there are no longer enough exogenous variables to identify the equations.
2. **They were static.** Adding lags helps. A lagged endogenous variable is called **predetermined**, and
   if the errors are uncorrelated with the past it can act as its own instrument.
3. **The series are persistent.** Aggregate consumption, income and investment look like they have [unit
   roots](../04-predictive/unit-roots-and-stationarity.qmd), which breaks the weak-dependence
   assumptions standard OLS and 2SLS inference relies on. Specifying the system in growth rates helps, but
   that is a *different* model from one in levels.

**Worked example 3: the permanent income hypothesis.** Campbell and Mankiw (1990) tested whether
consumption growth responds to predictable income growth, which the pure PIH says it should not.
[@wooldridge_intro] Under the PIH the error is unpredictable from anything known last year, so **lagged**
consumption growth, income growth and real interest rates are valid instruments. Using annual US data for
1959–1995 ($n = 35$), Wooldridge's 2SLS estimate of the income-growth coefficient is **.586** (SE .135,
$t = 4.34$): a 1% rise in disposable income raises consumption by over half a percent. The real-interest
coefficient is tiny and insignificant. The pure PIH is strongly rejected, as Campbell and Mankiw found.
[@wooldridge_intro]

## SEMs with panel data: differencing first, then instruments

Panel data let you remove a fixed unobserved effect from each equation, a taste for leisure, say, while
still dealing with simultaneity. The recipe is two steps: **difference or time-demean** to remove the
[fixed effect](panel-and-fixed-effects.qmd), then **find instruments** for the endogenous variable in the
transformed equation. [@wooldridge_intro]

The catch is that the instruments must **change over time**, and differently for different units.
Wooldridge's warning case: in a differenced labour-supply equation for people who work every year,
experience rises by exactly one for everyone, every year. Its change is a constant and useless as an
instrument. [@wooldridge_intro]

**Worked example 4: prisons and violent crime.** Levitt (1996) wanted the effect of prison population on
crime, but crime rates and prison populations drive each other. His instrument was **prison-overcrowding
litigation**: whether a final court decision on overcrowding had been reached in the current year or the
previous two. Such rulings force prison populations down for reasons unrelated to that year's crime.
[@wooldridge_intro] On Wooldridge's subset of Levitt's data (51 units × 14 years, 1980–1993, 714
observations) for the change in log violent crime: [@wooldridge_intro]

| Estimator | Effect of log prison population | SE |
|---|---|---|
| Pooled OLS, first differences | −.181 | .048 |
| **Pooled 2SLS**, litigation instruments | **−1.032** | **.370** |

The 2SLS effect is much larger and, as usual, much less precise. Levitt found similar results with a longer
period and more instruments. [@wooldridge_intro] The direction of the gap is what the simultaneity story
predicts: rising crime pushes prison populations up, which masks the crime-reducing effect of
incarceration in OLS.

## Common pitfalls

::: {.callout-warning title="Where this goes wrong"}

- **Writing an SEM for choices made by one agent.** Housing and saving, study hours and work hours: if the
  same person chooses both, neither equation has a ceteris paribus meaning. Model each outcome as a function
  of the exogenous variables instead. [@wooldridge_intro]

- **Reading a regression through equilibrium points as a demand (or supply) curve.** It is neither. Its
  slope depends on which curve was shocked harder, and the sign can be wrong, as with police "raising"
  murder. [@wooldridge_intro]

- **Satisfying the order condition and calling it identification.** Counting excluded variables is
  necessary, not sufficient. Check in the reduced form that the excluded variables actually move the
  endogenous regressor. Land area's $t > 9$ is the good case. [@wooldridge_intro]

- **Treating exclusion restrictions as facts.** "Experience does not affect desired hours" and "age does not
  affect the wage offer" are assumptions, and Wooldridge points out how each could fail. Defend them with
  economics; no test will certify them. [@wooldridge_intro]

- **Assuming macro aggregates are exogenous.** Taxes, government spending and interest rates respond to the
  economy. Old-style aggregate SEMs fail here first. [@wooldridge_intro]

- **Using an instrument that does not vary after differencing.** In panel SEMs, an instrument whose change
  is the same for everyone (like a year of experience) carries no information. [@wooldridge_intro]

:::

## Appendix · the mathematics {.unnumbered}

::: {.math-appendix}

**The reduced form.** With intercepts suppressed, take
$y_1 = \alpha_1 y_2 + \beta_1 z_1 + u_1$ and $y_2 = \alpha_2 y_1 + \beta_2 z_2 + u_2$. Substituting the
first into the second gives $(1-\alpha_2\alpha_1)\,y_2 = \alpha_2\beta_1 z_1 + \beta_2 z_2 + \alpha_2 u_1 +
u_2$. If $\alpha_1\alpha_2 \ne 1$:

$$
y_2 = \pi_{21} z_1 + \pi_{22} z_2 + v_2, \qquad
\pi_{21} = \frac{\alpha_2\beta_1}{1-\alpha_1\alpha_2},\;
\pi_{22} = \frac{\beta_2}{1-\alpha_1\alpha_2},\;
v_2 = \frac{\alpha_2 u_1 + u_2}{1-\alpha_1\alpha_2}.
$$

The reduced-form parameters are nonlinear functions of the structural ones. Because $v_2$ is a combination
of the structural errors, it is uncorrelated with $z_1, z_2$, so the reduced form can be estimated
consistently by OLS. That is the first stage of 2SLS. [@wooldridge_intro]

**The sign of the bias.** Drop $z_1$ and assume $\operatorname{Cov}(u_1,u_2)=0$. Then

$$
\operatorname{Cov}(y_2,u_1) = \operatorname{Cov}(v_2,u_1) = \frac{\alpha_2}{1-\alpha_1\alpha_2}\,\sigma_1^2 ,
$$

so the inconsistency in the OLS estimate of $\alpha_1$ has the sign of $\alpha_2/(1-\alpha_1\alpha_2)$.
[@wooldridge_intro] (Our addition: in this one-regressor case,
$\operatorname{plim}\hat\alpha_1^{\text{OLS}} = \alpha_1 + \operatorname{Cov}(y_2,u_1)/\operatorname{Var}(y_2)$,
the same omitted-variable-style formula as in [regression and
causality](../03-regression/regression-and-causality.qmd).)

**Why the IV slope works in the figure.** In the milk market, demand is $q = \beta_d + \alpha_d p + u_d$
and the feed price $z$ enters only supply. If $\operatorname{Cov}(z,u_d)=0$,

$$
\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(z,q)}{\operatorname{Cov}(z,p)} = \frac{\alpha_d\operatorname{Cov}(z,p) + \operatorname{Cov}(z,u_d)}{\operatorname{Cov}(z,p)} = \alpha_d ,
$$

provided $\operatorname{Cov}(z,p)\ne 0$, which is the rank condition in this setting. The supply slope
never appears, which is why the right panel's orange line lands on demand whatever the supply curve looks
like.

**Counting restrictions.** For an equation with $G_1$ right-hand-side endogenous variables and $K$ total
exogenous variables in the system, of which $K_1$ appear in the equation, the order condition is
$K - K_1 \ge G_1$. The number of overidentifying restrictions is the total number of exogenous variables
in the system minus the total number of explanatory variables in the equation, $K - (K_1 + G_1)$.
[@wooldridge_intro] The rank condition for a general system requires matrix algebra (Wooldridge points to
his graduate text, Chapter 9).

**Panel SEMs.** With unobserved effects $a_{i1}$, the equation
$y_{it1} = \alpha_1 y_{it2} + z_{it1}\beta_1 + a_{i1} + u_{it1}$ is first-differenced to
$\Delta y_{it1} = \alpha_1\Delta y_{it2} + \Delta z_{it1}\beta_1 + \Delta u_{it1}$ and estimated by pooled
2SLS with the time-varying elements of $z_{it2}$ excluded from $z_{it1}$ as instruments. Time-demeaning
plus pooled 2SLS is equivalent to 2SLS with unit dummies acting as their own instruments; if the demeaned
equation is estimated directly, the degrees of freedom must be corrected to $N(T-1) - k_1$.
[@wooldridge_intro]

:::
