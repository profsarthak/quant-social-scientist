Correlated Random Effects & Hausman–Taylor

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source

Fixed effects buys consistency by throwing away everything that never changes. Two devices buy some of it back — a way to test the fixed-vs-random choice, and a way to estimate the effect of a trait that is constant within a unit.

Fixed effects sweeps away every time-invariant confounder — and, in the same stroke, every time-invariant variable you wanted to measure. Gender, race, a person’s schooling, a firm’s founding cohort: all differenced to zero. This entry is about the escape hatch. Mundlak shows that random effects, augmented with the time-averages of the regressors, reproduces fixed effects exactly — turning the fixed-vs-random choice into a single testable coefficient. Hausman–Taylor goes further: using internal instruments, it estimates the effect of a time-invariant regressor even when some of your variables are correlated with the unit effect.

Why it matters

The panel & fixed-effects entry ends on a hard limit, stated three times: fixed effects (FE) cannot estimate the effect of anything that is constant within a unit, because the within transformation demeans it to zero. (Li 2023) That is often exactly the coefficient a social scientist wants — the return to years of schooling, the wage penalty of a demographic category, the effect of a country’s legal origin. Random effects (RE) can estimate those, but only by assuming the unit effect is uncorrelated with every regressor — the assumption you reached for panel data precisely to avoid. (Li 2023)

So the applied researcher is caught between an estimator that is safe but blind and one that can see but is usually biased. The two devices here — the Mundlak / correlated-random-effects reformulation and the Hausman–Taylor estimator — are the standard middle path, and they clarify what the Hausman test is really doing. (Li 2023)

The dilemma, stated precisely

Write the panel model with the time-invariant regressors split out explicitly: y_{it} = X_{it}'\beta + Z_i'\gamma + \mu_i + \nu_{it}, where X_{it} varies over time (experience, tenure), Z_i is constant within a unit (schooling, sex), and \mu_i is the unobserved unit effect. (Li 2023) The trouble is a suspicion that \mu_i is correlated with some regressors — the ability that raises both schooling Z_i and wages y.

  • RE assumes \operatorname{Cov}(\mu_i,\,X_{it})=\operatorname{Cov}(\mu_i,\,Z_i)=0. Under it, RE is efficient and estimates both \beta and \gamma. Violate it and both are inconsistent. (Li 2023)
  • FE (the within estimator) is consistent for \beta whatever \mu_i does — but Z_i is annihilated, so \gamma is simply not identified. (Li 2023)

The all-or-nothing choice is the problem. Mundlak dissolves it conceptually; Hausman–Taylor dissolves it in practice.

Mundlak’s device: random effects that contains fixed effects

Mundlak’s (1978) move is to stop pretending \mu_i is unrelated to the regressors and instead model the relationship. (Li 2023) Posit an auxiliary regression of the unit effect on the time-averages of the time-varying regressors: \mu_i = \bar X_i'\,\pi + \varepsilon_i, \qquad \varepsilon_i \perp X_{it}. Substituting this back and estimating the augmented model — random effects with each regressor’s unit-mean \bar X_i added as an extra control — produces something remarkable: the slope on X_{it} comes out numerically equal to the fixed-effects (within) estimate, \hat\beta_{\text{GLS}}=\tilde\beta_{\text{within}}. (Li 2023) The correlation between \mu_i and the regressors, once written down as \pi, is absorbed by the group means, and what is left is the clean within variation.

This reframes the whole fixed-vs-random debate:

  • \pi = 0 means the unit effect is not related to the regressors — the RE assumption holds, and RE and FE agree. The Hausman test is, exactly, a test of H_0:\pi=0. (Li 2023)
  • \pi \ne 0 means they are related — and the augmented model has already handled it, delivering the FE slope from inside a random-effects (likelihood) framework.

That last point is why the device (now usually called correlated random effects, CRE) is more than a curiosity: it carries the FE logic into places where the within transformation cannot go — notably nonlinear panels (logit, probit, Tobit), where there is no demeaning trick and adding the group means is the practical route to consistency. (Li 2023)

Hausman–Taylor: estimating what fixed effects deletes

Mundlak recovers \beta; it still cannot hand you \gamma, the coefficient on the time-invariant Z_i. Hausman & Taylor (1981) target exactly that, replacing the all-or-nothing correlation assumption with a partition: (Li 2023)

X = [\,\underbrace{X_1}_{\text{exogenous}}\ \underbrace{X_2}_{\text{endogenous}}\,], \qquad Z = [\,\underbrace{Z_1}_{\text{exogenous}}\ \underbrace{Z_2}_{\text{endogenous}}\,].

X_1, Z_1 are uncorrelated with \mu_i; X_2, Z_2 are correlated with it. The insight is that a panel supplies its own instruments — no outside variable required: (Li 2023)

  1. The within-transformed exogenous and endogenous time-varying regressors (QX_1, QX_2) instrument X — the within transformation has already purged \mu_i from them.
  2. The exogenous time-invariant Z_1 instruments itself.
  3. Crucially, the time-averages of the exogenous time-varying regressors \bar X_1 instrument the endogenous time-invariant Z_2. Because X_1 is uncorrelated with \mu_i, its unit mean \bar X_1 is a valid instrument; because it is correlated with Z_2, it is a relevant one. (Li 2023)

That third step is the whole trick: it uses the exogenous variation in the panel to isolate the part of Z_2 that is clean of the unit effect, and so estimates \gamma without assuming Z_2 is exogenous. The price is an order condition — you need at least as many exogenous time-varying regressors as endogenous time-invariant ones, k_1 \ge g_2 — and, of course, the honesty of your split into exogenous and endogenous. (Li 2023) Amemiya–MaCurdy (1986) and Breusch–Mizon–Schmidt (1989) sharpen the instrument set for efficiency under stronger exogeneity. (Li 2023)

The figure makes the payoff concrete: for a time-invariant coefficient \gamma, the naïve levels regression is biased, fixed effects cannot estimate it at all, and Hausman–Taylor lands on the truth.

Show the figure code (fixed effects, Mundlak means, and Hausman–Taylor 2SLS, all by hand) 
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(11)
N, T, n_sim = 300, 5, 200
b1, b2, g1, g2 = 0.5, 0.5, 0.5, 1.0     # g2 = target: the time-invariant, endogenous coefficient

def within(M):                          # demean each unit (rows = units, cols = time)
    return M - M.mean(1, keepdims=True)

ols_g2, ht_g2 = [], []
for _ in range(n_sim):
    a  = rng.normal(0, 1, N)                          # unit effect  mu_i (alpha)
    w  = rng.normal(0, 1, N)                          # a clean channel, independent of a
    Z2 = 0.8 * a + 0.8 * w                            # time-invariant, ENDOGENOUS (corr with mu via a)
    Z1 = rng.normal(0, 1, N)                          # time-invariant, exogenous
    X1 = 0.9 * w[:, None] + rng.normal(0, 1, (N, T))  # time-varying, EXOGENOUS (⊥ mu), corr w/ Z2 via w
    X2 = 0.8 * a[:, None] + rng.normal(0, 1, (N, T))  # time-varying, endogenous (corr with mu)
    y  = b1*X1 + b2*X2 + (g1*Z1 + g2*Z2 + a)[:, None] + rng.normal(0, 1, (N, T))

    # (1) pooled OLS on the levels — tries to estimate g2, inconsistent (Z2 tied to mu)
    Xp = np.column_stack([np.ones(N*T), X1.ravel(), X2.ravel(),
                          np.repeat(Z1, T), np.repeat(Z2, T)])
    ols_g2.append(np.linalg.lstsq(Xp, y.ravel(), rcond=None)[0][4])

    # (3) Hausman–Taylor
    #   step 1: within (FE) slopes on the time-varying regressors — consistent despite X2 endogeneity
    Xw = np.column_stack([within(X1).ravel(), within(X2).ravel()])
    bw = np.linalg.lstsq(Xw, within(y).ravel(), rcond=None)[0]
    #   step 2: one between-unit residual per unit
    d  = y.mean(1) - bw[0]*X1.mean(1) - bw[1]*X2.mean(1)
    #   step 3: 2SLS of d on [1, Z1, Z2] with instruments [1, Z1, mean(X1)]  (X1 exogenous ⊥ mu)
    Zmat = np.column_stack([np.ones(N), Z1, Z2])
    A    = np.column_stack([np.ones(N), Z1, X1.mean(1)])
    PAZ  = A @ np.linalg.solve(A.T @ A, A.T @ Zmat)   # project the regressors onto the instruments
    gamma = np.linalg.solve(PAZ.T @ Zmat, PAZ.T @ d)
    ht_g2.append(gamma[2])

ols_g2, ht_g2 = np.array(ols_g2), np.array(ht_g2)

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7.2, 4.2))
ax.bar(0, ols_g2.mean(), yerr=ols_g2.std(), width=0.6, color="#8a8a7e",
       ecolor="#161613", capsize=4)
ax.bar(2, ht_g2.mean(),  yerr=ht_g2.std(),  width=0.6, color="#e8531d",
       ecolor="#161613", capsize=4)
ax.axhline(g2, color="#161613", ls="--", lw=1.8)
ax.text(2.44, g2, f"  true γ = {g2:.1f}", va="center", ha="left", fontsize=9, color="#161613")
ax.plot([0.72, 1.28], [0, 0], color="#5e5e54", lw=1.4)
ax.text(1, 0.42, "not identified\n(Z differenced out)", ha="center", va="center",
        fontsize=9, color="#5e5e54", style="italic")
ax.set_xticks([0, 1, 2])
ax.set_xticklabels(["OLS / RE\n(levels)", "fixed effects", "Hausman–Taylor"])
ax.set_ylabel("estimated γ on the\ntime-invariant regressor")
ax.set_title("Recovering an effect that never changes within a unit")
ax.set_ylim(0, max(ols_g2.mean() + ols_g2.std(), g2) * 1.28)
fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()

The ladder of assumptions

The four estimators form a single ladder, trading robustness for reach: (Li 2023)

  • Pooled OLS — assumes \mu_i is pure noise, uncorrelated with everything. Estimates all coefficients; inconsistent the moment a regressor correlates with \mu_i.
  • Random effects — same exogeneity assumption on \mu_i, but efficient; still inconsistent under correlation.
  • Correlated random effects (Mundlak) — lets \mu_i depend on the regressors’ means; recovers the FE slope for \beta and, if Z_i is exogenous, \gamma too; its coefficient \pi is the Hausman test.
  • Hausman–Taylor — allows a declared subset of regressors to be endogenous and still estimates \gamma, via internal instruments.
  • Fixed effects — assumes nothing about \mu_i’s correlations; consistent for \beta, but silent on any Z_i.

Moving down the ladder assumes less and less about the unit effect; moving up estimates more and more. Mundlak and Hausman–Taylor are the rungs in the middle that most applied work actually stands on.

Common pitfalls

WarningWhere this goes wrong

  • Reading a Hausman rejection as “FE is right” rather than “RE is inconsistent.” The test says the two estimators disagree; via Mundlak it is literally \pi\ne0. That indicts RE’s exogeneity assumption — it does not certify that your FE specification is well-behaved. (Li 2023)

  • Trusting Hausman–Taylor’s split without defending it. The whole estimator rests on your declaration of which regressors are exogenous. Misclassify an endogenous variable as exogenous and you have simply invented an invalid instrument — the bias returns, now hidden. (Li 2023)

  • Weak internal instruments. \bar X_1 instruments Z_2 only if the exogenous time-varying regressor is genuinely correlated with the time-invariant one. If it barely is, \gamma is estimated with the same imprecision (and finite-sample bias) as any weak-IV problem. (Li 2023)

  • Forgetting the order condition. You need at least as many exogenous time-varying regressors as endogenous time-invariant ones (k_1 \ge g_2); short of that, \gamma is under-identified and Hausman–Taylor collapses back to the within estimator. (Li 2023)

  • Reaching for CRE/HT when FE would do. If you do not need \gamma and are content to bracket confounders, plain fixed effects is simpler and assumes less. These devices earn their keep only when a time-invariant effect is the estimand — or the model is nonlinear. (Li 2023)

From the sources

NoteWho says what
  • Dong Li, Panel Data Econometrics — the split model y_{it}=X_{it}'\beta+Z_i'\gamma+\mu_i+\nu_{it} and the endogeneity of the unit effect; Mundlak’s (1978) auxiliary regression \mu_i=\bar X_i'\pi+\varepsilon_i, the result that GLS on the mean-augmented model returns the within estimator (\hat\beta_{\text{GLS}}=\tilde\beta_{\text{within}}), and that the Hausman test is a test of H_0:\pi=0; Hausman & Taylor (1981) — the exogenous/endogenous × time-varying/time-invariant partition, the internal instrument set A_1=[QX_1, QX_2, Z_1, \bar X_1], the order condition k_1\ge g_2, and the three identification cases (under-, just-, and over-identified); the Amemiya–MaCurdy (1986) and Breusch–Mizon–Schmidt (1989) more-efficient instrument sets; and the reading of correlated random effects as the route to consistency in nonlinear panels.

Appendix · the mathematics

Mundlak: why the means deliver the within slope. Stack Mundlak’s auxiliary regression as \mu = P X \pi/T + \varepsilon where P=I_N\otimes \bar J_T averages over time. The model becomes y = X\beta + (PX)\pi + (Z_\mu\varepsilon + \nu), with the new composite error having variance V=\sigma^2_\varepsilon(I_N\otimes J_T)+\sigma^2_\nu I_{NT}. Using the partitioned inverse, GLS on this augmented regression yields \hat\beta_{\text{GLS}} = (X'QX)^{-1}X'Qy = \tilde\beta_{\text{within}}, \qquad \hat\pi_{\text{GLS}} = \hat\beta_{\text{between}} - \tilde\beta_{\text{within}}, where Q=I_{NT}-P is the within (deviation-from-means) operator. So the augmented random-effects model returns the fixed-effects slope, and \hat\pi is exactly the between-minus-within gap that the Hausman statistic measures — H_0:\pi=0 is H_0:\operatorname{Cov}(\mu_i,X_{it})=0.

Hausman–Taylor in three steps. (i) The within estimator gives \tilde\beta_W, consistent for \beta regardless of the endogeneity of X_2, since Q annihilates \mu_i. (ii) Form the between-unit residual \hat d_i=\bar y_i-\bar X_i'\tilde\beta_W, which equals Z_i'\gamma+\mu_i+\bar\nu_i up to sampling error. (iii) Run 2SLS of \hat d_i on Z=[Z_1,Z_2] using instruments A=[Z_1,\bar X_1]: \hat\gamma_{\text{2SLS}} = (Z'P_A Z)^{-1} Z'P_A \hat d, \qquad P_A = A(A'A)^{-1}A'. \bar X_1 is a valid instrument because \operatorname{Cov}(\bar X_1,\mu_i)=0 (the X_1 are exogenous), and a relevant one because \operatorname{Cov}(\bar X_1,Z_2)\ne0. The matrix Z'P_AZ is invertible iff k_1\ge g_2 — the order condition. The full-efficiency HT estimator then re-runs the whole system on \hat\Omega^{-1/2}- transformed data with the same instruments; the figure uses the transparent three-step version, which is consistent and shows the identification cleanly.

Connections

  • Builds on: panel data & fixed effects (the within estimator and the time-invariant-regressor limit this resolves), random effects and the Hausman test (reframed here as \pi=0), and instrumental variables (Hausman–Taylor is IV with internal instruments; the same relevance/validity logic).
  • Relates to: dynamic panels & GMM (Arellano–Bond also instruments with the panel’s own lags — the same “internal instruments” idea in the dynamic case) and mixed-effects / hierarchical models (random effects as a modelling choice, where correlated random effects is the endogeneity-robust cousin).
  • Cautioned by: weak instruments — internal instruments can be weak, and a misdeclared exogenous variable is an invalid one.

sex), and $\mu_i$ is the unobserved unit effect. [@dongli_panel] The trouble is a suspicion that $\mu_i$ is
**correlated with some regressors** — the ability that raises both schooling $Z_i$ and wages $y$.

- **RE** assumes $\operatorname{Cov}(\mu_i,\,X_{it})=\operatorname{Cov}(\mu_i,\,Z_i)=0$. Under it, RE is
  efficient and estimates *both* $\beta$ and $\gamma$. Violate it and **both are inconsistent.**
  [@dongli_panel]
- **FE** (the within estimator) is consistent for $\beta$ whatever $\mu_i$ does — but $Z_i$ is annihilated,
  so **$\gamma$ is simply not identified.** [@dongli_panel]

The all-or-nothing choice is the problem. Mundlak dissolves it conceptually; Hausman–Taylor dissolves it in
practice.

## Mundlak's device: random effects that *contains* fixed effects

Mundlak's (1978) move is to stop pretending $\mu_i$ is unrelated to the regressors and instead **model the
relationship**. [@dongli_panel] Posit an auxiliary regression of the unit effect on the **time-averages** of
the time-varying regressors:
$$
\mu_i = \bar X_i'\,\pi + \varepsilon_i, \qquad \varepsilon_i \perp X_{it}.
$$
Substituting this back and estimating the augmented model — random effects with **each regressor's unit-mean
$\bar X_i$ added as an extra control** — produces something remarkable: the slope on $X_{it}$ comes out
**numerically equal to the fixed-effects (within) estimate**, $\hat\beta_{\text{GLS}}=\tilde\beta_{\text{within}}$.
[@dongli_panel] The correlation between $\mu_i$ and the regressors, once written down as $\pi$, is absorbed by
the group means, and what is left is the clean within variation.

This reframes the whole [fixed-vs-random debate](panel-and-fixed-effects.qmd):

- $\pi = 0$ means the unit effect is *not* related to the regressors — the RE assumption holds, and RE and FE
  agree. The **Hausman test is, exactly, a test of $H_0:\pi=0$.** [@dongli_panel]
- $\pi \ne 0$ means they are related — and the augmented model has already handled it, delivering the FE slope
  from inside a random-effects (likelihood) framework.

That last point is why the device (now usually called **correlated random effects**, CRE) is more than a
curiosity: it carries the FE logic into places where the within transformation cannot go — notably
**nonlinear panels** (logit, probit, Tobit), where there is no demeaning trick and adding the group means is
the practical route to consistency. [@dongli_panel]

## Hausman–Taylor: estimating what fixed effects deletes

Mundlak recovers $\beta$; it still cannot hand you $\gamma$, the coefficient on the time-invariant $Z_i$.
**Hausman & Taylor (1981)** target exactly that, replacing the all-or-nothing correlation assumption with a
**partition**: [@dongli_panel]

$$
X = [\,\underbrace{X_1}_{\text{exogenous}}\ \underbrace{X_2}_{\text{endogenous}}\,],
\qquad
Z = [\,\underbrace{Z_1}_{\text{exogenous}}\ \underbrace{Z_2}_{\text{endogenous}}\,].
$$

$X_1, Z_1$ are uncorrelated with $\mu_i$; $X_2, Z_2$ are correlated with it. The insight is that a panel
supplies its **own instruments** — no outside variable required: [@dongli_panel]

1. The **within-transformed** exogenous *and* endogenous time-varying regressors ($QX_1, QX_2$) instrument
   $X$ — the within transformation has already purged $\mu_i$ from them.
2. The exogenous time-invariant $Z_1$ instruments itself.
3. Crucially, the **time-averages of the exogenous time-varying regressors** $\bar X_1$ instrument the
   *endogenous time-invariant* $Z_2$. Because $X_1$ is uncorrelated with $\mu_i$, its unit mean $\bar X_1$ is a
   **valid** instrument; because it is correlated with $Z_2$, it is a **relevant** one. [@dongli_panel]

That third step is the whole trick: it uses the *exogenous* variation in the panel to isolate the part of
$Z_2$ that is clean of the unit effect, and so estimates $\gamma$ **without assuming $Z_2$ is exogenous**.
The price is an **order condition** — you need at least as many exogenous time-varying regressors as
endogenous time-invariant ones, $k_1 \ge g_2$ — and, of course, the honesty of your split into exogenous and
endogenous. [@dongli_panel] Amemiya–MaCurdy (1986) and Breusch–Mizon–Schmidt (1989) sharpen the instrument
set for efficiency under stronger exogeneity. [@dongli_panel]

The figure makes the payoff concrete: for a time-invariant coefficient $\gamma$, the naïve levels regression
is biased, fixed effects cannot estimate it at all, and Hausman–Taylor lands on the truth.

```{python}
#| label: fig-hausman-taylor
#| fig-cap: "Estimating the coefficient γ on a time-invariant regressor Z that is correlated with the unit effect μ (think: returns to schooling, with schooling tied to unobserved ability). 200 simulated panels (N=300, T=5). LEFT (olive): OLS/RE on the levels tries to estimate γ and is biased — it soaks up the unit effect's correlation with Z. MIDDLE: fixed effects cannot estimate γ at all — Z is constant within a unit, so the within transformation deletes it. RIGHT (orange): Hausman–Taylor recovers the truth (dashed ink line) using the panel's own internal instruments — the time-averages of an exogenous time-varying regressor. Bars are the mean estimate across simulations; whiskers are ±1 SD."
#| code-fold: true
#| code-summary: "Show the figure code (fixed effects, Mundlak means, and Hausman–Taylor 2SLS, all by hand)"

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(11)
N, T, n_sim = 300, 5, 200
b1, b2, g1, g2 = 0.5, 0.5, 0.5, 1.0     # g2 = target: the time-invariant, endogenous coefficient

def within(M):                          # demean each unit (rows = units, cols = time)
    return M - M.mean(1, keepdims=True)

ols_g2, ht_g2 = [], []
for _ in range(n_sim):
    a  = rng.normal(0, 1, N)                          # unit effect  mu_i (alpha)
    w  = rng.normal(0, 1, N)                          # a clean channel, independent of a
    Z2 = 0.8 * a + 0.8 * w                            # time-invariant, ENDOGENOUS (corr with mu via a)
    Z1 = rng.normal(0, 1, N)                          # time-invariant, exogenous
    X1 = 0.9 * w[:, None] + rng.normal(0, 1, (N, T))  # time-varying, EXOGENOUS (⊥ mu), corr w/ Z2 via w
    X2 = 0.8 * a[:, None] + rng.normal(0, 1, (N, T))  # time-varying, endogenous (corr with mu)
    y  = b1*X1 + b2*X2 + (g1*Z1 + g2*Z2 + a)[:, None] + rng.normal(0, 1, (N, T))

    # (1) pooled OLS on the levels — tries to estimate g2, inconsistent (Z2 tied to mu)
    Xp = np.column_stack([np.ones(N*T), X1.ravel(), X2.ravel(),
                          np.repeat(Z1, T), np.repeat(Z2, T)])
    ols_g2.append(np.linalg.lstsq(Xp, y.ravel(), rcond=None)[0][4])

    # (3) Hausman–Taylor
    #   step 1: within (FE) slopes on the time-varying regressors — consistent despite X2 endogeneity
    Xw = np.column_stack([within(X1).ravel(), within(X2).ravel()])
    bw = np.linalg.lstsq(Xw, within(y).ravel(), rcond=None)[0]
    #   step 2: one between-unit residual per unit
    d  = y.mean(1) - bw[0]*X1.mean(1) - bw[1]*X2.mean(1)
    #   step 3: 2SLS of d on [1, Z1, Z2] with instruments [1, Z1, mean(X1)]  (X1 exogenous ⊥ mu)
    Zmat = np.column_stack([np.ones(N), Z1, Z2])
    A    = np.column_stack([np.ones(N), Z1, X1.mean(1)])
    PAZ  = A @ np.linalg.solve(A.T @ A, A.T @ Zmat)   # project the regressors onto the instruments
    gamma = np.linalg.solve(PAZ.T @ Zmat, PAZ.T @ d)
    ht_g2.append(gamma[2])

ols_g2, ht_g2 = np.array(ols_g2), np.array(ht_g2)

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7.2, 4.2))
ax.bar(0, ols_g2.mean(), yerr=ols_g2.std(), width=0.6, color="#8a8a7e",
       ecolor="#161613", capsize=4)
ax.bar(2, ht_g2.mean(),  yerr=ht_g2.std(),  width=0.6, color="#e8531d",
       ecolor="#161613", capsize=4)
ax.axhline(g2, color="#161613", ls="--", lw=1.8)
ax.text(2.44, g2, f"  true γ = {g2:.1f}", va="center", ha="left", fontsize=9, color="#161613")
ax.plot([0.72, 1.28], [0, 0], color="#5e5e54", lw=1.4)
ax.text(1, 0.42, "not identified\n(Z differenced out)", ha="center", va="center",
        fontsize=9, color="#5e5e54", style="italic")
ax.set_xticks([0, 1, 2])
ax.set_xticklabels(["OLS / RE\n(levels)", "fixed effects", "Hausman–Taylor"])
ax.set_ylabel("estimated γ on the\ntime-invariant regressor")
ax.set_title("Recovering an effect that never changes within a unit")
ax.set_ylim(0, max(ols_g2.mean() + ols_g2.std(), g2) * 1.28)
fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

## The ladder of assumptions

The four estimators form a single ladder, trading robustness for reach: [@dongli_panel]

- **Pooled OLS** — assumes $\mu_i$ is pure noise, uncorrelated with everything. Estimates all coefficients;
  inconsistent the moment a regressor correlates with $\mu_i$.
- **Random effects** — same exogeneity assumption on $\mu_i$, but efficient; still inconsistent under
  correlation.
- **Correlated random effects (Mundlak)** — lets $\mu_i$ depend on the regressors' means; recovers the FE
  slope for $\beta$ and, if $Z_i$ is exogenous, $\gamma$ too; its coefficient $\pi$ *is* the Hausman test.
- **Hausman–Taylor** — allows a declared subset of regressors to be endogenous and still estimates $\gamma$,
  via internal instruments.
- **Fixed effects** — assumes nothing about $\mu_i$'s correlations; consistent for $\beta$, but silent on any
  $Z_i$.

Moving down the ladder assumes less and less about the unit effect; moving up estimates more and more. Mundlak
and Hausman–Taylor are the rungs in the middle that most applied work actually stands on.

## Common pitfalls

::: {.callout-warning title="Where this goes wrong"}

- **Reading a Hausman rejection as "FE is right" rather than "RE is inconsistent."** The test says the two
  estimators *disagree*; via Mundlak it is literally $\pi\ne0$. That indicts RE's exogeneity assumption — it
  does not certify that your FE specification is well-behaved. [@dongli_panel]

- **Trusting Hausman–Taylor's split without defending it.** The whole estimator rests on your declaration of
  which regressors are exogenous. Misclassify an endogenous variable as exogenous and you have simply invented
  an invalid instrument — the bias returns, now hidden. [@dongli_panel]

- **Weak internal instruments.** $\bar X_1$ instruments $Z_2$ only if the exogenous time-varying regressor is
  genuinely correlated with the time-invariant one. If it barely is, $\gamma$ is estimated with the same
  imprecision (and finite-sample bias) as any [weak-IV](instrumental-variables.qmd) problem. [@dongli_panel]

- **Forgetting the order condition.** You need at least as many exogenous time-varying regressors as
  endogenous time-invariant ones ($k_1 \ge g_2$); short of that, $\gamma$ is under-identified and
  Hausman–Taylor collapses back to the within estimator. [@dongli_panel]

- **Reaching for CRE/HT when FE would do.** If you do not need $\gamma$ and are content to bracket confounders,
  plain fixed effects is simpler and assumes less. These devices earn their keep only when a time-invariant
  effect is the estimand — or the model is nonlinear. [@dongli_panel]

:::

  identification cases (under-, just-, and over-identified); the **Amemiya–MaCurdy (1986)** and
  **Breusch–Mizon–Schmidt (1989)** more-efficient instrument sets; and the reading of correlated random effects
  as the route to consistency in **nonlinear** panels.

## Appendix · the mathematics {.unnumbered}

::: {.math-appendix}

**Mundlak: why the means deliver the within slope.** Stack Mundlak's auxiliary regression as
$\mu = P X \pi/T + \varepsilon$ where $P=I_N\otimes \bar J_T$ averages over time. The model becomes
$$
y = X\beta + (PX)\pi + (Z_\mu\varepsilon + \nu),
$$
with the new composite error having variance $V=\sigma^2_\varepsilon(I_N\otimes J_T)+\sigma^2_\nu I_{NT}$. Using
the partitioned inverse, GLS on this augmented regression yields
$$
\hat\beta_{\text{GLS}} = (X'QX)^{-1}X'Qy = \tilde\beta_{\text{within}},
\qquad
\hat\pi_{\text{GLS}} = \hat\beta_{\text{between}} - \tilde\beta_{\text{within}},
$$
where $Q=I_{NT}-P$ is the within (deviation-from-means) operator. So the augmented random-effects model returns
the fixed-effects slope, and $\hat\pi$ is exactly the between-minus-within gap that the Hausman statistic
measures — $H_0:\pi=0$ is $H_0:\operatorname{Cov}(\mu_i,X_{it})=0$.

**Hausman–Taylor in three steps.** (i) The within estimator gives $\tilde\beta_W$, consistent for $\beta$
regardless of the endogeneity of $X_2$, since $Q$ annihilates $\mu_i$. (ii) Form the between-unit residual
$\hat d_i=\bar y_i-\bar X_i'\tilde\beta_W$, which equals $Z_i'\gamma+\mu_i+\bar\nu_i$ up to sampling error.
(iii) Run 2SLS of $\hat d_i$ on $Z=[Z_1,Z_2]$ using instruments $A=[Z_1,\bar X_1]$:
$$
\hat\gamma_{\text{2SLS}} = (Z'P_A Z)^{-1} Z'P_A \hat d,
\qquad P_A = A(A'A)^{-1}A'.
$$
$\bar X_1$ is a valid instrument because $\operatorname{Cov}(\bar X_1,\mu_i)=0$ (the $X_1$ are exogenous), and a
relevant one because $\operatorname{Cov}(\bar X_1,Z_2)\ne0$. The matrix $Z'P_AZ$ is invertible iff $k_1\ge g_2$
— the order condition. The full-efficiency HT estimator then re-runs the whole system on $\hat\Omega^{-1/2}$-
transformed data with the same instruments; the figure uses the transparent three-step version, which is
consistent and shows the identification cleanly.

:::
