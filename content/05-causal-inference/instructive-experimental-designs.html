Designing Experiments That Answer the Question: Seven Instructive Cases

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source

Random assignment makes a comparison fair. It does not decide which question the comparison answers. That is settled by design choices: what you vary, whom you sample, what you measure, and what you plan for when things go wrong.

A randomised experiment answers exactly the question its design asks, and often a different one from the question the researcher had in mind. Seven field experiments from Gerber and Green’s casebook show the gap and how design closes it: vary each lever of a theory separately, put subjects where the question is, measure the whole outcome, cross the factors, compare only groups formed by random assignment, write out what a failed treatment does, and spend money on follow-up rather than on sample size.

Why it matters

The randomised experiment entry explains why random assignment gives an unbiased estimate of an average treatment effect. This entry is about everything else. Gerber and Green close their chapter of design cases by refusing the “gold standard” label. A better metaphor, they suggest, is gold prospecting: slow, laborious work that, when conditions are right, extracts small flecks of gold from tons of sediment (Gerber and Green 2012). Experiments are part of a cumulative enterprise in which each new design addresses a flaw in the last one.

In each case below the randomisation was sound. What could still go wrong was the question. Two theories predicted the same result. The subgroup that mattered was too small to study. The measured outcome was not the outcome of interest. The treatment a subject received depended on who they were. Failing to receive the treatment had an effect of its own. Outcomes went missing in a way that might be related to the treatment. Gerber and Green handle each case the same way, and it is the habit this entry wants you to take away: write out the potential outcomes, define the estimand, and ask which quantities the design actually identifies before you collect a single observation (Gerber and Green 2012).

Two theories, one prediction: separate the levers

Should health products such as water disinfectant be given away free in poor countries, or sold? One argument against free distribution is a sunk-cost effect: having paid makes it psychologically costly not to use the product. The obvious experiment randomises the price and compares usage among those who buy at each price. But a second theory, screening, makes the same prediction. Households willing to pay more are, on average, the ones who expected more benefit, so higher prices filter out the low-benefit buyers. Both theories say usage rises with price among buyers, so the obvious experiment cannot tell them apart (Gerber and Green 2012).

Ashraf, Berry and Shapiro’s study of the water disinfectant Clorin in Zambia notices that price does two jobs. It decides whether a household buys, and it may affect how much a buyer uses. So they split it into two randomly assigned prices. The offer price is the one a household agrees to. Then, after agreeing, some households are told they qualify for a discount and pay a lower transaction price (Gerber and Green 2012).

Pays the high price Pays the discounted price
Agreed to the high price usage among high-price buyers, paying high the same buyers, paying less
Agreed to the low price (never observed: the price is never raised) usage among low-price buyers, paying low

Read across the top row: the same kind of household (those who agreed to the high price) pays two different amounts, so any usage difference is the sunk-cost effect. Read down the right column: the same price is paid by two kinds of household, so any difference is screening. The fourth cell is never needed (Gerber and Green 2012).

The design rests on an exclusion restriction: usage depends only on the transaction price. If households remembered only the price they were first quoted, the design would wrongly find no sunk-cost effect. That worry is testable (ask buyers what they recall paying), and the researchers also reinforced the transaction price at the point of sale to make it salient (Gerber and Green 2012). The general rule: when a causal claim follows from more than one theory, randomise the components of the treatment separately so that each theory makes a different prediction (Gerber and Green 2012).

Put the subjects where the question is

In the early 1990s the Minnesota Department of Revenue tested whether a letter warning of a possible audit raised the income taxpayers reported (Slemrod, Blumenthal and Christian 2001). Audits and the close tracking of returns are expensive, and sending warnings to a large share of the state risked spillovers through conversation and the press, so only 1,537 letters went out, about one per 1,200 households (Gerber and Green 2012). The question is which households should get them.

A random sample of the state would put two-thirds of the letters on middle-income, low-opportunity households (wage earners whose income is reported by employers) and only 7 on high-income taxpayers with income that is easy to hide, the group with the most revenue at stake. The study oversampled instead: high-opportunity treatments rose about tenfold (529 instead of an expected 51) and high-income treatments more than tripled (187 instead of 50) (Gerber and Green 2012). Because a standard error scales with one over the square root of the group size, halving the middle-income low-opportunity group raises its standard error by about 40%, while the high-income high-opportunity group, roughly ten times larger (80 letters instead of 7), gets a standard error about 70% smaller (see the left panel of Figure 1).

Oversampling has two costs, both in the analysis.

  1. The overall average needs weights. Treatment probabilities differ across the six blocks, so the raw treatment–control difference is biased: high-income households were 12.2% of the treatment group but 4.5% of the control group. Estimate the effect within each block, then average the six with weights equal to each block’s share of the population (Gerber and Green 2012). Gerber and Green report a population effect of about −$278, with a confidence interval (−$1,187, $631) that includes zero. (Our recomputation from their rounded table gives between about −$250 and −$270; the difference is rounding, and it is immaterial against that interval.) Ignoring the weights would give roughly −$2,040 here, about seven times too large, because the block with the most extreme effect is heavily over-represented.
  2. Subgroup tests need a multiple-comparisons correction. With six blocks, the chance of at least one false positive at the 5% level is about 26%, so Bonferroni puts each test at 0.05/6 ≈ 0.008 (multiple testing) (Gerber and Green 2012).

The block effects varied a lot, and one was a surprise. High-income, high-opportunity taxpayers who got the letter reported $45,663 less (standard error 17,394), the opposite of what the department expected (Gerber and Green 2012). Gerber and Green treat it with caution. Year-to-year changes in high incomes have a few huge values, so the normal approximation behind the p-value may mislead, and randomisation inference would give an exact test (Gerber and Green 2012). Their reported p-value is 0.004. Our check: the ratio is z ≈ 2.63, which gives 0.004 one-tailed but about 0.009 two-tailed. For a test “regardless of sign” that sits right on the 0.008 Bonferroni line, which strengthens the case for caution.

Measure the whole outcome, not its first sign

A clothing retailer’s customers were randomly assigned to receive 12 or 17 catalogues over eight months (Simester, Hu, Brynjolfsson and Anderson 2009). Measured over the advertising period and the four months after it, the extra catalogues clearly raised catalogue sales. Measured more widely, they did not pay: catalogue sales fell in the following eight months, and the heavily mailed customers bought less through the company’s website. A full accounting showed that the extra catalogues lost money (Gerber and Green 2012).

The short-run comparison is a form of excludability violation. If the outcome of interest is total purchases, and assignment also affects purchases you didn’t measure (later ones, or through another channel), then the estimate is off by exactly the effect on what you left out (Gerber and Green 2012). The same problem has two common forms:

  • Intertemporal substitution. A get-out-the-vote treatment brings people to this town meeting but makes them skip the next one. Or it raises meeting attendance and lowers voting. A single measured behaviour can move the opposite way from the construct it stands for (Gerber and Green 2012).
  • Proxy or surrogate outcomes. Heart-disease trials often measure cholesterol because heart attacks are rare and slow to accrue. That adds an assumption about how cholesterol relates to heart attacks. A drug that leaves cholesterol unchanged but prevents heart attacks would look useless (Gerber and Green 2012). (This is the experimental version of the measurement problem in concepts & measurement and of Goodhart’s law in the tyranny of metrics.)

The design rule is to measure a range of related outcomes over time, so the conclusion does not rest on the assumption that they all move together (Gerber and Green 2012).

Cross the factors and diversify the treatments

Audit studies of discrimination traditionally send matched pairs of confederates (one Black, one white) to apply for jobs, flats or loans. Two problems: people differ in more ways than any matching can hold fixed, and coached confederates know the purpose of the study and may act on it without meaning to (Gerber and Green 2012). Bertrand and Mullainathan (2004) emailed fictitious résumés to employers who had advertised jobs, so no confederates were needed. Race was signalled by randomly assigned names chosen from population records and a pilot survey, and résumé quality was randomised too, giving a 2 × 2 factorial design (Gerber and Green 2012).

Callback rate (%) Low quality High quality High − low
White names 8.50 10.79 2.29 (p = 0.06)
Black names 6.19 6.70 0.51 (p = 0.61)
Overall 7.35 8.75 1.40 (p = 0.07)

White names were called back 9.65% of the time and Black names 6.45%, a gap of 3.20 points (p < 0.01) (Gerber and Green 2012). The second factor adds what a one-factor study could not: a better résumé helped white applicants (+2.29) much more than Black applicants (+0.51). The interaction is 1.78 points and not significant (p = 0.24), but without the factorial design nobody would have seen that better qualifications were worth little to Black applicants (Gerber and Green 2012). (Factorial ANOVA has the general arithmetic of main effects and interactions.)

Three further lessons come from the same study.

  • Diversify the treatment. One name per race could have been sunk by chance. Gerber and Green mention an audit study whose single name became prominent in a national news story while the study was in the field. Bertrand and Mullainathan used dozens of names, randomly assigned, and could check that the effect did not depend on which name was used. They varied the skills, education and experience that defined “quality” in the same way (Gerber and Green 2012).
  • A factorial estimand is a mixture. The 3.20-point race gap is the effect for a pool of résumés that is half high and half low quality. Reweighting gives the effect for any other mix. The cost is precision: if you only care about one cell, spreading subjects across four cells leaves fewer for it (Gerber and Green 2012).
  • Non-interference can make a design within-subject. Each firm received four résumés, so its responses could be compared within the firm. That is valid only if a firm’s response to one résumé doesn’t depend on the others it received (non-interference), which is plausible when a few test résumés are mixed into a large pile and doubtful when they are most of it (Gerber and Green 2012). When it holds, the gain is power. Of 1,323 firms, 111 favoured the white applicants and 46 the Black applicants. For quality, 99 firms favoured the better résumé and 66 the worse, a two-tailed p of 0.01 compared with 0.07 from the pooled rates (Gerber and Green 2012). (Our exact sign test gives 0.012.) Most firms (1,103) called nobody back.

When the treatment depends on who you are

Many experiments tell voters something true about their own representatives. Chong, De La O, Karlan and Wantchekon gave Mexican precincts a flyer reporting the result of a federal audit of their municipality’s spending. Simplify it to two municipalities, one rated honest and one corrupt, with one precinct in each randomly treated. The design is sound, but the version of the treatment a precinct gets is decided by where it is, not by random assignment (Gerber and Green 2012).

Gerber and Green’s hypothetical table gives every potential outcome (incumbent vote share, %):

Precinct Municipality If told “honest” If told “corrupt” No flyer
1 honest 60 45 60
2 honest 50 35 50
3 corrupt 60 30 40
4 corrupt 70 50 50
Average 60 40 50

The truth: the honest flyer has an average effect of +10, the corrupt flyer −10, so their difference is 20 (Gerber and Green 2012). The experiment only ever shows precincts 1–2 the honest flyer and precincts 3–4 the corrupt one, because only truthful flyers are distributed.

  • Treatment versus control is still unbiased for the effect of getting an audit report of whichever kind: 47.5 − 50 = −2.5 (Gerber and Green 2012).
  • Honest-flyer precincts versus corrupt-flyer precincts is biased. In expectation it is 55 − 40 = 15, not 20, because it mixes the effect of the message with the fact that the honest municipality’s precincts favour the incumbent anyway (Gerber and Green 2012).
  • Within each municipality, the comparison is randomised: the honest flyer’s effect in the honest municipality is 0, and the corrupt flyer’s effect in the corrupt municipality is −5 (Gerber and Green 2012).

The last line invites a story about voters ignoring good news and punishing bad news. Gerber and Green warn against it. What you have is two separate experiments with different treatments on different subjects. The honest municipality never saw the corrupt flyer, and the corrupt municipality never saw the honest one (Gerber and Green 2012). The contrast equals the difference in message effects only under a strong assumption (equation 12.9 in the appendix), for example that each message would have the same effect in both places. The alternative design (assign every message to every precinct, true or not) was ruled out here because the authors would only distribute true information (Gerber and Green 2012). The principle is that only groups formed by random assignment can be compared.

When failing to receive the treatment is a treatment

Restorative-justice conferences bring offenders and victims together in a police-moderated meeting where the offender discusses the crime, apologises and signs an agreement to make amends. Experiments randomly assign offender–victim pairs to a conference or to the ordinary criminal-justice track, and survey the victims (Gerber and Green 2012). The treatment has several ingredients, and each can fail: in Sherman and colleagues’ trials, 16% of victims in the treatment group never received an apology, and roughly a quarter of offenders did not keep their agreements (Gerber and Green 2012).

The usual complier logic divides the ITT by the share who received the treatment to get the effect on compliers. That needs the exclusion restriction: a victim whose offender was assigned to apologise but didn’t turn up should fare the same as a victim in the control group. Here that is implausible, because victims were outraged when an offender failed to appear (Gerber and Green 2012). If a no-show has its own effect, the ITT mixes two effects, the effect of an apology on compliers and the effect of a no-show on never-takers. Dividing by the compliance rate does not separate them. It adds a bias equal to the no-show effect times the ratio of never-takers to compliers (appendix) (Gerber and Green 2012).

Two design fixes are suggested. The simplest is to stop the treatment from failing; later trials did so by working with offenders who were in police custody. The other adds a third arm with stronger encouragement, creating Compliers, Reluctant Compliers and Never-Takers. With assumptions (a no-show has the same effect whoever fails to show, and an apology the same effect whoever gives it) the three arms identify both the apology effect and the no-show effect (Gerber and Green 2012). The same issue arises with natural experiments. A low draft-lottery number may change lives through channels other than military service (Gerber and Green 2012).

Missing data: spend on follow-up, not on sample size

Howell and Peterson’s school-voucher experiments gave about $2,000 a year in private-school vouchers to randomly chosen applicants. In New York, around 1,000 winners and 1,000 randomly chosen non-winners were tested each year for three years. Tests were held outside school, and many families did not come: about a quarter of outcomes were missing after year one, and attrition was higher in the control group every year (for African American students, 26.1% vs 21.3% after one year) (Gerber and Green 2012). Those who stayed away were, on average, poorer and had lower baseline scores, so missingness was probably not random (Gerber and Green 2012).

Take the reported one-year effect for African American students, +3.35 percentile points (SE 1.48), and simplify attrition to 25% in both arms. With no assumptions about the missing students, the extreme-value bounds fill them with the best and worst possible scores (the scale runs 0–100): from −22.5 to +27.5, a range 50 points wide around an effect of 3 points (Gerber and Green 2012).

Gerber and Green then compare two designs with the same budget (Gerber and Green 2012):

  • Original: 1,000 treated and 1,000 controls, standard follow-up. Tested: 2,000 × 0.75 = 1,500.
  • Modified: cut each arm by 200 to 1,600 subjects. The $400,000 saved on vouchers pays for intensive follow-up ($2,000 per family: invitations, scheduling, incentives) of a random half of the 400 who don’t show, recovering 90% of them. Tested: 1,200 + 180 = 1,380.

If attrition were harmless, the modified design would cost a little precision. Gerber and Green put the standard-error ratio at 1.08. (As printed, √(1500/1380) ≈ 1.04; 1.08 is closer to the ratio before the square root. Either way the cost is a few per cent.) If attrition is not harmless, the gain is large. Because the followed-up students are a random sample of the missing ones, only the 10% still missing among them need worst-case values. The bounds shrink from 50 points wide to 5 (Gerber and Green 2012). The condition is that the follow-up effort itself does not change test performance (Gerber and Green 2012).

The same section has two smaller lessons.

  • Missing covariates are not a bias problem. Kindergartners had no baseline test, but assignment was still random. Estimate the effect on change scores for students with a baseline and on levels for those without, then combine the two estimates with weights equal to the group shares. If the effect is assumed constant, use precision (inverse-variance) weights instead (Gerber and Green 2012) (meta-analysis uses the same rule).
  • Optimistic compliance figures understate the effect on compliers. The complier effect is the ITT divided by the difference in take-up between arms. If 80% of the voucher group attend private school and you assume 5% of controls do, the denominator is 0.75. If the true control rate is 15%, it is 0.65 and the true effect is about 15% larger. Assuming too rosy a picture of compliance makes you understate the effect (Gerber and Green 2012).

The design habit

Gerber and Green’s summary advice is a checklist you can run on your own design or on a published one (Gerber and Green 2012):

  1. Write out the potential outcomes, including for treatments that can partly fail.
  2. Describe the subgroups defined by how subjects respond to assignment (compliers, never-takers, reporters).
  3. Define the estimand, and say which population and which mix of treatments it averages over.
  4. Check which parameters the design identifies, and under which assumptions (random assignment, excludability, non-interference).
  5. If an assumption is doubtful, ask whether a different design would remove the need for it.

Common pitfalls

WarningWhere this goes wrong
  • Testing a prediction that two theories share. If sunk cost and screening both predict higher usage at higher prices, a price experiment cannot choose between them. Randomise the components separately. (Gerber and Green 2012)
  • Comparing raw means after oversampling. When the probability of treatment differs by block, weight the block estimates by population shares, or reweight subjects by the inverse of their assignment probability. In the Minnesota data, ignoring the weights makes the overall effect about seven times too large. (Gerber and Green 2012)
  • Celebrating one significant subgroup out of six. Correct for multiple comparisons, check whether the p-value is one- or two-tailed, and prefer randomisation inference when outcomes have extreme values. (Gerber and Green 2012)
  • Measuring the first sign of the outcome and calling it the outcome. Short-run sales, one form of civic participation, or a surrogate such as cholesterol can move differently from the outcome you care about. (Gerber and Green 2012)
  • Comparing treatment versions that were not randomly assigned. If the content of the treatment depends on subjects’ characteristics, contrasts between versions mix message effects with differences between subjects. Compare only groups formed by random assignment. (Gerber and Green 2012)
  • Dividing by the compliance rate when a failed treatment has its own effect. If a no-show hurts, ITT / take-up is not the effect of the treatment on compliers. Write out the outcomes for “assigned but not received” first. (Gerber and Green 2012)
  • Buying sample size when the threat is attrition. A slightly smaller experiment with intensive follow-up of a random sample of the missing can shrink the no-assumption bounds tenfold for a few per cent of precision. (Gerber and Green 2012)

Appendix · the mathematics

Separating sunk cost from screening. Let Y_i(x)=1 if household i agrees to buy at offer price x, and Q_i(x) its usage at transaction price x. With prices p' > p, add and subtract E[Q_i(p)\mid Y_i(p')=1]: \underbrace{E[Q_i(p')\mid Y_i(p')=1]-E[Q_i(p)\mid Y_i(p')=1]}_{\text{sunk cost: same buyers, two prices}} +\underbrace{E[Q_i(p)\mid Y_i(p')=1]-E[Q_i(p)\mid Y_i(p)=1]}_{\text{screening: same price, two kinds of buyer}} . The ordinary price experiment estimates only the sum. Randomising the offer and transaction prices separately estimates each term (Gerber and Green 2012).

Population weighting. With blocks b of population share w_b = N_b/N and within-block difference in means \widehat{\text{ATE}}_b, the unbiased population estimate is \sum_b w_b\,\widehat{\text{ATE}}_b. Equivalently, weight each subject by (block size)/(number in their block assigned to their arm) and take the difference of weighted means (Gerber and Green 2012). Its variance is \sum_b w_b^2\,\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{\text{ATE}}_b), which is why oversampling a small block hardly helps the overall estimate but transforms the block’s own one.

Tailored treatments. Let X_i\in\{h,c\} be the municipality type and \pi_h=1-\pi_c its share. The ATE of the honest flyer over everyone is E[Y_i(h)-Y_i(0)] = \pi_h\,E[Y_i(h)-Y_i(0)\mid X_i=h] + \pi_c\,E[Y_i(h)-Y_i(0)\mid X_i=c], and similarly for the corrupt flyer. The design identifies only the first conditional term for the honest flyer and the corresponding term for the corrupt one. Their difference equals the difference in overall ATEs only when \pi_c\big\{E[Y(h)-Y(0)\mid c]-E[Y(h)-Y(0)\mid h]\big\} = \pi_h\big\{E[Y(c)-Y(0)\mid h]-E[Y(c)-Y(0)\mid c]\big\}, which holds, for example, when each flyer’s effect is the same in both municipalities (G&G eq. 12.9). In the table, the honest flyer’s effect is 0 in the honest municipality and +20 in the corrupt one, so the condition fails.

When a failed treatment has an effect. Let Y_i(z,d) be the outcome under assignment z and receipt d, with one-sided noncompliance and complier share \alpha=E[D_i(1)]. Then \text{ITT} = \alpha\,E[Y(1,1)-Y(0,0)\mid C] + (1-\alpha)\,E[Y(1,0)-Y(0,0)\mid NT], so \frac{\text{ITT}}{\alpha} = \text{CACE} + \frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\,E[Y(1,0)-Y(0,0)\mid NT]. The second term is zero under excludability, Y(1,0)=Y(0,0) (Gerber and Green 2012). With 16% of victims missing their apology, (1-\alpha)/\alpha = 0.16/0.84 \approx 0.19, so each point of no-show effect adds about 0.19 points of bias.

Bounds with follow-up. With observed share \pi of outcomes, observed effect \hat\tau, outcome range [y_{\min},y_{\max}], and simplifying to equal attrition in both arms, G&G’s no-assumption bounds are \pi\hat\tau + (1-\pi)\,y_{\min} and \pi\hat\tau + (1-\pi)\,y_{\max} for effects in [-100,100]: width (1-\pi)\cdot 200 = 50. If a random subsample of the missing is followed up and a share r of them recovered, with effect F among those recovered, the missing part becomes r F + (1-r)\,y and \text{bounds} = \pi\hat\tau + (1-\pi)\big[rF + (1-r)\,y_{\min}\big],\ \ \pi\hat\tau + (1-\pi)\big[rF + (1-r)\,y_{\max}\big], width (1-\pi)(1-r)\cdot 200. With \pi=0.75, r=0.9: 0.25\times0.1\times200 = 5, and the bounds are 0.225F to 5.0 + 0.225F (G&G eqs. 12.14–12.15) (Gerber and Green 2012).

Mismeasured compliance. \widehat{\text{CACE}} = \widehat{\text{ITT}}/(\hat d_1-\hat d_0). Overstating the compliance gap (0.80 − 0.05 = 0.75 instead of 0.80 − 0.15 = 0.65) shrinks the estimate by a factor 0.65/0.75 ≈ 0.87 (Gerber and Green 2012).

## Why it matters

The [randomised experiment](randomized-experiments.qmd) entry explains why random assignment gives an
unbiased estimate of an average treatment effect. This entry is about everything else. Gerber and Green
close their chapter of design cases by refusing the "gold standard" label. A better metaphor, they
suggest, is **gold prospecting**: slow, laborious work that, when conditions are right, extracts small
flecks of gold from tons of sediment [@gerber_green]. Experiments are part of a cumulative enterprise in
which each new design addresses a flaw in the last one.

In each case below the randomisation was sound. What could still go wrong was the *question*. Two
theories predicted the same result. The subgroup that mattered was too small to study. The measured
outcome was not the outcome of interest. The treatment a subject received depended on who they were.
Failing to receive the treatment had an effect of its own. Outcomes went missing in a way that might be
related to the treatment. Gerber and Green handle each case the same way, and it is the habit this entry
wants you to take away: **write out the potential outcomes, define the estimand, and ask which
quantities the design actually identifies** before you collect a single observation [@gerber_green].

## Two theories, one prediction: separate the levers {#sec-separate-mechanisms}

Should health products such as water disinfectant be given away free in poor countries, or sold?
One argument against free distribution is a **sunk-cost** effect: having paid makes it psychologically
costly not to use the product. The obvious experiment randomises the price and compares usage among
those who buy at each price. But a second theory, **screening**, makes the same prediction. Households
willing to pay more are, on average, the ones who expected more benefit, so higher prices filter out the
low-benefit buyers. Both theories say usage rises with price among buyers, so the obvious experiment
cannot tell them apart [@gerber_green].

Ashraf, Berry and Shapiro's study of the water disinfectant *Clorin* in Zambia notices that price does
two jobs. It decides **whether** a household buys, and it may affect **how much** a buyer uses. So they
split it into two randomly assigned prices. The **offer price** is the one a household agrees to. Then,
after agreeing, some households are told they qualify for a discount and pay a lower **transaction
price** [@gerber_green].

| | Pays the high price | Pays the discounted price |
|---|---|---|
| **Agreed to the high price** | usage among high-price buyers, paying high | the same buyers, paying less |
| **Agreed to the low price** | (never observed: the price is never raised) | usage among low-price buyers, paying low |

Read across the top row: the same *kind* of household (those who agreed to the high price) pays two
different amounts, so any usage difference is the **sunk-cost** effect. Read down the right column:
the same price is paid by two kinds of household, so any difference is **screening**. The fourth cell is
never needed [@gerber_green].

The design rests on an exclusion restriction: usage depends only on the transaction price. If
households remembered only the price they were first quoted, the design would wrongly find no sunk-cost
effect. That worry is testable (ask buyers what they recall paying), and the researchers also reinforced
the transaction price at the point of sale to make it salient [@gerber_green]. The general rule: **when a
causal claim follows from more than one theory, randomise the components of the treatment separately so
that each theory makes a different prediction** [@gerber_green].

## Put the subjects where the question is {#sec-oversampling}

In the early 1990s the Minnesota Department of Revenue tested whether a letter warning of a possible
audit raised the income taxpayers reported (Slemrod, Blumenthal and Christian 2001). Audits and the
close tracking of returns are expensive, and sending warnings to a large share of the state risked
spillovers through conversation and the press, so only **1,537** letters went out, about one per 1,200
households [@gerber_green]. The question is *which* households should get them.

A random sample of the state would put two-thirds of the letters on middle-income, low-opportunity
households (wage earners whose income is reported by employers) and only **7** on high-income
taxpayers with income that is easy to hide, the group with the most revenue at stake. The study
oversampled instead: high-opportunity treatments rose about tenfold (529 instead of an expected 51) and
high-income treatments more than tripled (187 instead of 50) [@gerber_green]. Because a standard error
scales with one over the square root of the group size, halving the middle-income low-opportunity group
raises its standard error by about 40%, while the high-income high-opportunity group, roughly ten times larger (80 letters instead of 7), gets
a standard error about 70% smaller (see the left panel of @fig-instructive-designs).

Oversampling has two costs, both in the analysis.

1. **The overall average needs weights.** Treatment probabilities differ across the six blocks, so the raw
   treatment–control difference is biased: high-income households were 12.2% of the treatment group but
   4.5% of the control group. Estimate the effect within each block, then average the six with weights
   equal to each block's share of the *population* [@gerber_green]. Gerber and Green report a population
   effect of about −$278, with a confidence interval (−$1,187, $631) that includes zero. (Our recomputation
   from their rounded table gives between about −$250 and −$270; the difference is rounding, and it is
   immaterial against that interval.) Ignoring the weights would give roughly −$2,040 here, about seven times too
   large, because the block with the most extreme effect is heavily over-represented.
2. **Subgroup tests need a multiple-comparisons correction.** With six blocks, the chance of at least one
   false positive at the 5% level is about 26%, so Bonferroni puts each test at 0.05/6 ≈ 0.008
   ([multiple testing](../02-inference/multiple-testing.qmd)) [@gerber_green].

The block effects varied a lot, and one was a surprise. High-income, high-opportunity taxpayers who got
the letter reported **$45,663 less** (standard error 17,394), the opposite of what the department
expected [@gerber_green]. Gerber and Green treat it with caution. Year-to-year changes in high incomes have
a few huge values, so the normal approximation behind the p-value may mislead, and randomisation
inference would give an exact test [@gerber_green]. Their reported p-value is 0.004. Our check: the
ratio is z ≈ 2.63, which gives 0.004 one-tailed but about 0.009 two-tailed. For a test "regardless of sign"
that sits right on the 0.008 Bonferroni line, which strengthens the case for caution.

## Measure the whole outcome, not its first sign {#sec-comprehensive-outcomes}

A clothing retailer's customers were randomly assigned to receive 12 or 17 catalogues over eight
months (Simester, Hu, Brynjolfsson and Anderson 2009). Measured over the advertising period and the
four months after it, the extra catalogues clearly raised catalogue sales. Measured more widely, they
did not pay: catalogue sales fell in the following eight months, and the heavily mailed customers bought
less through the company's website. A full accounting showed that the extra catalogues **lost money**
[@gerber_green].

The short-run comparison is a form of excludability violation. If the outcome of interest is total
purchases, and assignment also affects purchases you didn't measure (later ones, or through another
channel), then the estimate is off by exactly the effect on what you left out [@gerber_green]. The same
problem has two common forms:

- **Intertemporal substitution.** A get-out-the-vote treatment brings people to this town meeting but
  makes them skip the next one. Or it raises meeting attendance and lowers voting. A single measured
  behaviour can move the opposite way from the construct it stands for [@gerber_green].
- **Proxy or surrogate outcomes.** Heart-disease trials often measure cholesterol because heart attacks
  are rare and slow to accrue. That adds an assumption about how cholesterol relates to heart attacks. A
  drug that leaves cholesterol unchanged but prevents heart attacks would look useless [@gerber_green].
  (This is the experimental version of the measurement problem in
  [concepts & measurement](../00-overture/concepts-and-measurement.qmd) and of Goodhart's law in
  [the tyranny of metrics](../06-field-realities/tyranny-of-metrics.qmd).)

The design rule is to **measure a range of related outcomes over time**, so the conclusion does not rest
on the assumption that they all move together [@gerber_green].

## Cross the factors and diversify the treatments {#sec-factorial-audit}

Audit studies of discrimination traditionally send matched pairs of confederates (one Black, one
white) to apply for jobs, flats or loans. Two problems: people differ in more ways than any matching can
hold fixed, and coached confederates know the purpose of the study and may act on it without meaning to
[@gerber_green]. Bertrand and Mullainathan (2004) emailed fictitious résumés to employers who had
advertised jobs, so no confederates were needed. Race was signalled by randomly assigned names chosen
from population records and a pilot survey, and résumé **quality** was randomised too, giving a 2 × 2
factorial design [@gerber_green].

| Callback rate (%) | Low quality | High quality | High − low |
|---|---|---|---|
| **White names** | 8.50 | 10.79 | 2.29 (p = 0.06) |
| **Black names** | 6.19 | 6.70 | 0.51 (p = 0.61) |
| **Overall** | 7.35 | 8.75 | 1.40 (p = 0.07) |

White names were called back 9.65% of the time and Black names 6.45%, a gap of 3.20 points
(p < 0.01) [@gerber_green]. The second factor adds what a one-factor study could not: a better résumé
helped white applicants (+2.29) much more than Black applicants (+0.51). The interaction is 1.78 points
and not significant (p = 0.24), but without the factorial design nobody would have seen that better
qualifications were worth little to Black applicants [@gerber_green].
([Factorial ANOVA](../03-regression/factorial-anova-and-ancova.qmd) has the general arithmetic of main
effects and interactions.)

Three further lessons come from the same study.

- **Diversify the treatment.** One name per race could have been sunk by chance. Gerber and Green
  mention an audit study whose single name became prominent in a national news story while the study was
  in the field. Bertrand and Mullainathan used dozens of names, randomly assigned, and could check that
  the effect did not depend on which name was used. They varied the skills, education and experience
  that defined "quality" in the same way [@gerber_green].
- **A factorial estimand is a mixture.** The 3.20-point race gap is the effect for a pool of résumés that
  is half high and half low quality. Reweighting gives the effect for any other mix. The cost is
  precision: if you only care about one cell, spreading subjects across four cells leaves fewer for it
  [@gerber_green].
- **Non-interference can make a design within-subject.** Each firm received four résumés, so its responses
  could be compared within the firm. That is valid only if a firm's response to one résumé doesn't depend on
  the others it received ([non-interference](peer-effects-and-spillovers.qmd)), which is plausible when a few
  test résumés are mixed into a large pile and doubtful when they are most of it [@gerber_green]. When it
  holds, the gain is power. Of 1,323 firms, 111 favoured the white applicants and 46 the Black applicants.
  For quality, 99 firms favoured the better résumé and 66 the worse, a two-tailed p of 0.01 compared with
  0.07 from the pooled rates [@gerber_green]. (Our exact sign test gives 0.012.) Most firms (1,103) called
  nobody back.

## When the treatment depends on who you are {#sec-tailored-treatments}

Many experiments tell voters something true about their own representatives. Chong, De La O, Karlan and
Wantchekon gave Mexican precincts a flyer reporting the result of a federal audit of their
municipality's spending. Simplify it to two municipalities, one rated honest and one corrupt, with one
precinct in each randomly treated. The design is sound, but the *version* of the treatment a precinct
gets is decided by where it is, not by random assignment [@gerber_green].

Gerber and Green's hypothetical table gives every potential outcome (incumbent vote share, %):

| Precinct | Municipality | If told "honest" | If told "corrupt" | No flyer |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | honest | 60 | 45 | 60 |
| 2 | honest | 50 | 35 | 50 |
| 3 | corrupt | 60 | 30 | 40 |
| 4 | corrupt | 70 | 50 | 50 |
| **Average** | | 60 | 40 | 50 |

The truth: the honest flyer has an average effect of +10, the corrupt flyer −10, so their difference
is 20 [@gerber_green]. The experiment only ever shows precincts 1–2 the honest flyer and precincts 3–4
the corrupt one, because only truthful flyers are distributed.

- **Treatment versus control** is still unbiased for the effect of *getting an audit report of whichever
  kind*: 47.5 − 50 = −2.5 [@gerber_green].
- **Honest-flyer precincts versus corrupt-flyer precincts** is biased. In expectation it is 55 − 40 = 15,
  not 20, because it mixes the effect of the message with the fact that the honest municipality's precincts
  favour the incumbent anyway [@gerber_green].
- **Within each municipality**, the comparison is randomised: the honest flyer's effect *in the honest
  municipality* is 0, and the corrupt flyer's effect *in the corrupt municipality* is −5 [@gerber_green].

The last line invites a story about voters ignoring good news and punishing bad news. Gerber and Green
warn against it. What you have is **two separate experiments with different treatments on different
subjects**. The honest municipality never saw the corrupt flyer, and the corrupt municipality never saw
the honest one [@gerber_green]. The contrast equals the difference in message effects only under a strong
assumption (equation 12.9 in the appendix), for example that each message would have the same effect in
both places. The alternative design (assign every message to every precinct, true or not) was ruled out
here because the authors would only distribute true information [@gerber_green]. The principle is that
**only groups formed by random assignment can be compared**.

## When failing to receive the treatment is a treatment {#sec-failed-treatment}

Restorative-justice conferences bring offenders and victims together in a police-moderated meeting
where the offender discusses the crime, apologises and signs an agreement to make amends. Experiments
randomly assign offender–victim pairs to a conference or to the ordinary criminal-justice track, and
survey the victims [@gerber_green]. The treatment has several ingredients, and each can fail: in Sherman
and colleagues' trials, 16% of victims in the treatment group never received an apology, and roughly a
quarter of offenders did not keep their agreements [@gerber_green].

The usual [complier](instrumental-variables.qmd#sec-compliers) logic divides the ITT by the share who
received the treatment to get the effect on compliers. That needs the exclusion restriction: a victim
whose offender was assigned to apologise but didn't turn up should fare the same as a victim in the
control group. Here that is implausible, because victims were outraged when an offender failed to
appear [@gerber_green]. If a no-show has its own effect, the ITT mixes two effects, the effect of an
apology on compliers and the effect of a no-show on never-takers. Dividing by the compliance rate does
not separate them. It adds a bias equal to the no-show effect times the ratio of never-takers to
compliers (appendix) [@gerber_green].

Two design fixes are suggested. The simplest is to stop the treatment from failing; later trials did so
by working with offenders who were in police custody. The other adds a third arm with **stronger
encouragement**, creating Compliers, Reluctant Compliers and Never-Takers. With assumptions (a no-show has
the same effect whoever fails to show, and an apology the same effect whoever gives it) the three arms
identify both the apology effect and the no-show effect [@gerber_green]. The same issue arises with
natural experiments. A low draft-lottery number may change lives through channels other than military
service [@gerber_green].

## Missing data: spend on follow-up, not on sample size {#sec-follow-up}

Howell and Peterson's school-voucher experiments gave about $2,000 a year in private-school vouchers to
randomly chosen applicants. In New York, around 1,000 winners and 1,000 randomly chosen non-winners were
tested each year for three years. Tests were held outside school, and many families did not come: about a
quarter of outcomes were missing after year one, and attrition was higher in the control group every
year (for African American students, 26.1% vs 21.3% after one year) [@gerber_green]. Those who stayed away
were, on average, poorer and had lower baseline scores, so missingness was probably not random
[@gerber_green].

Take the reported one-year effect for African American students, **+3.35 percentile points** (SE 1.48),
and simplify attrition to 25% in both arms. With no assumptions about the missing students, the
[extreme-value bounds](partial-identification.qmd) fill them with the best and worst possible scores
(the scale runs 0–100): from **−22.5 to +27.5**, a range 50 points wide around an effect of 3 points
[@gerber_green].

Gerber and Green then compare two designs **with the same budget** [@gerber_green]:

- **Original:** 1,000 treated and 1,000 controls, standard follow-up. Tested: 2,000 × 0.75 = 1,500.
- **Modified:** cut each arm by 200 to 1,600 subjects. The $400,000 saved on vouchers pays for intensive
  follow-up ($2,000 per family: invitations, scheduling, incentives) of a random half of the 400 who
  don't show, recovering 90% of them. Tested: 1,200 + 180 = 1,380.

If attrition were harmless, the modified design would cost a little precision. Gerber and Green put the
standard-error ratio at 1.08. (As printed, √(1500/1380) ≈ 1.04; 1.08 is closer to the ratio before the
square root. Either way the cost is a few per cent.) If attrition is not harmless, the gain is large.
Because the followed-up students are a random sample of the missing ones, only the 10% still missing
among them need worst-case values. The bounds shrink from 50 points wide to **5** [@gerber_green]. The
condition is that the follow-up effort itself does not change test performance [@gerber_green].

The same section has two smaller lessons.

- **Missing covariates are not a bias problem.** Kindergartners had no baseline test, but assignment was
  still random. Estimate the effect on change scores for students with a baseline and on levels for those
  without, then combine the two estimates with weights equal to the group shares. If the effect is assumed
  constant, use precision (inverse-variance) weights instead [@gerber_green]
  ([meta-analysis](../06-field-realities/meta-analysis.qmd) uses the same rule).
- **Optimistic compliance figures understate the effect on compliers.** The complier effect is the ITT
  divided by the difference in take-up between arms. If 80% of the voucher group attend private school
  and you assume 5% of controls do, the denominator is 0.75. If the true control rate is 15%, it is 0.65
  and the true effect is about 15% larger. Assuming too rosy a picture of compliance makes you understate
  the effect [@gerber_green].

## The design habit

Gerber and Green's summary advice is a checklist you can run on your own design or on a published one
[@gerber_green]:

1. Write out the potential outcomes, including for treatments that can partly fail.
2. Describe the subgroups defined by how subjects respond to assignment (compliers, never-takers,
   reporters).
3. Define the estimand, and say which population and which mix of treatments it averages over.
4. Check which parameters the design identifies, and under which assumptions (random assignment,
   excludability, non-interference).
5. If an assumption is doubtful, ask whether a different design would remove the need for it.

## Common pitfalls

::: {.callout-warning title="Where this goes wrong"}
- **Testing a prediction that two theories share.** If sunk cost and screening both predict higher usage
  at higher prices, a price experiment cannot choose between them. Randomise the components separately.
  [@gerber_green]
- **Comparing raw means after oversampling.** When the probability of treatment differs by block, weight
  the block estimates by population shares, or reweight subjects by the inverse of their assignment
  probability. In the Minnesota data, ignoring the weights makes the overall effect about seven times too
  large. [@gerber_green]
- **Celebrating one significant subgroup out of six.** Correct for multiple comparisons, check whether the
  p-value is one- or two-tailed, and prefer randomisation inference when outcomes have extreme values.
  [@gerber_green]
- **Measuring the first sign of the outcome and calling it the outcome.** Short-run sales, one form of
  civic participation, or a surrogate such as cholesterol can move differently from the outcome you care
  about. [@gerber_green]
- **Comparing treatment versions that were not randomly assigned.** If the content of the treatment
  depends on subjects' characteristics, contrasts between versions mix message effects with differences
  between subjects. Compare only groups formed by random assignment. [@gerber_green]
- **Dividing by the compliance rate when a failed treatment has its own effect.** If a no-show hurts,
  ITT / take-up is not the effect of the treatment on compliers. Write out the outcomes for
  "assigned but not received" first. [@gerber_green]
- **Buying sample size when the threat is attrition.** A slightly smaller experiment with intensive
  follow-up of a random sample of the missing can shrink the no-assumption bounds tenfold for a few per
  cent of precision. [@gerber_green]
:::

## Appendix · the mathematics {.unnumbered}

::: {.math-appendix}

**Separating sunk cost from screening.** Let $Y_i(x)=1$ if household $i$ agrees to buy at offer price $x$, and
$Q_i(x)$ its usage at transaction price $x$. With prices $p' > p$, add and subtract
$E[Q_i(p)\mid Y_i(p')=1]$:
$$
\underbrace{E[Q_i(p')\mid Y_i(p')=1]-E[Q_i(p)\mid Y_i(p')=1]}_{\text{sunk cost: same buyers, two prices}}
+\underbrace{E[Q_i(p)\mid Y_i(p')=1]-E[Q_i(p)\mid Y_i(p)=1]}_{\text{screening: same price, two kinds of buyer}} .
$$
The ordinary price experiment estimates only the sum. Randomising the offer and transaction prices separately
estimates each term [@gerber_green].

**Population weighting.** With blocks $b$ of population share $w_b = N_b/N$ and within-block difference in means
$\widehat{\text{ATE}}_b$, the unbiased population estimate is $\sum_b w_b\,\widehat{\text{ATE}}_b$. Equivalently,
weight each subject by (block size)/(number in their block assigned to their arm) and take the difference of
weighted means [@gerber_green]. Its variance is $\sum_b w_b^2\,\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{\text{ATE}}_b)$, which is why
oversampling a small block hardly helps the overall estimate but transforms the block's own one.

**Tailored treatments.** Let $X_i\in\{h,c\}$ be the municipality type and $\pi_h=1-\pi_c$ its share. The ATE of
the honest flyer over everyone is
$$
E[Y_i(h)-Y_i(0)] = \pi_h\,E[Y_i(h)-Y_i(0)\mid X_i=h] + \pi_c\,E[Y_i(h)-Y_i(0)\mid X_i=c],
$$
and similarly for the corrupt flyer. The design identifies only the first conditional term for the honest flyer
and the corresponding term for the corrupt one. Their difference equals the difference in overall ATEs only when
$$
\pi_c\big\{E[Y(h)-Y(0)\mid c]-E[Y(h)-Y(0)\mid h]\big\} = \pi_h\big\{E[Y(c)-Y(0)\mid h]-E[Y(c)-Y(0)\mid c]\big\},
$$
which holds, for example, when each flyer's effect is the same in both municipalities (G&G eq. 12.9). In the
table, the honest flyer's effect is 0 in the honest municipality and +20 in the corrupt one, so the condition fails.

**When a failed treatment has an effect.** Let $Y_i(z,d)$ be the outcome under assignment $z$ and receipt $d$,
with one-sided noncompliance and complier share $\alpha=E[D_i(1)]$. Then
$$
\text{ITT} = \alpha\,E[Y(1,1)-Y(0,0)\mid C] + (1-\alpha)\,E[Y(1,0)-Y(0,0)\mid NT],
$$
so
$$
\frac{\text{ITT}}{\alpha} = \text{CACE} + \frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\,E[Y(1,0)-Y(0,0)\mid NT].
$$
The second term is zero under excludability, $Y(1,0)=Y(0,0)$ [@gerber_green]. With 16% of victims missing their
apology, $(1-\alpha)/\alpha = 0.16/0.84 \approx 0.19$, so each point of no-show effect adds about 0.19 points of bias.

**Bounds with follow-up.** With observed share $\pi$ of outcomes, observed effect $\hat\tau$, outcome range
$[y_{\min},y_{\max}]$, and simplifying to equal attrition in both arms, G&G's no-assumption bounds are
$\pi\hat\tau + (1-\pi)\,y_{\min}$ and $\pi\hat\tau + (1-\pi)\,y_{\max}$ for effects in $[-100,100]$: width
$(1-\pi)\cdot 200 = 50$. If a random subsample of the missing is followed up and a share $r$ of them recovered, with
effect $F$ among those recovered, the missing part becomes $r F + (1-r)\,y$ and
$$
\text{bounds} = \pi\hat\tau + (1-\pi)\big[rF + (1-r)\,y_{\min}\big],\ \ \pi\hat\tau + (1-\pi)\big[rF + (1-r)\,y_{\max}\big],
$$
width $(1-\pi)(1-r)\cdot 200$. With $\pi=0.75$, $r=0.9$: $0.25\times0.1\times200 = 5$, and the bounds are
$0.225F$ to $5.0 + 0.225F$ (G&G eqs. 12.14–12.15) [@gerber_green].

**Mismeasured compliance.** $\widehat{\text{CACE}} = \widehat{\text{ITT}}/(\hat d_1-\hat d_0)$. Overstating the
compliance gap (0.80 − 0.05 = 0.75 instead of 0.80 − 0.15 = 0.65) shrinks the estimate by a factor 0.65/0.75
≈ 0.87 [@gerber_green].
