Causal Machine Learning: Heterogeneous Effects

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source

Bend the predictive toolkit of Part IV toward causation — to learn not just the average effect, but for whom it is large, and to act on it.

The whole of Part V chased a single number: an average effect. But “never cross a river that is on average four feet deep.” Causal machine learning estimates the effect as a function of who you are — the conditional average treatment effect \tau(x) — using the flexibility of ML without inheriting its bias. The trick is to estimate the effect, not the outcome, and to protect that estimate from overfitting with orthogonalisation and cross-fitting. It closes the loop: the predictive culture, disciplined back into the service of cause.

Why it matters

An ATE can hide everything that matters for policy: a drug that helps the young and harms the old can average to “no effect.” (Chernozhukov et al. 2024) Modern social science wants heterogeneous treatment effects — for whom does this work? — and the obvious idea, “throw a random forest at it,” fails in a way worth understanding deeply. This entry is where Part IV’s machinery — regularization, cross-validation, forests, the bias–variance trade — is repurposed for causation, and where the double/debiased ML preview is finally cashed out. It is the natural summit of the summit: the predictive and causal cultures, reconciled.

From ATE to CATE

The target shifts from a scalar to a function — the conditional average treatment effect: \tau(x)=\mathbb E\big[Y(1)-Y(0)\mid X=x\big]. \tau(x) is the effect for units with characteristics x — the engine of personalised policy, letting you move from a uniform rollout to targeting whoever benefits most. (Chernozhukov et al. 2024) Everything from potential outcomes still holds; we’ve simply stopped averaging away the variation we now want to see.

Why naive ML is biased for effects

The tempting move — fit a flexible ML model of Y on (D,X) and read off D’s influence — is biased, for a reason that is subtle and important: (Chernozhukov et al. 2024)

  • Regularization bias. ML methods are built to minimise prediction error for Y, so they regularize — shrinking coefficients, pruning splits — toward whatever predicts the outcome best. When the treatment effect is small relative to other predictors, the algorithm cheerfully shrinks the treatment’s role toward zero, biasing the effect. (Chernozhukov et al. 2024)
  • Effect ≠ prediction. Estimating an effect is fundamentally harder than predicting an outcome: it targets the difference between two counterfactual states, only one of which is ever observed. (Chernozhukov et al. 2024) A model superb at predicting Y can be terrible at isolating \partial Y/\partial D.

The fix is to stop estimating the outcome and start estimating the effect, with the nuisances partialled out.

Double/debiased machine learning

Double machine learning (DML), due to Chernozhukov and coauthors, lets you use any flexible ML for the hard prediction parts while keeping valid inference on the causal parameter. (Chernozhukov et al. 2024) Three moves:

  1. Estimate two nuisance functions with ML: an outcome model g_0(X)=\mathbb E[Y\mid X] and a treatment/propensity model m_0(X)=\mathbb E[D\mid X]. (Chernozhukov et al. 2024)
  2. Orthogonalise (partial out). Residualise both Y and D on X — regress the Y-residual on the D-residual. This is Frisch–Waugh–Lovell with ML doing the conditioning, and it yields a Neyman-orthogonal moment: the effect estimate is first-order insensitive to small errors in g_0 and m_0. (Chernozhukov et al. 2024) You can plug in imperfect ML nuisances and the errors don’t spill into the treatment effect — the “debiasing.”
  3. Cross-fit. Estimate the nuisances on one fold and the effect on a held-out fold, so no unit’s own outcome contaminates its own nuisance prediction — removing overfitting bias. (Chernozhukov et al. 2024) (Exactly the cross-validation discipline, now load-bearing for inference, not just tuning.)

Orthogonality kills the regularization bias; cross-fitting kills the overfitting bias. Together they let ML serve identification instead of sabotaging it. The figure shows the payoff DML and causal forests deliver — recovering an effect that varies with x.

Show the figure code 
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(21)
n = 6000
x = rng.uniform(0, 1, n)
D = rng.integers(0, 2, n)                  # randomized treatment (clean identification)
tau_true = 0.5 + 3.0 * x                   # effect grows with x (heterogeneous)
y = 1.0 + 2.0 * x + tau_true * D + rng.normal(0, 1.0, n)

# Honest binned CATE: within each x-bin, treated mean - control mean (+ 95% CI).
edges = np.linspace(0, 1, 11)
centers, est, lo, hi = [], [], [], []
for a, b in zip(edges[:-1], edges[1:]):
    m = (x >= a) & (x < b)
    yt, yc = y[m & (D == 1)], y[m & (D == 0)]
    d = yt.mean() - yc.mean()
    se = np.sqrt(yt.var(ddof=1)/yt.size + yc.var(ddof=1)/yc.size)
    centers.append((a+b)/2); est.append(d); lo.append(d-1.96*se); hi.append(d+1.96*se)
centers, est = np.array(centers), np.array(est)
ate = (y[D==1].mean() - y[D==0].mean())

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7.2, 4.2))
xs = np.linspace(0, 1, 100)
ax.plot(xs, 0.5 + 3.0*xs, color="#5e5e54", lw=2.4, label="true CATE  τ(x)")
ax.errorbar(centers, est, yerr=[est-np.array(lo), np.array(hi)-est], fmt="o",
            color="#161613", ms=5, capsize=3, label="honest binned estimate")
ax.axhline(ate, color="#b7b6a8", ls="--", lw=1.6, label=f"ATE = {ate:.2f} (hides heterogeneity)")
ax.set_xlabel("covariate x"); ax.set_ylabel("treatment effect")
ax.set_title("Who benefits? The effect as a function of x")
ax.legend(fontsize=8, frameon=False, loc="upper left")
fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()

Causal forests and honesty

To estimate \tau(x) flexibly, causal forests (generalized random forests) adapt random forests with two changes: (Chernozhukov et al. 2024)

  • Causal splitting. Instead of splitting to reduce prediction error (RSS), trees split to maximise the heterogeneity of the estimated treatment effect across the children — actively hunting for the covariates along which the effect varies. (Chernozhukov et al. 2024)
  • Honesty. A tree is honest if it uses one subsample to choose the splits and a separate subsample to estimate the effect in each leaf. (Chernozhukov et al. 2024) This separation is what makes the leaf estimates statistically valid — it earns trustworthy confidence intervals on \tau(x), instead of the overconfident ones you’d get from estimating effects in the very leaves you carved to maximise them.

Even honest forests come with a warning. Their confidence intervals are “more brittle and are not as assumption-lean as inference based on OLS” (Chernozhukov et al. 2024). They are valid only for low-dimensional X and particular hyperparameter choices, and those choices are “typically violated in practice”, especially once the hyperparameters are tuned by cross-validation.

Why τ(x) is hard, and how to lower your sights

Chernozhukov and coauthors put the difficulty bluntly. This is “the first time” in the book that the causal target “is actually a function” (Chernozhukov et al. 2024). Even if you could see both counterfactuals, estimating \tau(x) would be as hard as a high-dimensional regression of Y(1)-Y(0) on X. A deep result makes it worse: there are no adaptive confidence bands for the whole curve \tau_0(x) except under very restrictive assumptions (Genovese and Wasserman 2008) (Chernozhukov et al. 2024). So we have to “re-calibrate our expectations and potentially relax our goals.” The book offers four ways to do that (Chernozhukov et al. 2024):

  1. Infer a summary, not the curve. Project \tau(x) onto a few pre-specified features to get a best linear approximation or a set of group effects. These come with ordinary confidence intervals.
  2. Infer other summaries, such as the effect among the top responders or the value of a policy.
  3. Pointwise intervals from causal forests. These work only for low-dimensional X, with the brittleness noted above.
  4. Give up on intervals. Aim for a \tau(x) with small prediction error, then validate it on held-out data.

The rest of this entry covers routes 1 and 4.

Inference on a summary: best linear predictor and group effects

The key tool is a doubly robust pseudo-outcome for each unit: Y_i(\hat\eta)=\hat g(1,Z_i)-\hat g(0,Z_i)+\Big(\tfrac{D_i}{\hat\mu(Z_i)}-\tfrac{1-D_i}{1-\hat\mu(Z_i)}\Big)\big(Y_i-\hat g(D_i,Z_i)\big), with the outcome model \hat g and the propensity \hat\mu cross-fitted (Chernozhukov et al. 2024). It is the AIPW score computed one unit at a time. Each Y_i(\hat\eta) is a very noisy estimate of that unit’s effect, but its conditional mean is exactly \tau_0(X). It is also Neyman-orthogonal, so small errors in the two nuisances do not bias it to first order (Chernozhukov et al. 2024).

That turns CATE inference into ordinary regression. Regress Y_i(\hat\eta) on a handful of chosen features p(X_i) with OLS and robust standard errors. The result is the best linear predictor (BLP) of \tau(x) in those features, and it has the same large-sample behaviour as if the nuisances were known (Chernozhukov et al. 2024). If the features are group dummies, such as income quintiles, the BLP coefficients are the group average treatment effects (GATEs) (Chernozhukov et al. 2024). You get pointwise intervals for one pre-chosen group and wider simultaneous bands that cover all of them at once.

Two running examples from the book show the scope:

  • 401(k) eligibility and net financial assets. GATEs by income quintile, and a polynomial in log income, show the effect rising with income (Chernozhukov et al. 2024). Forests trained on the same pseudo-outcomes trace a sigmoid curve between roughly $5k and $22k of income, with very wide uncertainty in the top tail (Chernozhukov et al. 2024).
  • The “welfare” wording experiment. The General Social Survey randomly asked respondents about spending on “welfare programs” or on “assistance to the poor”. Same meaning, different words. Across 12,907 respondents from 1986–2010, with 42 covariates, the simple two-means ATE of the “welfare” wording is −0.3681 (SE 0.007) (Chernozhukov et al. 2024). Both kinds of forest single out political views as the most important moderator.

The meta-learners: five ways to build τ(x) from any ML method

Route 4 asks for the best possible estimate of the curve. The book’s answer is meta-learning: treat any regression or classification method as a black-box “oracle”, and combine oracle calls into an estimate of \tau(x) (Chernozhukov et al. 2024). The recipes differ in what they ask the oracle to predict:

Learner Recipe Strength Weakness
S (single) one model of Y on (D,Z); \hat\tau=\hat g(1,Z)-\hat g(0,Z) low variance can “overly regularize the treatment variable”, shrinking a small effect toward zero
T (two) separate models for treated and controls; subtract simple; good when both arms are well represented errors in two outcome models add up
X (cross) impute each unit’s effect from the other arm’s model, then blend the two by the propensity shines when one arm is rare and the effect is simpler than the baselines still essentially outcome modelling
DR (doubly robust) regress the pseudo-outcome Y(\hat\eta) on X the error depends only on the product of nuisance errors unstable with extreme propensities
R (residual) regress \check Y=Y-\hat h(Z) on \check D=D-\hat\mu(Z), with the slope allowed to vary in X Neyman-orthogonal and stable needs an accurate propensity; targets an overlap-weighted projection, not \tau^* itself

(Chernozhukov et al. 2024)

The R-learner is the DML partialling-out idea from above, with the slope allowed to vary. It minimises \mathbb E[(\check Y-\tau(X)\check D)^2], which is a weighted regression of \check Y/\check D with weights \check D^2 (Chernozhukov et al. 2024). Its weakness shows when \tau is misspecified. It then converges to the approximation that best fits units with plenty of overlap, and for the R-learner propensity accuracy “cannot be compensated by more accurate estimation of the outcome regression” (Chernozhukov et al. 2024).

Which learner wins depends on the problem. The book’s simulations make this concrete (Chernozhukov et al. 2024). All use 500 units, and in all of them only 5% (or 95%) of units are treated:

  • DGP 1: a constant effect of 0.5 on top of a baseline with a step in it. The R-learner’s RMSE is 0.002, against 0.038 for the T-learner.
  • DGP 2: the effect exists only for Z\in[0.6,0.8], and treatment is rare. Here the R-learner is the worst (0.374).
  • DGP 3: the same as DGP 2, but treatment is common. The T-learner (0.036) and the DR-learner (0.049) do best.

Hence the honest summary: “there is no clear winner among the X-, R- and DR-Learner methods” (Chernozhukov et al. 2024).

Choosing and stacking learners

If no learner wins everywhere, you need a way to choose. You cannot score a CATE model against the truth, because Y(1)-Y(0) is never observed. But the pseudo-outcome stands in for it. The DR loss \mathbb E_n[(Y(\hat\eta)-\tau(X))^2] ranks candidate models consistently, since the differences in loss between models target the differences in their true mean squared error. A theorem in the book gives confidence intervals for those differences (Chernozhukov et al. 2024). Instead of picking one model you can stack them. Q-aggregation, a convex-weight scheme, stays “consistently close to the best performer across the board” in the simulations: 0.017, 0.165 and 0.037 RMSE on the three DGPs (Chernozhukov et al. 2024).

Two cautions come with this. First, the stacked ensemble “does not come with confidence intervals”, and it can shift with the data split and the random seed (Chernozhukov et al. 2024). Second, the evidence may simply not be there. In the 401(k) data the authors “cannot statistically conclude” that any meta-learner predicts effects better than a constant (Chernozhukov et al. 2024). A distilled tree still says something sensible: about $6k for lower-income, younger savers and about $13k for high earners. But that description is a hypothesis, not a finding.

Did the model find real heterogeneity? Validation on held-out data

Before anyone acts on a CATE model, the book prescribes diagnostics on a test set that played no part in fitting or choosing the model (Chernozhukov et al. 2024). All of them run on the held-out pseudo-outcomes:

  • The BLP slope test. Regress Y(\hat\eta) on (1,\tau^*(X)). The slope estimates \mathrm{Cov}(\tau_0,\tau^*)/\mathrm{Var}(\tau^*). A significant slope means the model has found real heterogeneity, and a perfectly calibrated model would have a slope of exactly 1 (Chernozhukov et al. 2024).
  • Calibration by group. Split the test set into quartiles of predicted effect and estimate each quartile’s GATE from the pseudo-outcomes. A good model’s predicted and estimated group effects line up (Chernozhukov et al. 2024).
  • Uplift curves. The TOC asks how much larger the effect is among the top q share of predicted responders than on average. The Qini scales that by the share treated. The areas under them (AUTOC, AUQC) give one-sided tests for useful targeting (Chernozhukov et al. 2024). This is the same RATE logic as in heterogeneous effects in experiments, now with doubly robust scores so that it also works with observational data.

The results show how much the data can and cannot say (Chernozhukov et al. 2024):

  • 401(k) (9,716 households, split 60/20/20). The BLP slope is 2.24 (95% CI 0.83 to 3.65), so the model carries real signal. The AUTOC is 5,229 (SE 1,472). The authors warn that the specific numbers varied a lot depending on the random split.
  • “Welfare” wording. The slope is 1.47 (CI 0.94 to 1.99), which includes the ideal value of 1. A shallow tree distilled from the ensemble finds the least adverse reaction among left-leaning respondents with a four-year degree, and the most adverse among right-leaning respondents who do not identify as Black. Political views alone create less variation than the combination of views and education.

Validation like this is what turns a pretty CATE plot into evidence.

Policy learning

The point of knowing \tau(x) is often to act. Policy learning turns CATEs into an assignment rule \pi(x). (Chernozhukov et al. 2024) The unconstrained optimum is simple — treat everyone with a positive predicted effect: \pi^*(x)=\mathbf 1\{\tau(x)\ge 0\}. Framed as a classification problem, it predicts the sign of the effect, weighted by its magnitude — misclassifying a large positive effect costs more than fumbling a near-zero one (cost-sensitive classification). (Chernozhukov et al. 2024) This is where causal inference meets decision-making: not “what is the effect?” but “whom should we treat?”

The cautions: ML is not a license

The most important sentence in the chapter: machine learning does not fix identification. (Chernozhukov et al. 2024) Every method here delivers causal answers only if the identifying assumptions still hold — conditional exogeneity (unconfoundedness) and overlap 0<\Pr(D=1\mid X)<1. DML and causal forests make you efficient and flexible under those assumptions; they cannot conjure them. Throwing a forest at confounded data yields a beautifully estimated biased effect. Two further risks: (Chernozhukov et al. 2024)

  • Fairness. ML models absorb the prejudices in their training data (gender and racial bias in text embeddings, biased historical decisions) — a Part IV worry with higher stakes when the output assigns treatment.
  • Interpretability ≠ mechanism. Tools like SHAP or distilling a forest into a shallow tree explain how the model guesses, not the causal mechanism of the world. (Chernozhukov et al. 2024)

Common pitfalls

WarningWhere this goes wrong

  • Naive ML on the outcome. Regularization shrinks small treatment effects toward zero. Orthogonalise (DML) — estimate the effect, not Y. (Chernozhukov et al. 2024)

  • Skipping cross-fitting. Using a unit’s own data to fit its nuisance reintroduces overfitting bias into the effect. Split the folds. (Chernozhukov et al. 2024)

  • Dishonest forests. Choosing splits and estimating effects on the same data gives invalid, overconfident intervals on \tau(x). Use honest estimation. (Chernozhukov et al. 2024)

  • Believing ML earns identification. Unconfoundedness and overlap are still required; ML buys flexibility, not exogeneity. (Chernozhukov et al. 2024)

  • Acting on a biased CATE. A policy rule is only as causal as the \tau(x) beneath it — garbage identification in, harmful targeting out. (Chernozhukov et al. 2024)

  • Promising confidence bands for the whole curve. No adaptive bands exist for \tau_0(x) in general. Put intervals on pre-specified summaries (BLP, GATEs), and validate everything else. (Chernozhukov et al. 2024)

  • Crowning one meta-learner. S, T, X, DR and R each win on some data-generating processes and lose on others. Score them with the DR loss on held-out data, or stack them. (Chernozhukov et al. 2024)

  • Skipping validation. A CATE plot is not evidence of heterogeneity. Run the BLP slope test, calibration and uplift curves on a test set the model never saw, and check that the conclusion survives a different split. (Chernozhukov et al. 2024)

Appendix · the mathematics

The orthogonalised (partialling-out) estimator. In the partially linear model Y=\theta D+g_0(X)+\varepsilon, D=m_0(X)+v, define residuals \tilde Y=Y-\mathbb E[Y\mid X] and \tilde D=D-m_0(X). The DML estimate solves the Neyman-orthogonal moment \hat\theta=\frac{\frac1n\sum_i \tilde D_i\,\tilde Y_i}{\frac1n\sum_i \tilde D_i^2}, each nuisance fit on a separate fold. Orthogonality means \partial_{\eta}\mathbb E[\psi(W;\theta,\eta)]=0 at the truth: small errors \hat g-g_0, \hat m-m_0 enter the estimating equation only at second order, so \hat\theta stays \sqrt n-consistent and asymptotically normal even though the nuisances converge slowly. This is FWL made robust to ML noise.

Honest CATE intervals. Split the data: use sample \mathcal I_1 to grow the tree (choose splits maximising \sum_{\text{children}} n_c\,\hat\tau_c^2, effect heterogeneity), and the independent sample \mathcal I_2 to estimate each leaf’s \hat\tau(x). Because the leaf was not chosen using \mathcal I_2’s outcomes, the leaf estimate is (asymptotically) unbiased with a usable variance — the formal basis for confidence intervals on \tau(x).

Why the pseudo-outcome works. Write H=D/\mu_0(Z)-(1-D)/(1-\mu_0(Z)). With the true nuisances, \mathbb E[H(Y-g_0(D,Z))\mid Z]=0, because the residual has mean zero within each arm and H only reweights the arms. So \mathbb E[Y(\eta_0)\mid Z]=g_0(1,Z)-g_0(0,Z), and averaging over Z given X gives \tau_0(X). With estimated nuisances the bias is \mathbb E[(H(\mu_0)-H(\hat\mu))(\hat g-g_0)\mid X], a product of the two errors. That product is the source of both double robustness and the DR-learner’s error rate, \lesssim r_n^2+\text{Error}(\hat g)^2\,\text{Error}(H(\hat\mu))^2, where r_n is the oracle’s own rate.

BLP and GATEs. \beta_0=\arg\min_\beta\mathbb E[(\tau_0(X)-p(X)'\beta)^2] =(\mathbb E[pp'])^{-1}\mathbb E[p(X)Y(\eta_0)], so OLS of Y_i(\hat\eta) on p(X_i) estimates it, with the sandwich variance \hat Q^{-1}\mathbb E_n[pp'(Y(\hat\eta)-p'\hat\beta)^2]\hat Q^{-1}. If p(X)=(\mathbf 1\{X\in R_1\},\dots,\mathbf 1\{X\in R_K\}) for disjoint groups, then \beta_{0k} is the mean of \tau_0 on R_k, which is exactly the GATE.

The R-loss. In Y=\delta_0(Z)D+g(Z)+\varepsilon, residualising gives \check Y=\delta_0(Z)\check D+\varepsilon. The R-learner minimises \mathbb E[(\check Y-\tau(X)\check D)^2]. If \tau cannot represent \delta_0, the limit is \arg\min_\tau\mathbb E[(\delta_0(Z)-\tau(X))^2\,\mathrm{Var}(D\mid Z)]. Units with propensity near 0 or 1 get almost no weight, which is why the result is an overlap-weighted projection.

The BLP slope test. In the population regression of Y(\eta_0) on (1,\tau^*(X)), the slope is \beta_1=\mathrm{Cov}(\tau_0(X),\tau^*(X))/\mathrm{Var}(\tau^*(X)), because \mathbb E[Y(\eta_0)\mid X]=\tau_0(X). \beta_1\neq0 implies that \tau_0 varies with X. \beta_1=1 holds if \tau^* is calibrated, meaning \mathbb E[\tau_0\mid\tau^*]=\tau^*.

Uplift curves. With \mu(\tau^*,q) the (1-q) quantile of the predictions, \mathrm{TOC}(q)=\mathbb E[Y(1)-Y(0)\mid\tau^*\ge\mu(\tau^*,q)]-\mathrm{ATE} and \mathrm{QINI}(q)=\mathrm{TOC}(q)\cdot P(\tau^*\ge\mu). Both are covariances of the individual effect with an indicator of being in the top group, so both are identified by replacing Y(1)-Y(0) with Y(\eta_0).

Policy value. A rule \pi earns welfare V(\pi)=\mathbb E[\,Y(\pi(X))\,]=\mathbb E[\,\pi(X)\tau(X)\,]+\text{const}, maximised pointwise by \pi^*(x)=\mathbf 1\{\tau(x)\ge0\} — treat exactly where the effect is positive; the regret of any rule is \mathbb E[(\pi^*-\pi)\tau], which weights mistakes by effect size.

The whole of Part V chased a single number: an average effect. But "never cross a river that is on
average four feet deep." Causal machine learning estimates the effect **as a function of who you are** —
the conditional average treatment effect $\tau(x)$ — using the [flexibility of
ML](../04-predictive/trees-and-forests.qmd) without inheriting its bias. The trick is to estimate the
*effect*, not the outcome, and to protect that estimate from overfitting with orthogonalisation and
cross-fitting. It closes the loop: the predictive culture, disciplined back into the service of cause.

## Why it matters

An [ATE](potential-outcomes.qmd) can hide everything that matters for policy: a drug that helps the
young and harms the old can average to "no effect." [@chernozhukov_causalml] Modern social science wants
**heterogeneous treatment effects** — *for whom* does this work? — and the obvious idea, "throw a random
forest at it," **fails** in a way worth understanding deeply. This entry is where
[Part IV's machinery](../04-predictive/interpretability-and-ml.qmd) — regularization, cross-validation,
forests, the bias–variance trade — is **repurposed for causation**, and where the
[double/debiased ML](../04-predictive/interpretability-and-ml.qmd) preview is finally cashed out. It is
the natural summit of the summit: the predictive and causal cultures, reconciled.

## From ATE to CATE

The target shifts from a scalar to a function — the **conditional average treatment effect**:
$$
\tau(x)=\mathbb E\big[Y(1)-Y(0)\mid X=x\big].
$$
$\tau(x)$ is the effect **for units with characteristics $x$** — the engine of **personalised** policy,
letting you move from a uniform rollout to **targeting** whoever benefits most. [@chernozhukov_causalml]
Everything from [potential outcomes](potential-outcomes.qmd) still holds; we've simply stopped averaging
away the variation we now want to see.

## Why naive ML is biased for effects

The tempting move — fit a flexible ML model of $Y$ on $(D,X)$ and read off $D$'s influence — is
**biased**, for a reason that is subtle and important: [@chernozhukov_causalml]

- **Regularization bias.** ML methods are built to minimise *prediction* error for $Y$, so they
  [regularize](../04-predictive/regularization.qmd) — shrinking coefficients, pruning splits — toward
  whatever predicts the outcome best. When the treatment effect is **small relative to other
  predictors**, the algorithm cheerfully **shrinks the treatment's role toward zero**, biasing the
  effect. [@chernozhukov_causalml]
- **Effect ≠ prediction.** Estimating an effect is *fundamentally harder* than predicting an outcome:
  it targets the **difference between two counterfactual states**, only one of which is ever observed.
  [@chernozhukov_causalml] A model superb at predicting $Y$ can be terrible at isolating $\partial
  Y/\partial D$.

The fix is to stop estimating the outcome and start estimating the **effect**, with the nuisances
*partialled out*.

## Double/debiased machine learning

**Double machine learning (DML)**, due to Chernozhukov and coauthors, lets you use any flexible ML for
the hard prediction parts while keeping **valid inference** on the causal parameter.
[@chernozhukov_causalml] Three moves:

1. **Estimate two nuisance functions** with ML: an **outcome model** $g_0(X)=\mathbb E[Y\mid X]$ and a
   **treatment/propensity model** $m_0(X)=\mathbb E[D\mid X]$. [@chernozhukov_causalml]
2. **Orthogonalise (partial out).** Residualise *both* $Y$ and $D$ on $X$ — regress the $Y$-residual on
   the $D$-residual. This is [Frisch–Waugh–Lovell](../03-regression/multiple-regression.qmd) with ML
   doing the conditioning, and it yields a **Neyman-orthogonal** moment: the effect estimate is
   **first-order insensitive** to small errors in $g_0$ and $m_0$. [@chernozhukov_causalml] You can
   plug in imperfect ML nuisances and the errors **don't spill** into the treatment effect — the
   "debiasing."
3. **Cross-fit.** Estimate the nuisances on one fold and the effect on a *held-out* fold, so no unit's
   own outcome contaminates its own nuisance prediction — removing overfitting bias.
   [@chernozhukov_causalml] (Exactly the [cross-validation](../04-predictive/cross-validation.qmd)
   discipline, now load-bearing for *inference*, not just tuning.)

Orthogonality kills the *regularization* bias; cross-fitting kills the *overfitting* bias. Together they
let ML serve identification instead of sabotaging it. The figure shows the payoff DML and causal forests
deliver — recovering an effect that **varies with $x$**.

```{python}
#| label: fig-cate
#| fig-cap: "Heterogeneous treatment effects. The true CATE τ(x) (solid grey line) rises with a covariate x, so the treatment helps high-x units far more than low-x units. The ATE (light dashed line) hides this completely. An honest, binned estimator (ink points, with 95% intervals) recovers τ(x) by comparing treated and control outcomes within strata of x. That is the intuition behind causal forests, which split to maximise effect heterogeneity and estimate honestly for valid intervals. Binning by x is also a GATE estimator, the simplest version of the 'inference on a summary' route below."
#| code-fold: true
#| code-summary: "Show the figure code"

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(21)
n = 6000
x = rng.uniform(0, 1, n)
D = rng.integers(0, 2, n)                  # randomized treatment (clean identification)
tau_true = 0.5 + 3.0 * x                   # effect grows with x (heterogeneous)
y = 1.0 + 2.0 * x + tau_true * D + rng.normal(0, 1.0, n)

# Honest binned CATE: within each x-bin, treated mean - control mean (+ 95% CI).
edges = np.linspace(0, 1, 11)
centers, est, lo, hi = [], [], [], []
for a, b in zip(edges[:-1], edges[1:]):
    m = (x >= a) & (x < b)
    yt, yc = y[m & (D == 1)], y[m & (D == 0)]
    d = yt.mean() - yc.mean()
    se = np.sqrt(yt.var(ddof=1)/yt.size + yc.var(ddof=1)/yc.size)
    centers.append((a+b)/2); est.append(d); lo.append(d-1.96*se); hi.append(d+1.96*se)
centers, est = np.array(centers), np.array(est)
ate = (y[D==1].mean() - y[D==0].mean())

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7.2, 4.2))
xs = np.linspace(0, 1, 100)
ax.plot(xs, 0.5 + 3.0*xs, color="#5e5e54", lw=2.4, label="true CATE  τ(x)")
ax.errorbar(centers, est, yerr=[est-np.array(lo), np.array(hi)-est], fmt="o",
            color="#161613", ms=5, capsize=3, label="honest binned estimate")
ax.axhline(ate, color="#b7b6a8", ls="--", lw=1.6, label=f"ATE = {ate:.2f} (hides heterogeneity)")
ax.set_xlabel("covariate x"); ax.set_ylabel("treatment effect")
ax.set_title("Who benefits? The effect as a function of x")
ax.legend(fontsize=8, frameon=False, loc="upper left")
fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

## Causal forests and honesty

To estimate $\tau(x)$ flexibly, **causal forests** (generalized random forests) adapt
[random forests](../04-predictive/trees-and-forests.qmd) with two changes: [@chernozhukov_causalml]

- **Causal splitting.** Instead of splitting to reduce prediction error (RSS), trees split to
  **maximise the heterogeneity of the estimated treatment effect** across the children — actively
  hunting for the covariates along which the effect *varies*. [@chernozhukov_causalml]
- **Honesty.** A tree is **honest** if it uses **one subsample to choose the splits** and a **separate
  subsample to estimate the effect** in each leaf. [@chernozhukov_causalml] This separation is what
  makes the leaf estimates statistically *valid* — it earns trustworthy **confidence intervals** on
  $\tau(x)$, instead of the overconfident ones you'd get from estimating effects in the very leaves you
  carved to maximise them.

Even honest forests come with a warning. Their confidence intervals are "more brittle and are not as
assumption-lean as inference based on OLS" [@chernozhukov_causalml]. They are valid only for
low-dimensional $X$ and particular hyperparameter choices, and those choices are "typically violated in
practice", especially once the hyperparameters are tuned by cross-validation.

## Why τ(x) is hard, and how to lower your sights

Chernozhukov and coauthors put the difficulty bluntly. This is "the first time" in the book that the
causal target "is actually a function" [@chernozhukov_causalml]. Even if you could see both
counterfactuals, estimating $\tau(x)$ would be as hard as a high-dimensional regression of $Y(1)-Y(0)$ on
$X$. A deep result makes it worse: there are no **adaptive confidence bands** for the whole curve
$\tau_0(x)$ except under very restrictive assumptions (Genovese and Wasserman 2008)
[@chernozhukov_causalml]. So we have to "re-calibrate our expectations and potentially relax our goals."
The book offers four ways to do that [@chernozhukov_causalml]:

1. **Infer a summary, not the curve.** Project $\tau(x)$ onto a few pre-specified features to get a best
   linear approximation or a set of group effects. These come with ordinary confidence intervals.
2. **Infer other summaries**, such as the effect among the top responders or the value of a policy.
3. **Pointwise intervals from causal forests.** These work only for low-dimensional $X$, with the
   brittleness noted above.
4. **Give up on intervals.** Aim for a $\tau(x)$ with small prediction error, then *validate* it on
   held-out data.

The rest of this entry covers routes 1 and 4.

## Inference on a summary: best linear predictor and group effects

The key tool is a **doubly robust pseudo-outcome** for each unit:
$$
Y_i(\hat\eta)=\hat g(1,Z_i)-\hat g(0,Z_i)+\Big(\tfrac{D_i}{\hat\mu(Z_i)}-\tfrac{1-D_i}{1-\hat\mu(Z_i)}\Big)\big(Y_i-\hat g(D_i,Z_i)\big),
$$
with the outcome model $\hat g$ and the propensity $\hat\mu$ cross-fitted
[@chernozhukov_causalml]. It is the [AIPW](combining-and-triangulating-designs.qmd) score computed one unit
at a time. Each $Y_i(\hat\eta)$ is a very noisy estimate of that unit's effect, but its conditional mean is
exactly $\tau_0(X)$. It is also Neyman-orthogonal, so small errors in the two nuisances do not bias it to
first order [@chernozhukov_causalml].

That turns CATE inference into ordinary regression. Regress $Y_i(\hat\eta)$ on a handful of chosen
features $p(X_i)$ with OLS and robust standard errors. The result is the **best linear predictor (BLP)**
of $\tau(x)$ in those features, and it has the same large-sample behaviour as if the nuisances were known
[@chernozhukov_causalml]. If the features are group dummies, such as income quintiles, the BLP
coefficients *are* the **group average treatment effects (GATEs)** [@chernozhukov_causalml]. You get
pointwise intervals for one pre-chosen group and wider simultaneous bands that cover all of them at once.

Two running examples from the book show the scope:

- **401(k) eligibility** and net financial assets. GATEs by income quintile, and a polynomial in log
  income, show the effect rising with income [@chernozhukov_causalml]. Forests trained on the same
  pseudo-outcomes trace a sigmoid curve between roughly \$5k and \$22k of income, with very wide
  uncertainty in the top tail [@chernozhukov_causalml].
- **The "welfare" wording experiment.** The General Social Survey randomly asked respondents about
  spending on "welfare programs" or on "assistance to the poor". Same meaning, different words. Across
  12,907 respondents from 1986–2010, with 42 covariates, the simple two-means ATE of the "welfare"
  wording is **−0.3681** (SE 0.007) [@chernozhukov_causalml]. Both kinds of forest single out political
  views as the most important moderator.

## The meta-learners: five ways to build τ(x) from any ML method

Route 4 asks for the best possible estimate of the *curve*. The book's answer is **meta-learning**: treat
any regression or classification method as a black-box "oracle", and combine oracle calls into an
estimate of $\tau(x)$ [@chernozhukov_causalml]. The recipes differ in what they ask the oracle to
predict:

| Learner | Recipe | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| **S** (single) | one model of $Y$ on $(D,Z)$; $\hat\tau=\hat g(1,Z)-\hat g(0,Z)$ | low variance | can "overly regularize the treatment variable", shrinking a small effect toward zero |
| **T** (two) | separate models for treated and controls; subtract | simple; good when both arms are well represented | errors in two outcome models add up |
| **X** (cross) | impute each unit's effect from the *other* arm's model, then blend the two by the propensity | shines when one arm is rare and the effect is simpler than the baselines | still essentially outcome modelling |
| **DR** (doubly robust) | regress the pseudo-outcome $Y(\hat\eta)$ on $X$ | the error depends only on the *product* of nuisance errors | unstable with extreme propensities |
| **R** (residual) | regress $\check Y=Y-\hat h(Z)$ on $\check D=D-\hat\mu(Z)$, with the slope allowed to vary in $X$ | Neyman-orthogonal and stable | needs an accurate propensity; targets an **overlap-weighted** projection, not $\tau^*$ itself |

[@chernozhukov_causalml]

The R-learner is the DML [partialling-out](../04-predictive/double-lasso.qmd) idea from above, with the
slope allowed to vary. It minimises $\mathbb E[(\check Y-\tau(X)\check D)^2]$, which is a weighted regression of
$\check Y/\check D$ with weights $\check D^2$ [@chernozhukov_causalml]. Its weakness shows when $\tau$ is
misspecified. It then converges to the approximation that best fits units with plenty of overlap, and for
the R-learner propensity accuracy "cannot be compensated by more accurate estimation of the outcome
regression" [@chernozhukov_causalml].

Which learner wins depends on the problem. The book's simulations make this concrete
[@chernozhukov_causalml]. All use 500 units, and in all of them only 5% (or 95%) of units are treated:

- **DGP 1:** a constant effect of 0.5 on top of a baseline with a step in it. The R-learner's RMSE is 0.002, against 0.038
  for the T-learner.
- **DGP 2:** the effect exists only for $Z\in[0.6,0.8]$, and treatment is rare. Here the R-learner is the
  *worst* (0.374).
- **DGP 3:** the same as DGP 2, but treatment is common. The T-learner (0.036) and the DR-learner (0.049)
  do best.

Hence the honest summary: "there is no clear winner among the X-, R- and DR-Learner methods"
[@chernozhukov_causalml].

## Choosing and stacking learners

If no learner wins everywhere, you need a way to choose. You cannot score a CATE model against the truth,
because $Y(1)-Y(0)$ is never observed. But the pseudo-outcome stands in for it. The **DR loss**
$\mathbb E_n[(Y(\hat\eta)-\tau(X))^2]$ ranks candidate models consistently, since the differences in loss
between models target the differences in their true mean squared error. A theorem in the book gives
confidence intervals for those differences [@chernozhukov_causalml]. Instead of picking one model you can
**stack** them. Q-aggregation, a convex-weight scheme, stays "consistently close to the best performer
across the board" in the simulations: 0.017, 0.165 and 0.037 RMSE on the three DGPs
[@chernozhukov_causalml].

Two cautions come with this. First, the stacked ensemble "does not come with confidence intervals", and
it can shift with the data split and the random seed [@chernozhukov_causalml]. Second, the evidence may
simply not be there. In the 401(k) data the authors "cannot statistically conclude" that any meta-learner
predicts effects better than a constant [@chernozhukov_causalml]. A distilled tree still says something
sensible: about \$6k for lower-income, younger savers and about \$13k for high earners. But that
description is a hypothesis, not a finding.

## Did the model find real heterogeneity? Validation on held-out data

Before anyone acts on a CATE model, the book prescribes diagnostics on a **test set** that played no part
in fitting or choosing the model [@chernozhukov_causalml]. All of them run on the held-out pseudo-outcomes:

- **The BLP slope test.** Regress $Y(\hat\eta)$ on $(1,\tau^*(X))$. The slope estimates
  $\mathrm{Cov}(\tau_0,\tau^*)/\mathrm{Var}(\tau^*)$. A significant slope means the model has found real
  heterogeneity, and a perfectly calibrated model would have a slope of exactly 1 [@chernozhukov_causalml].
- **Calibration by group.** Split the test set into quartiles of predicted effect and estimate each
  quartile's GATE from the pseudo-outcomes. A good model's predicted and estimated group effects line up
  [@chernozhukov_causalml].
- **Uplift curves.** The **TOC** asks how much larger the effect is among the top $q$ share of predicted
  responders than on average. The **Qini** scales that by the share treated. The areas under them (AUTOC,
  AUQC) give one-sided tests for useful targeting [@chernozhukov_causalml]. This is the same RATE logic
  as in [heterogeneous effects in experiments](heterogeneous-effects-in-experiments.qmd), now with
  doubly robust scores so that it also works with observational data.

The results show how much the data can and cannot say [@chernozhukov_causalml]:

- **401(k)** (9,716 households, split 60/20/20). The BLP slope is 2.24 (95% CI 0.83 to 3.65), so the model
  carries real signal. The AUTOC is 5,229 (SE 1,472). The authors warn that the specific numbers varied a
  lot depending on the random split.
- **"Welfare" wording.** The slope is 1.47 (CI 0.94 to 1.99), which includes the ideal value of 1. A
  shallow tree distilled from the ensemble finds the least adverse reaction among left-leaning
  respondents with a four-year degree, and the most adverse among right-leaning respondents who do not
  identify as Black. Political views alone create less variation than the combination of views and
  education.

Validation like this is what turns a pretty CATE plot into evidence.

## Policy learning

The point of knowing $\tau(x)$ is often to **act**. **Policy learning** turns CATEs into an assignment
rule $\pi(x)$. [@chernozhukov_causalml] The unconstrained optimum is simple — *treat everyone with a
positive predicted effect*:
$$
\pi^*(x)=\mathbf 1\{\tau(x)\ge 0\}.
$$
Framed as a [classification](../04-predictive/classification-and-knn.qmd) problem, it predicts the
**sign** of the effect, **weighted by its magnitude** — misclassifying a *large* positive effect costs
more than fumbling a near-zero one (cost-sensitive classification). [@chernozhukov_causalml] This is
where causal inference meets [decision-making](../06-field-realities/index.qmd): not "what is the
effect?" but "whom should we treat?"

## The cautions: ML is not a license

The most important sentence in the chapter: **machine learning does not fix identification.**
[@chernozhukov_causalml] Every method here delivers *causal* answers only if the
[identifying assumptions still hold](matching-and-propensity-scores.qmd) — **conditional exogeneity
(unconfoundedness)** and **overlap** $0<\Pr(D=1\mid X)<1$. DML and causal forests make you *efficient
and flexible* under those assumptions; they cannot conjure them. Throwing a forest at confounded data
yields a beautifully estimated **biased** effect. Two further risks: [@chernozhukov_causalml]

- **Fairness.** ML models absorb the prejudices in their training data (gender and racial bias in text
  embeddings, biased historical decisions) — a [Part IV worry](../04-predictive/interpretability-and-ml.qmd)
  with higher stakes when the output *assigns treatment*.
- **Interpretability ≠ mechanism.** Tools like [SHAP](../04-predictive/interpretability-and-ml.qmd) or
  distilling a forest into a shallow tree explain **how the model guesses**, not the **causal
  mechanism** of the world. [@chernozhukov_causalml]

## Common pitfalls

::: {.callout-warning title="Where this goes wrong"}

- **Naive ML on the outcome.** Regularization shrinks small treatment effects toward zero. Orthogonalise
  (DML) — estimate the *effect*, not $Y$. [@chernozhukov_causalml]

- **Skipping cross-fitting.** Using a unit's own data to fit its nuisance reintroduces overfitting bias
  into the effect. Split the folds. [@chernozhukov_causalml]

- **Dishonest forests.** Choosing splits *and* estimating effects on the same data gives invalid,
  overconfident intervals on $\tau(x)$. Use honest estimation. [@chernozhukov_causalml]

- **Believing ML earns identification.** Unconfoundedness and overlap are still required; ML buys
  flexibility, not exogeneity. [@chernozhukov_causalml]

- **Acting on a biased CATE.** A policy rule is only as causal as the $\tau(x)$ beneath it — garbage
  identification in, harmful targeting out. [@chernozhukov_causalml]

- **Promising confidence bands for the whole curve.** No adaptive bands exist for $\tau_0(x)$ in general.
  Put intervals on pre-specified summaries (BLP, GATEs), and validate everything else.
  [@chernozhukov_causalml]

- **Crowning one meta-learner.** S, T, X, DR and R each win on some data-generating processes and lose on
  others. Score them with the DR loss on held-out data, or stack them. [@chernozhukov_causalml]

- **Skipping validation.** A CATE plot is not evidence of heterogeneity. Run the BLP slope test,
  calibration and uplift curves on a test set the model never saw, and check that the conclusion
  survives a different split. [@chernozhukov_causalml]

:::

## Appendix · the mathematics {.unnumbered}

::: {.math-appendix}

**The orthogonalised (partialling-out) estimator.** In the partially linear model
$Y=\theta D+g_0(X)+\varepsilon$, $D=m_0(X)+v$, define residuals $\tilde Y=Y-\mathbb E[Y\mid X]$ and
$\tilde D=D-m_0(X)$. The DML estimate solves the **Neyman-orthogonal** moment
$$
\hat\theta=\frac{\frac1n\sum_i \tilde D_i\,\tilde Y_i}{\frac1n\sum_i \tilde D_i^2},
$$
each nuisance fit on a *separate fold*. Orthogonality means $\partial_{\eta}\mathbb E[\psi(W;\theta,\eta)]=0$
at the truth: small errors $\hat g-g_0$, $\hat m-m_0$ enter the estimating equation only at **second
order**, so $\hat\theta$ stays $\sqrt n$-consistent and asymptotically normal even though the nuisances
converge slowly. This is [FWL](../03-regression/multiple-regression.qmd) made robust to ML noise.

**Honest CATE intervals.** Split the data: use sample $\mathcal I_1$ to grow the tree (choose splits
maximising $\sum_{\text{children}} n_c\,\hat\tau_c^2$, effect heterogeneity), and the *independent*
sample $\mathcal I_2$ to estimate each leaf's $\hat\tau(x)$. Because the leaf was not chosen using
$\mathcal I_2$'s outcomes, the leaf estimate is (asymptotically) unbiased with a usable variance — the
formal basis for confidence intervals on $\tau(x)$.

**Why the pseudo-outcome works.** Write $H=D/\mu_0(Z)-(1-D)/(1-\mu_0(Z))$. With the true nuisances,
$\mathbb E[H(Y-g_0(D,Z))\mid Z]=0$, because the residual has mean zero within each arm and $H$ only
reweights the arms. So $\mathbb E[Y(\eta_0)\mid Z]=g_0(1,Z)-g_0(0,Z)$, and averaging over $Z$ given $X$
gives $\tau_0(X)$. With estimated nuisances the bias is
$\mathbb E[(H(\mu_0)-H(\hat\mu))(\hat g-g_0)\mid X]$, a **product** of the two errors. That product is the
source of both double robustness and the DR-learner's error rate,
$\lesssim r_n^2+\text{Error}(\hat g)^2\,\text{Error}(H(\hat\mu))^2$, where $r_n$ is the oracle's own rate.

**BLP and GATEs.** $\beta_0=\arg\min_\beta\mathbb E[(\tau_0(X)-p(X)'\beta)^2]
=(\mathbb E[pp'])^{-1}\mathbb E[p(X)Y(\eta_0)]$, so OLS of $Y_i(\hat\eta)$ on $p(X_i)$ estimates it, with
the sandwich variance $\hat Q^{-1}\mathbb E_n[pp'(Y(\hat\eta)-p'\hat\beta)^2]\hat Q^{-1}$. If
$p(X)=(\mathbf 1\{X\in R_1\},\dots,\mathbf 1\{X\in R_K\})$ for disjoint groups, then $\beta_{0k}$ is the mean
of $\tau_0$ on $R_k$, which is exactly the GATE.

**The R-loss.** In $Y=\delta_0(Z)D+g(Z)+\varepsilon$, residualising gives $\check Y=\delta_0(Z)\check D+\varepsilon$.
The R-learner minimises $\mathbb E[(\check Y-\tau(X)\check D)^2]$. If $\tau$ cannot represent $\delta_0$, the
limit is $\arg\min_\tau\mathbb E[(\delta_0(Z)-\tau(X))^2\,\mathrm{Var}(D\mid Z)]$. Units with propensity near 0
or 1 get almost no weight, which is why the result is an overlap-weighted projection.

**The BLP slope test.** In the population regression of $Y(\eta_0)$ on $(1,\tau^*(X))$, the slope is
$\beta_1=\mathrm{Cov}(\tau_0(X),\tau^*(X))/\mathrm{Var}(\tau^*(X))$, because $\mathbb E[Y(\eta_0)\mid X]=\tau_0(X)$.
$\beta_1\neq0$ implies that $\tau_0$ varies with $X$. $\beta_1=1$ holds if $\tau^*$ is calibrated, meaning
$\mathbb E[\tau_0\mid\tau^*]=\tau^*$.

**Uplift curves.** With $\mu(\tau^*,q)$ the $(1-q)$ quantile of the predictions,
$\mathrm{TOC}(q)=\mathbb E[Y(1)-Y(0)\mid\tau^*\ge\mu(\tau^*,q)]-\mathrm{ATE}$ and
$\mathrm{QINI}(q)=\mathrm{TOC}(q)\cdot P(\tau^*\ge\mu)$. Both are covariances of the individual effect with an
indicator of being in the top group, so both are identified by replacing $Y(1)-Y(0)$ with $Y(\eta_0)$.

**Policy value.** A rule $\pi$ earns welfare
$V(\pi)=\mathbb E[\,Y(\pi(X))\,]=\mathbb E[\,\pi(X)\tau(X)\,]+\text{const}$, maximised pointwise by
$\pi^*(x)=\mathbf 1\{\tau(x)\ge0\}$ — treat exactly where the effect is positive; the regret of any rule
is $\mathbb E[(\pi^*-\pi)\tau]$, which weights mistakes by effect size.

:::
