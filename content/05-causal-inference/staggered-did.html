Staggered Difference-in-Differences

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source

When units adopt a policy at different times, the workhorse two-way fixed-effects regression can return the wrong sign — even if the policy helped everyone. The modern fix: only ever compare against clean controls.

The canonical 2×2 DiD has two groups and two periods. Real policies roll out staggered — states adopt in different years. For two decades the reflex was a two-way fixed-effects (TWFE) regression with one treatment coefficient. A wave of work since 2018 showed that, under staggered timing and effects that change over time, TWFE is a contaminated weighted average that can be biased to the wrong sign. The cure is conceptually simple: build the estimate only from clean controls — units not yet (or never) treated.

Why it matters

Staggered adoption is the default in panel policy evaluation — minimum-wage laws, Medicaid expansions, school reforms. If your identification strategy is “DiD with TWFE and a single treatment dummy,” the recent literature says your estimate may be uninterpretable. This entry is the upgrade: what breaks, why, and which estimator to use instead. It is the modern frontier of the DiD design and a centrepiece of credible panel causal inference.

Generalising 2×2 to staggered timing

The 2×2 DiD compares the change in a treated group to the change in an untreated group, valid under parallel trends — absent treatment, both groups’ average outcomes would have moved in parallel (Baker et al. 2026). With staggered adoption, units enter treatment at different dates G_i, forming multiple treatment cohorts (Baker et al. 2026). The framework generalises by indexing potential outcomes by when a unit is first treated, Y_{i,t}(g), and the targets become group-time average treatment effects (Baker et al. 2026):

\text{ATT}(g,t) = \mathbb{E}\!\left[\,Y_{i,t}(g) - Y_{i,t}(\infty)\ \middle|\ G_i = g\right],

the effect, on cohort g at time t, of having started treatment at g. Often parallel trends is only assumed conditional on covariates X — units with similar characteristics would have evolved similarly (Baker et al. 2026).

The TWFE trap: Goodman–Bacon and negative weights

The old standard regresses the outcome on unit and time fixed effects plus a single treatment dummy, reporting one \beta_{\text{TWFE}} (Baker et al. 2026). Goodman-Bacon (2021) proved what that number actually is: a weighted average of every possible 2×2 DiD in the data. The weights are all positive, larger for bigger groups and for groups treated nearer the middle of the panel. (Baker et al. 2026) The comparisons are:

  • treated vs never-treated (clean);
  • early-treated vs later-treated while the latter is still untreated (clean);
  • later-treated vs already-treated early adopters — the forbidden comparison (Baker et al. 2026).

The forbidden comparison is the poison. When effects are dynamic (they grow or fade), an already-treated unit’s outcome is still changing because of its own treatment — so using it as a “control” subtracts a moving target (Baker et al. 2026). Rewrite those comparisons in terms of true effects and some cohort-period effects enter with a minus sign: the estimand puts negative weights on them, even though every 2×2 enters the estimator with a positive weight. The headline result is that these negative weights can flip the sign: \beta_{\text{TWFE}} can come out negative even when every single cohort was helped by the treatment (Baker et al. 2026).

The modern robust estimators

The shared fix: only ever compare treated units to clean controls — units not yet treated or never treated (Baker et al. 2026).

  • Callaway & Sant’Anna (CS). Estimate each \text{ATT}(g,t) building block using only clean controls, then aggregate the blocks into interpretable summaries (e.g. an average effect per year of exposure) (Baker et al. 2026).
  • Sun & Abraham (SA). An interaction-weighted event study: standard TWFE event studies are biased when effects differ across cohorts, so SA saturate the model with cohort-specific event-time dummies, weighting by cohort size (Baker et al. 2026).
  • de Chaisemartin & D’Haultfœuille. Document how pervasive negative weights are and offer a Wald-style estimator that avoids them, focused on instantaneous effects (Baker et al. 2026).

The unifying innovation is the not-yet-treated control: to estimate the 2014 cohort’s effect in 2015, use units that don’t adopt until 2016+ as a valid, uncontaminated comparison (Baker et al. 2026).

Event studies: dynamics and pre-trends

Event-study specifications re-centre time on “event time” e = t - g (e=0 is the adoption year) and are the main tool for both visualising effects and testing assumptions (Baker et al. 2026):

  • Lags (e \ge 0) trace the dynamic trajectory — does the effect grow, plateau, or fade? (Baker et al. 2026)
  • Leads (e < -1) are placebo tests: under parallel trends they should be ≈ 0, indicating no pre-existing divergence between groups (Baker et al. 2026).
Show the figure code 
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
T = 12
units = 600
# assign cohorts: adopt at 4, at 8, or never
g = rng.choice([4, 8, 99], size=units, p=[0.35, 0.35, 0.30])
alpha = rng.normal(0, 1, units)                 # unit fixed effects
delta = np.linspace(0, 2, T) + rng.normal(0, 0.05, T)   # common time trend (parallel)

def true_tau(e):                                # dynamic effect, identical across cohorts
    return 0.4 * (e + 1) if e >= 0 else 0.0

Y = np.empty((units, T))
for i in range(units):
    for t in range(T):
        e = t - g[i]
        eff = true_tau(e) if g[i] != 99 else 0.0
        Y[i, t] = alpha[i] + delta[t] + eff + rng.normal(0, 0.7)

# Callaway–Sant'Anna-style event study with not-yet-treated clean controls
events = np.arange(-3, 4)
est, se = [], []
for e in events:
    atts, vs, ns = [], [], []
    for gg in [4, 8]:
        t = gg + e
        if t < 0 or t >= T:
            continue
        base = gg - 1
        treated = (g == gg)
        ctrl = g > max(t, base)                 # not yet treated by t (or never)
        if treated.sum() < 5 or ctrl.sum() < 5:
            continue
        dt = Y[treated, t] - Y[treated, base]
        dc = Y[ctrl, t] - Y[ctrl, base]
        atts.append(dt.mean() - dc.mean())
        vs.append(dt.var(ddof=1)/treated.sum() + dc.var(ddof=1)/ctrl.sum())
        ns.append(treated.sum())
    w = np.array(ns, float) / sum(ns)           # cohort-size weights
    est.append(np.dot(w, atts)); se.append(np.sqrt(np.dot(w**2, vs)))
est, se = np.array(est), np.array(se)

# the naive TWFE coefficient: Y_it = a_i + d_t + beta*D_it, solved by two-way demeaning
D = np.array([[1.0 if (g[i] != 99 and t >= g[i]) else 0.0 for t in range(T)] for i in range(units)])
def two_way_demean(M):
    return M - M.mean(1, keepdims=True) - M.mean(0, keepdims=True) + M.mean()
Dd, Yd = two_way_demean(D), two_way_demean(Y)
beta_twfe = (Dd * Yd).sum() / (Dd ** 2).sum()
# the target it should summarise: the average true effect over all treated unit-periods
att_true = np.mean([true_tau(t - g[i]) for i in range(units) for t in range(T) if D[i, t] == 1])

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7.4, 4.2))
ax.axvspan(-3.5, -0.5, color="#e4e3d8")
ax.axhline(0, color="#b7b6a8", lw=0.8); ax.axvline(-0.5, color="#b7b6a8", lw=0.8, ls=":")
xs = np.linspace(-3, 3, 100)
ax.plot(xs, [true_tau(int(np.floor(e))) if e >= 0 else 0 for e in xs],
        color="#5e5e54", lw=2.2, label="true dynamic effect τ(e)")
ax.errorbar(events, est, yerr=1.96*se, fmt="o", color="#161613", ms=5, capsize=3,
            label="clean event-study estimate (±1.96 SE)")
ax.axhline(beta_twfe, color="#e8531d", ls="--", lw=1.6,
           label=f"naïve TWFE single coefficient = {beta_twfe:.2f}")
ax.axhline(att_true, color="#5e5e54", ls=":", lw=1.4,
           label=f"true average effect on the treated = {att_true:.2f}")
ax.text(-3.3, 0.32, "pre-trend (placebo)\nleads ≈ 0", fontsize=8, color="#5e5e54")
ax.set_xlabel("event time  e = t − g  (0 = adoption)"); ax.set_ylabel("effect")
ax.set_title("Event study: clean controls recover the dynamics TWFE hides")
ax.set_ylim(-0.15, 2.45)
ax.legend(fontsize=8, frameon=False, loc="upper left")
fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()
WarningDon’t eyeball the pre-trends

Baker et al. caution against the “eye-test” — glancing at the leads and declaring them flat. With many event-time coefficients you are multiple-testing; they recommend formal sensitivity analyses and simultaneous confidence bands rather than a visual verdict (Baker et al. 2026).

Which parallel trends? Choosing the comparison group

With staggered timing, “parallel trends” stops being one assumption. It becomes a family of them, one per choice of comparison group. The guide names three: (Baker et al. 2026)

  • Never-treated only. This avoids changes in the comparison group’s composition and places no restriction on pre-trends. It also avoids comparing against units that may have chosen their adoption date because of recent outcomes. The cost is that never-treated units may be never-treated for reasons tied to their trends. Counties in states that never expanded Medicaid “may be too different” from expanders, and there may be too few of them.
  • Not-yet-treated. Every unit that has not adopted by period t serves as a control. This uses more data than never-treated alone without forcing every pre-period to be parallel. It is the guide’s preferred middle ground, and the one used for Medicaid.
  • All periods and groups. This is the most precise option, but it imposes parallel pre-trends across the whole history. If the pre-trends are not parallel, the estimates are biased. The more efficient imputation estimators (Borusyak–Jaravel–Spiess, Gardner) and Wooldridge’s extended TWFE live here.

Which one is right depends on the setting. The guide’s minimum demand is that you “clearly state the specific parallel trends assumption” you impose, so readers can argue about it. (Baker et al. 2026)

What pre-trends can and cannot tell you

The event-study leads are the most-looked-at and most-misread part of a DiD paper. The guide draws three lessons from recent work (Roth; Rambachan & Roth; Bilinski & Hatfield): (Baker et al. 2026)

  1. Parallel trends is not testable. The assumption concerns untreated outcomes after treatment. Pre-trends measure the same gap “in the ‘wrong’ periods”. Flat leads are reassuring, but they are not the assumption itself.
  2. Pre-tests are usually low-powered. In the 2014 Medicaid cohort, no lead is individually or jointly significant. Yet the leads average about +2.3, larger than all but one post-period estimate. They cannot rule out flat pre-trends, and they cannot rule out large ones either. Running the analysis only when a weak pre-test passes can make the bias worse (Roth 2022). This is the power problem in a new place.
  3. Take a stand on how big a violation could be. Rambachan and Roth bound the effect by assuming post-period violations are no larger than some multiple of the largest pre-period jump. In the Medicaid data the largest one-period pre-trend is about 4 deaths per 100,000. That turns the 2014 estimate of −2.6 into an identified set, -2.6\pm4=[-6.6,\,1.4]. Its robust confidence interval, [-11.1,\,5.1], “spans implausibly large effects in both directions”. The pre-trends simply don’t carry much information. This is the partial-identification mindset applied to DiD.

One more trap: the single summary coefficient. A common shortcut follows the event study with one TWFE regression that has a single post-treatment dummy. In a design with one treated cohort, that coefficient equals the average post-period estimate minus the average pre-period estimate. It therefore relies on parallel trends in every period, not just after treatment. In the 2014 cohort it gives −2.53, while the average of the post-period effects is −0.70. The gap comes from those positive leads. (Baker et al. 2026)

NoteWorked example: Medicaid expansion and adult mortality, all four waves

The guide’s running example ends here. Counties in states that expanded Medicaid in 2014, 2015, 2016 and 2019 are compared with 1,222 not-yet-treated counties, using population weights. Each \text{ATT}(g,t) comes from a doubly robust 2×2. The event-study weights are cohort sizes, and they are very unequal: the 2014 cohort holds 80% of treated adults and the 2016 cohort 3.5%. Note that event time is unbalanced. The 2019 cohort contributes only to e=0, so later event times average over a different mix of cohorts. A balanced aggregation fixes the cohort set when that matters. (Baker et al. 2026)

The overall effect over event times 0–5 is 0.09 deaths per 100,000 (SE 1.92, 95% CI [-3.67,\,3.84]). With covariates and conditional parallel trends it is −2.25 (SE 4.05, [-10.19,\,5.69]). The authors’ conclusion is a model of honest reporting. They do not claim “Medicaid had no effect”. They say the data rule out effects larger than 6 or more negative than −11 deaths per 100,000 over the six years after expansion. (Baker et al. 2026)

A forward-engineering checklist

The guide’s closing message is that complex designs are built from simple ones. Its worked example grows from a 2×2 into five timing groups, eleven years, six covariates, three parallel-trends assumptions and four estimation methods. Its eight steps reverse the old habit of picking a regression first and interpreting it afterwards: (Baker et al. 2026)

  1. Define target parameters. Write down the building-block \text{ATT}s and the summary that aggregates them, including the weights.
  2. State the identification assumptions formally. Which parallel trends, no anticipation, overlap? Then bring evidence that could falsify them.
  3. Choose the estimation method. Sample means, or regression-adjustment, IPW or doubly robust when parallel trends is conditional on covariates. State the extra modelling restrictions each one needs.
  4. Discuss sources of uncertainty. Decide on sampling-based or design-based inference and what is treated as fixed versus random.
  5. Estimate.
  6. Run a sensitivity analysis. How much do the estimates move under plausible-sized violations of step 2?
  7. Run a heterogeneity analysis. Check whether the aggregate hides subgroups, timing differences or different sources of variation.
  8. Keep learning. If the DiD assumptions look implausible, switch designs.

Running a simple TWFE regression is, in the guide’s words, “an implicit choice to reverse engineer a research design from the statistical method”. (Baker et al. 2026)

Common pitfalls

WarningWhere this goes wrong
  • Using TWFE with a single dummy under staggered timing. Forbidden comparisons and negative weights can flip the sign — switch to Callaway–Sant’Anna, Sun–Abraham, or de Chaisemartin–D’Haultfœuille. (Baker et al. 2026)
  • Using already-treated units as controls. Their changing effects contaminate the comparison; restrict to not-yet-/never-treated. (Baker et al. 2026)
  • Reading a TWFE event study at face value. It too is biased across heterogeneous cohorts; use an interaction-weighted (SA) version. (Baker et al. 2026)
  • Eyeballing pre-trends. Use simultaneous bands and sensitivity analysis, not a visual flatness check. (Baker et al. 2026)
  • Treating a passed pre-test as proof. Pre-tests are low-powered, and conditioning on passing one can add bias. Bound the effect under plausible violations (Rambachan–Roth) instead. (Baker et al. 2026)
  • Leaving the parallel-trends assumption unnamed. Never-treated, not-yet-treated and all-periods comparisons rest on different assumptions; say which one you impose. (Baker et al. 2026)
  • Summarising an event study with one TWFE dummy. That coefficient subtracts the average pre-period gap, so it depends on parallel trends in every period (Medicaid: −2.53 vs −0.70). (Baker et al. 2026)
  • Assuming a never-treated group exists. If everyone is eventually treated, lean on not-yet-treated controls and be explicit about the comparison. (Baker et al. 2026)

Appendix · the mathematics

The Goodman-Bacon weights. \hat\beta_{\text{TWFE}} = \sum_{k} s_k\,\widehat{\text{DiD}}_k, summing over all timing-group pairs k. The weights s_k are strictly positive, proportional to group sizes × the variance of the treatment dummy conditional on the fixed effects. (Baker et al. 2026) Sign reversal comes from the estimand. With one group treated in period 1 and one in period 2, the comparison that uses the early group as a control gives \beta_{\text{TWFE}}=\text{ATT}(2,2)+w_1\text{ATT}(1,1)-w_1\text{ATT}(1,2). The later effect of the early group enters negatively unless effects are static. (Baker et al. 2026)

Callaway–Sant’Anna aggregation. Estimate each \widehat{\text{ATT}}(g,t) from clean controls, then form summaries by averaging with chosen weights — e.g. the event-study parameter \theta(e) = \sum_g w_g\,\widehat{\text{ATT}}(g,\,g+e) averages cohorts at a fixed exposure e (the quantity the figure computes), and an overall ATT averages over all (g,t) with t\ge g. Because each block uses only valid comparisons, the aggregate inherits their lack of contamination.

Why leads test parallel trends. For e<0 the “effect” is mechanically zero under the null, so a nonzero estimated lead coefficient signals that treated and control groups were already diverging before treatment — direct evidence against parallel trends (subject to the multiple-testing caveat).

::: {.lead-kernel}
The [canonical 2×2 DiD](difference-in-differences.qmd) has two groups and two periods. Real policies roll
out **staggered** — states adopt in different years. For two decades the reflex was a **two-way
fixed-effects (TWFE)** regression with one treatment coefficient. A wave of work since 2018 showed that,
under staggered timing *and* effects that change over time, **TWFE is a contaminated weighted average** that
can be **biased to the wrong sign**. The cure is conceptually simple: build the estimate only from
**clean controls** — units not yet (or never) treated.
:::

## Why it matters

Staggered adoption is the *default* in panel policy evaluation — minimum-wage laws, Medicaid expansions,
school reforms. If your identification strategy is "DiD with TWFE and a single treatment dummy," the recent
literature says your estimate may be **uninterpretable**. This entry is the upgrade: what breaks, why, and
which estimator to use instead. It is the modern frontier of the [DiD design](difference-in-differences.qmd)
and a centrepiece of credible panel causal inference.

## Generalising 2×2 to staggered timing

The 2×2 DiD compares the change in a treated group to the change in an untreated group, valid under
**parallel trends** — absent treatment, both groups' average outcomes would have moved in parallel
[@did_guide]. With staggered adoption, units enter treatment at **different dates $G_i$**, forming multiple
**treatment cohorts** [@did_guide]. The framework generalises by indexing potential outcomes by *when* a
unit is first treated, $Y_{i,t}(g)$, and the targets become **group-time average treatment effects**
[@did_guide]:

$$
\text{ATT}(g,t) = \mathbb{E}\!\left[\,Y_{i,t}(g) - Y_{i,t}(\infty)\ \middle|\ G_i = g\right],
$$

the effect, on cohort $g$ at time $t$, of having started treatment at $g$. Often parallel trends is only
assumed **conditional on covariates $X$** — units with similar characteristics would have evolved similarly
[@did_guide].

## The TWFE trap: Goodman–Bacon and negative weights

The old standard regresses the outcome on unit and time fixed effects plus a single treatment dummy,
reporting one $\beta_{\text{TWFE}}$ [@did_guide]. **Goodman-Bacon (2021)** proved what that number actually
is: a **weighted average of *every* possible 2×2 DiD** in the data. The weights are all positive, larger
for bigger groups and for groups treated nearer the middle of the panel. [@did_guide] The comparisons
are:

- treated vs **never-treated** (clean);
- early-treated vs later-treated **while the latter is still untreated** (clean);
- **later-treated vs already-treated early adopters** — the **forbidden comparison** [@did_guide].

The forbidden comparison is the poison. When effects are **dynamic** (they grow or fade), an already-treated
unit's outcome is *still changing because of its own treatment* — so using it as a "control" subtracts a
moving target [@did_guide]. Rewrite those comparisons in terms of true effects and some cohort-period
effects enter with a **minus sign**: the *estimand* puts **negative weights** on them, even though every
2×2 enters the *estimator* with a positive weight. The headline result is that these negative weights
can **flip the sign**: $\beta_{\text{TWFE}}$ can come out
**negative even when every single cohort was helped** by the treatment [@did_guide].

## The modern robust estimators

The shared fix: **only ever compare treated units to *clean* controls — units not yet treated or never
treated** [@did_guide].

- **Callaway & Sant'Anna (CS).** Estimate each **$\text{ATT}(g,t)$ building block** using only clean
  controls, then **aggregate** the blocks into interpretable summaries (e.g. an average effect per year of
  exposure) [@did_guide].
- **Sun & Abraham (SA).** An **interaction-weighted event study**: standard TWFE event studies are biased
  when effects differ across cohorts, so SA **saturate** the model with **cohort-specific event-time
  dummies**, weighting by cohort size [@did_guide].
- **de Chaisemartin & D'Haultfœuille.** Document how pervasive negative weights are and offer a
  Wald-style estimator that **avoids them**, focused on instantaneous effects [@did_guide].

The unifying innovation is the **not-yet-treated control**: to estimate the 2014 cohort's effect in 2015,
use units that don't adopt until 2016+ as a valid, uncontaminated comparison [@did_guide].

## Event studies: dynamics and pre-trends

Event-study specifications **re-centre time on "event time" $e = t - g$** ($e=0$ is the adoption year) and
are the main tool for both visualising effects and testing assumptions [@did_guide]:

- **Lags ($e \ge 0$)** trace the **dynamic trajectory** — does the effect grow, plateau, or fade?
  [@did_guide]
- **Leads ($e < -1$)** are **placebo tests**: under parallel trends they should be **≈ 0**, indicating no
  pre-existing divergence between groups [@did_guide].

```{python}
#| label: fig-event-study
#| fig-cap: "A clean event study on a simulated staggered panel, computed by hand. Two cohorts adopt at different dates (plus never-treated units). Every treated cohort has the same positive effect, growing with exposure (solid grey line). Estimating each block only against not-yet-treated or never-treated controls (the Callaway–Sant'Anna idea) recovers it (black points). The leads (e<0, shaded) sit at zero, so the pre-trend placebo passes, and the lags trace the growing effect. A naïve TWFE regression with one treatment dummy, actually fitted to the same data (orange dashed), gives roughly two-thirds of the true average effect on the treated (dotted grey). Its forbidden comparisons use already-treated units, whose own effects are still growing, as controls."
#| code-fold: true
#| code-summary: "Show the figure code"

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
T = 12
units = 600
# assign cohorts: adopt at 4, at 8, or never
g = rng.choice([4, 8, 99], size=units, p=[0.35, 0.35, 0.30])
alpha = rng.normal(0, 1, units)                 # unit fixed effects
delta = np.linspace(0, 2, T) + rng.normal(0, 0.05, T)   # common time trend (parallel)

def true_tau(e):                                # dynamic effect, identical across cohorts
    return 0.4 * (e + 1) if e >= 0 else 0.0

Y = np.empty((units, T))
for i in range(units):
    for t in range(T):
        e = t - g[i]
        eff = true_tau(e) if g[i] != 99 else 0.0
        Y[i, t] = alpha[i] + delta[t] + eff + rng.normal(0, 0.7)

# Callaway–Sant'Anna-style event study with not-yet-treated clean controls
events = np.arange(-3, 4)
est, se = [], []
for e in events:
    atts, vs, ns = [], [], []
    for gg in [4, 8]:
        t = gg + e
        if t < 0 or t >= T:
            continue
        base = gg - 1
        treated = (g == gg)
        ctrl = g > max(t, base)                 # not yet treated by t (or never)
        if treated.sum() < 5 or ctrl.sum() < 5:
            continue
        dt = Y[treated, t] - Y[treated, base]
        dc = Y[ctrl, t] - Y[ctrl, base]
        atts.append(dt.mean() - dc.mean())
        vs.append(dt.var(ddof=1)/treated.sum() + dc.var(ddof=1)/ctrl.sum())
        ns.append(treated.sum())
    w = np.array(ns, float) / sum(ns)           # cohort-size weights
    est.append(np.dot(w, atts)); se.append(np.sqrt(np.dot(w**2, vs)))
est, se = np.array(est), np.array(se)

# the naive TWFE coefficient: Y_it = a_i + d_t + beta*D_it, solved by two-way demeaning
D = np.array([[1.0 if (g[i] != 99 and t >= g[i]) else 0.0 for t in range(T)] for i in range(units)])
def two_way_demean(M):
    return M - M.mean(1, keepdims=True) - M.mean(0, keepdims=True) + M.mean()
Dd, Yd = two_way_demean(D), two_way_demean(Y)
beta_twfe = (Dd * Yd).sum() / (Dd ** 2).sum()
# the target it should summarise: the average true effect over all treated unit-periods
att_true = np.mean([true_tau(t - g[i]) for i in range(units) for t in range(T) if D[i, t] == 1])

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7.4, 4.2))
ax.axvspan(-3.5, -0.5, color="#e4e3d8")
ax.axhline(0, color="#b7b6a8", lw=0.8); ax.axvline(-0.5, color="#b7b6a8", lw=0.8, ls=":")
xs = np.linspace(-3, 3, 100)
ax.plot(xs, [true_tau(int(np.floor(e))) if e >= 0 else 0 for e in xs],
        color="#5e5e54", lw=2.2, label="true dynamic effect τ(e)")
ax.errorbar(events, est, yerr=1.96*se, fmt="o", color="#161613", ms=5, capsize=3,
            label="clean event-study estimate (±1.96 SE)")
ax.axhline(beta_twfe, color="#e8531d", ls="--", lw=1.6,
           label=f"naïve TWFE single coefficient = {beta_twfe:.2f}")
ax.axhline(att_true, color="#5e5e54", ls=":", lw=1.4,
           label=f"true average effect on the treated = {att_true:.2f}")
ax.text(-3.3, 0.32, "pre-trend (placebo)\nleads ≈ 0", fontsize=8, color="#5e5e54")
ax.set_xlabel("event time  e = t − g  (0 = adoption)"); ax.set_ylabel("effect")
ax.set_title("Event study: clean controls recover the dynamics TWFE hides")
ax.set_ylim(-0.15, 2.45)
ax.legend(fontsize=8, frameon=False, loc="upper left")
fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

::: {.callout-warning title="Don't eyeball the pre-trends"}
Baker et al. caution against the **"eye-test"** — glancing at the leads and declaring them flat. With many
event-time coefficients you are [multiple-testing](../02-inference/multiple-testing.qmd); they recommend
formal **sensitivity analyses** and **simultaneous confidence bands** rather than a visual verdict
[@did_guide].
:::

## Which parallel trends? Choosing the comparison group

With staggered timing, "parallel trends" stops being one assumption. It becomes a family of them, one
per choice of comparison group. The guide names three: [@did_guide]

- **Never-treated only.** This avoids changes in the comparison group's composition and places no
  restriction on pre-trends. It also avoids comparing against units that may have *chosen* their
  adoption date because of recent outcomes. The cost is that never-treated units may be never-treated
  for reasons tied to their trends. Counties in states that never expanded Medicaid "may be too
  different" from expanders, and there may be too few of them.
- **Not-yet-treated.** Every unit that has not adopted by period $t$ serves as a control. This uses more
  data than never-treated alone without forcing *every* pre-period to be parallel. It is the guide's
  preferred middle ground, and the one used for Medicaid.
- **All periods and groups.** This is the most precise option, but it **imposes parallel pre-trends**
  across the whole history. If the pre-trends are not parallel, the estimates are biased. The more
  efficient imputation estimators (Borusyak–Jaravel–Spiess, Gardner) and Wooldridge's extended TWFE
  live here.

Which one is right depends on the setting. The guide's minimum demand is that you "clearly state the
specific parallel trends assumption" you impose, so readers can argue about it. [@did_guide]

## What pre-trends can and cannot tell you

The event-study leads are the most-looked-at and most-misread part of a DiD paper. The guide draws
three lessons from recent work (Roth; Rambachan & Roth; Bilinski & Hatfield): [@did_guide]

1. **Parallel trends is not testable.** The assumption concerns untreated outcomes *after* treatment.
   Pre-trends measure the same gap "in the 'wrong' periods". Flat leads are reassuring, but they are
   not the assumption itself.
2. **Pre-tests are usually low-powered.** In the 2014 Medicaid cohort, no lead is individually or
   jointly significant. Yet the leads average about **+2.3**, larger than all but one post-period
   estimate. They cannot rule out flat pre-trends, and they cannot rule out large ones either. Running
   the analysis only when a weak pre-test passes can make the bias *worse* (Roth 2022). This is the
   [power problem](../02-inference/statistical-power.qmd) in a new place.
3. **Take a stand on how big a violation could be.** Rambachan and Roth bound the effect by assuming
   post-period violations are no larger than some multiple of the largest pre-period jump. In the
   Medicaid data the largest one-period pre-trend is about 4 deaths per 100,000. That turns
   the 2014 estimate of −2.6 into an identified *set*, $-2.6\pm4=[-6.6,\,1.4]$. Its robust confidence
   interval, $[-11.1,\,5.1]$, "spans implausibly large effects in both directions". The pre-trends
   simply don't carry much information. This is the
   [partial-identification](partial-identification.qmd) mindset applied to DiD.

**One more trap: the single summary coefficient.** A common shortcut follows the event study with one
TWFE regression that has a single post-treatment dummy. In a design with one treated cohort, that
coefficient equals the average post-period estimate *minus the average pre-period estimate*. It
therefore relies on parallel trends in *every* period, not just after treatment. In the 2014 cohort
it gives **−2.53**, while the average of the post-period effects is **−0.70**. The gap comes from
those positive leads. [@did_guide]

::: {.callout-note title="Worked example: Medicaid expansion and adult mortality, all four waves"}
The guide's running example ends here. Counties in states that expanded Medicaid in 2014, 2015, 2016
and 2019 are compared with 1,222 not-yet-treated counties, using population weights. Each
$\text{ATT}(g,t)$ comes from a doubly robust 2×2. The event-study weights are cohort sizes, and they
are very unequal: the 2014 cohort holds **80%** of treated adults and the 2016 cohort **3.5%**. Note
that event time is unbalanced. The 2019 cohort contributes only to $e=0$, so later event times average
over a different mix of cohorts. A **balanced** aggregation fixes the cohort set when that matters.
[@did_guide]

The overall effect over event times 0–5 is **0.09** deaths per 100,000 (SE 1.92, 95% CI
$[-3.67,\,3.84]$). With covariates and conditional parallel trends it is **−2.25** (SE 4.05,
$[-10.19,\,5.69]$). The authors' conclusion is a model of honest reporting. They do not claim
"Medicaid had no effect". They say the data rule out effects larger than 6 or more negative than −11
deaths per 100,000 over the six years after expansion. [@did_guide]
:::

## A forward-engineering checklist

The guide's closing message is that complex designs are built from simple ones. Its worked example
grows from a 2×2 into five timing groups, eleven years, six covariates, three parallel-trends
assumptions and four estimation methods. Its eight steps reverse the old habit of picking a
regression first and interpreting it afterwards: [@did_guide]

1. **Define target parameters.** Write down the building-block $\text{ATT}$s and the summary that
   aggregates them, including the weights.
2. **State the identification assumptions formally.** Which parallel trends, no anticipation, overlap?
   Then bring evidence that could falsify them.
3. **Choose the estimation method.** Sample means, or regression-adjustment, IPW or doubly robust when
   parallel trends is conditional on covariates. State the extra modelling restrictions each one needs.
4. **Discuss sources of uncertainty.** Decide on sampling-based or design-based inference and what is
   treated as fixed versus random.
5. **Estimate.**
6. **Run a sensitivity analysis.** How much do the estimates move under plausible-sized violations of
   step 2?
7. **Run a heterogeneity analysis.** Check whether the aggregate hides subgroups, timing differences or
   different sources of variation.
8. **Keep learning.** If the DiD assumptions look implausible, switch designs.

Running a simple TWFE regression is, in the guide's words, "an implicit choice to reverse engineer a
research design from the statistical method". [@did_guide]

## Common pitfalls

::: {.callout-warning title="Where this goes wrong"}
- **Using TWFE with a single dummy under staggered timing.** Forbidden comparisons and negative weights can
  flip the sign — switch to Callaway–Sant'Anna, Sun–Abraham, or de Chaisemartin–D'Haultfœuille.
  [@did_guide]
- **Using already-treated units as controls.** Their changing effects contaminate the comparison; restrict
  to not-yet-/never-treated. [@did_guide]
- **Reading a TWFE event study at face value.** It too is biased across heterogeneous cohorts; use an
  interaction-weighted (SA) version. [@did_guide]
- **Eyeballing pre-trends.** Use simultaneous bands and sensitivity analysis, not a visual flatness check.
  [@did_guide]
- **Treating a passed pre-test as proof.** Pre-tests are low-powered, and conditioning on passing one
  can add bias. Bound the effect under plausible violations (Rambachan–Roth) instead. [@did_guide]
- **Leaving the parallel-trends assumption unnamed.** Never-treated, not-yet-treated and all-periods
  comparisons rest on different assumptions; say which one you impose. [@did_guide]
- **Summarising an event study with one TWFE dummy.** That coefficient subtracts the average pre-period
  gap, so it depends on parallel trends in every period (Medicaid: −2.53 vs −0.70). [@did_guide]
- **Assuming a never-treated group exists.** If everyone is eventually treated, lean on not-yet-treated
  controls and be explicit about the comparison. [@did_guide]
:::

## Appendix · the mathematics {.unnumbered}

::: {.math-appendix}

**The Goodman-Bacon weights.** $\hat\beta_{\text{TWFE}} = \sum_{k} s_k\,\widehat{\text{DiD}}_k$, summing over all
timing-group pairs $k$. The weights $s_k$ are **strictly positive**, proportional to group sizes × the
variance of the treatment dummy conditional on the fixed effects. [@did_guide] Sign reversal comes from
the *estimand*. With one group treated in period 1 and one in period 2, the comparison that uses the
early group as a control gives
$\beta_{\text{TWFE}}=\text{ATT}(2,2)+w_1\text{ATT}(1,1)-w_1\text{ATT}(1,2)$. The later effect of the
early group enters negatively unless effects are static. [@did_guide]

**Callaway–Sant'Anna aggregation.** Estimate each $\widehat{\text{ATT}}(g,t)$ from clean controls, then form
summaries by averaging with chosen weights — e.g. the **event-study** parameter
$\theta(e) = \sum_g w_g\,\widehat{\text{ATT}}(g,\,g+e)$ averages cohorts at a fixed exposure $e$ (the
quantity the figure computes), and an **overall ATT** averages over all $(g,t)$ with $t\ge g$. Because each
block uses only valid comparisons, the aggregate inherits their lack of contamination.

**Why leads test parallel trends.** For $e<0$ the "effect" is mechanically zero under the null, so a
nonzero estimated lead coefficient signals that treated and control groups were **already diverging** before
treatment — direct evidence against parallel trends (subject to the multiple-testing caveat).

:::
