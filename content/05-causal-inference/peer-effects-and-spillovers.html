Peer Effects and Spillovers: When One Unit’s Treatment Reaches Another

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source

Classmates, housemates, neighbouring villages, next week’s self. Most causal designs assume each unit responds only to its own treatment. When that fails, the comparison is biased; when it is the question, the design has to be built around it.

Two separate puzzles share one root. Peer effects ask whether the people around you change your outcome. Spillovers are what happens when a treatment given to some units reaches others it was not aimed at. Either way, your outcome depends on someone else’s treatment. Good students sit together for many reasons, and a control group that hears about the treatment is no longer a clean control. The fix is the same in both cases: randomise, or find as-good-as-random variation in, the thing that reaches you from others, and say exactly which channels you have allowed for.

Why it matters

Parents fight for places at selective schools, and education researchers argue about whether children should be bussed across a city. Behind both is the same belief: your classmates’ ability affects your learning. (Angrist and Pischke 2015) Regress students’ scores on their classmates’ average and you will always find a strong positive link. Angrist and Pischke call this regression a “rite of passage” for social scientists. (Angrist and Pischke 2015) Whether that link is causal decides a lot of school policy.

The same problem turns up in experiments, in reverse. A vaccination trial, a policing experiment, a get-out-the-vote mailing: each assumes the untreated group is untouched by the treatment. But vaccinated neighbours protect you, crime moves round the corner, and people talk to their housemates. Gerber and Green name the assumption that rules all this out, non-interference, and spend a whole chapter showing how much hangs on it. (Gerber and Green 2012) The potential-outcomes framework states it as the “no interference” half of SUTVA. This entry is about what to do when it is doubtful.

Two kinds of peer effect

Angrist and Pischke separate two questions that are often run together: (Angrist and Pischke 2009)

  1. Does a group characteristic affect a different individual outcome? Acemoglu and Angrist (2000) asked whether your wage depends on the average schooling in your state, over and above your own schooling. The theory is a social return to education: an educated workforce makes everyone more productive. (Angrist and Pischke 2009)
  2. Does the group average of a variable affect the same variable for an individual? Are you more likely to finish high school when your schoolmates are more likely to finish? This one, they say, is “even more difficult” to uncover. (Angrist and Pischke 2009)

The first kind: social returns that appear from nowhere

The obvious threat to a social-returns regression is omitted state-year factors. Public universities may expand in good times, so states with rising schooling also have rising wages. Acemoglu and Angrist used historical compulsory-attendance laws as instruments for state average schooling. (Angrist and Pischke 2009)

The subtler problem is mechanical. Put both your own schooling s_i and your state’s average \bar S_j in the same regression. Let \rho_0 be the plain OLS return to your own schooling, and \rho_1 the slope of wages on the state average alone. MHE shows that \rho_1 is the 2SLS estimate of the private return using a full set of state dummies as instruments. The coefficient on the state average then turns out to be (Angrist and Pischke 2009)

\pi_1 = \phi\,(\rho_1 - \rho_0), \qquad \phi = \frac{1}{1-R^2} > 1,

where R^2 is the first-stage R^2 of own schooling on state dummies. Any gap between OLS and that grouped IV estimate shows up as a “social return”. If grouping by state averages away measurement error in schooling, \rho_1 > \rho_0, and you find positive social returns that are not there. If it averages away ability bias instead, \rho_1 < \rho_0, and the social return looks negative. (Angrist and Pischke 2009) The coefficient on the group mean is really a Hausman test comparing OLS with grouped 2SLS. MHE notes that Borjas (1992) met the same problem in estimating effects of ethnic background. (Angrist and Pischke 2009)

The second kind: the regression that always says 1

Now regress each student’s graduation status s_{ij} on the graduation rate \bar S_j of their school. It looks like a sensible causal question. It is, in MHE’s word, nonsense: the slope is always exactly 1. (Angrist and Pischke 2009) The school average is just the fitted value from regressing s_{ij} on school dummies, and regressing a variable on its own fitted value returns a slope of one, whatever the world is like. (The one-line proof is in the appendix.)

Leaving student i out of the average, \bar S_{(i)j}, stops the slope being 1 automatically. It is still a problem. A shock common to the school, such as an unusually effective principal, raises everyone’s chances at once. That creates a correlation between you and your peers with no causal link between them. Angrist and Pischke “prefer not to see regressions like” this either. (Angrist and Pischke 2009) Common shocks are also why group-level regressors need clustered standard errors. Here they do more: they create the estimate itself.

What works: peers’ characteristics fixed in advance

MHE’s recommendation is to use ex ante peer characteristics: something about your peers that was set before the outcome, so school-year shocks cannot have moved it. (Angrist and Pischke 2009)

  • Ammermueller and Pischke (2006) related primary-school achievement in Europe to classmates’ family background, measured by the number of books at home. Books at home predate the test scores, so a good principal this year cannot have changed them. (Angrist and Pischke 2009)
  • Angrist and Lang (2004) studied how bussed-in, low-achieving newcomers affected the scores of high-achieving resident students in Boston (the Metco programme). The number of newcomers is set by students outside the estimation sample and by where they come from, not by residents’ outcomes. Because it is a school-level variable, school effects still matter for inference. (Angrist and Pischke 2009)

Ex-ante measures solve the common-shock problem. They do not solve sorting: if families with many books choose the same schools, peers’ books still stand in for your own background. A credible design also needs the mix of peers to vary for reasons unrelated to you. That is what the next example supplies.

Worked example: the elite illusion

Boston’s three exam schools admit pupils on an entrance test (strictly, a weighted average of the ISEE score and grades). Fewer than half of applicants win a place. (Angrist and Pischke 2015) Comparing exam-school pupils with everyone else is hopeless, since the schools admit only high achievers. Randomising admission would not help either: “if exam schools were to admit students randomly, then they wouldn’t be exam schools after all.” (Angrist and Pischke 2015) Instead Angrist and Pischke use the admissions cutoff for Boston Latin School (BLS), the most selective, as a regression discontinuity.

The design is fuzzy. Most pupils who just miss the BLS cutoff go to Boston Latin Academy, the next school down, so the chance of attending some exam school barely changes at the cutoff. What does change sharply is the peer group. (Angrist and Pischke 2015)

Step What it measures Estimate
Naive peer regression 7th-grade maths score on classmates’ 4th-grade average, controlling for own 4th-grade score (all Boston exam-school applicants) about .25σ per 1σ of peer quality
First stage Jump in peers’ 4th-grade maths score at the BLS cutoff .80σ, precisely estimated
Reduced form Jump in applicants’ own 7th/8th-grade maths scores at the cutoff −.02 (SE .10)
2SLS (fuzzy RD) Effect of peer quality on own score, with qualifying as the instrument −.023 (SE .132)

(Angrist and Pischke 2015)

The jump in peer quality, four-fifths of a standard deviation, is about the gap in average peer quality between inner-city Boston and its wealthy suburbs. Applicants’ own earlier scores, motivation and family background show no jump. (Angrist and Pischke 2015) So pupils just above and just below the line differ in their classmates, not in themselves, which is close to the ideal peer experiment. Their later maths scores do not jump at all. The reduced form divided by the first stage (−.02/.80 ≈ −.025, our arithmetic) is essentially the reported 2SLS estimate. Peer quality that rises by .8σ produces no gain in achievement, against the .25σ that the naive regression implies. (The standard error is clustered by applicant, because each pupil contributes a 7th- and an 8th-grade score.) (Angrist and Pischke 2015)

Two caveats from the authors keep this honest:

  • The exclusion restriction picks a channel. 2SLS assumes qualifying affects scores only through peer quality. Exam schools also have more white classmates, which matters for the bussing debate (“Separate educational facilities are inherently unequal,” 1954). Swap peer quality for share of white peers and you get the same zero, because the reduced form has not changed. (Angrist and Pischke 2015)
  • The likely bias is upward. Other things that change at the cutoff, such as better teachers and more advanced courses, probably help. If they are left in the error term, the 2SLS estimate is too big for a pure peer effect. “All the more surprising, then, that this estimate turns out to be zero.” (Angrist and Pischke 2015)

Spillovers in experiments: non-interference

Gerber and Green write non-interference as Y_i(\mathbf{d}) = Y_i(d_i): subject i’s potential outcomes depend only on i’s own treatment, not on the whole vector \mathbf{d} of who got what. The assumption is also called individualistic treatment response (Manski) or SUTVA (Rubin). Gerber and Green prefer to keep “no interference between units” in mind, because the acronym hides the idea. (Gerber and Green 2012) They also distinguish global non-interference, which must hold however many units are treated, from a weaker local version for one fixed number treated. The local version may stop holding if a programme is scaled up or down. (Gerber and Green 2012)

A small example of the bias

A shop will honour one of three sales agents as “Salesperson of the Month”, chosen by lottery. (Gerber and Green 2012)

August sales Mary honoured Peter honoured Limor honoured Nobody honoured
Mary 100 50 70 70
Peter 50 50 50 50
Limor 90 50 90 90

(Gerber and Green 2012)

The researcher defines the effect against the “nobody honoured” column: (30 + 0 + 0)/3 = 10. But honouring Peter demoralises the other two. The three lotteries give apparent effects of 30, 0 and 30, which average 20: double the intended estimand. Each untreated outcome depends on who else was treated. (Gerber and Green 2012)

Where interference comes from

Gerber and Green list the usual suspects: (Gerber and Green 2012)

  • Contagion. Being vaccinated matters less if everyone around you is vaccinated.
  • Displacement. Police in one place may push crime next door, which exaggerates the apparent effect.
  • Communication. Information spreads from the treated to the nominally untreated, which shrinks the apparent effect.
  • Social comparison. Housing help for the treatment group can make the control group rate its own housing lower.
  • Deterrence. Random audits of some local governments may deter corruption in others that hear about them.
  • Persistence and memory. In studies that follow a subject over time, earlier treatments can carry forward (see Interference across time below).

Interference can also come from the experimenter. A new curriculum may lead teachers to give the treatment group more time and the control group less. (Gerber and Green 2012)

Design for it: multi-level randomisation

Gerber and Green’s mail experiment is set in two-voter households. In 5,000 households both voters get get-out-the-vote mail, in 5,000 neither does, and in 10,000 exactly one, chosen at random. (Gerber and Green 2012) That gives four groups of 10,000 people and four potential outcomes per voter: Y_{00}, Y_{01}, Y_{10}, Y_{11}, where the first subscript is the housemate’s treatment and the second is one’s own. Comparing the groups identifies the direct effect (with and without a treated housemate) and the spillover effect (for treated and untreated voters).

Three lessons follow: (Gerber and Green 2012)

  • “No unmodelled spillovers.” This design does not violate non-interference, because it allows for the spillover within a household. It still assumes nothing spills between households. A useful check: there should be at least as many randomly assigned groups as columns of potential outcomes.
  • Interference is not clustering. Housemates may vote alike because they share a background. That affects standard errors. Interference affects bias. Either can occur without the other.
  • Ignoring spillovers biases the direct effect either way. Pooling everyone who got mail against everyone who did not overstates the direct effect if spillovers are larger among the treated, and understates it if they are larger among the untreated. Without a multi-level design this is guesswork.

Data from such a design are easy to read. Sinclair, McConnell and Green mailed all, half or none of the households in each nine-digit zip code. Among single-voter households: (Gerber and Green 2012)

Group Voted / registered Turnout (our arithmetic)
Untreated, zip code untreated 1,021 / 6,217 16.4%
Untreated, half the zip code treated 526 / 3,316 15.9%
Treated, half the zip code treated 620 / 2,949 21.0%
Treated, whole zip code treated 1,316 / 6,377 20.6%

Our reading (the book leaves it as an exercise): the direct effect is about 5 percentage points, in line with the 4–5 points such social-pressure mail usually produces, while untreated neighbours of treated households vote at much the same rate as voters in untouched zip codes (−0.6 points). On these numbers, the effect did not travel between households.

Spatial spillovers: unequal chances of exposure

Often spillovers are not neatly confined to a household or school. A clinic helps neighbouring villages, a police patrol affects nearby blocks. Two things then go wrong. (Gerber and Green 2012)

Measuring proximity needs a model. Walking distance is right for norms passed on foot, wrong for norms passed by mobile phone. For police, minutes to the nearest responder may matter more than miles. A fixed radius is a common compromise, but choosing it is another source of uncertainty. Flexible models of how effects decay with distance are often too imprecise to help. (Gerber and Green 2012)

Exposure is not randomly assigned with equal probability, even when treatment is. A unit in a cluster of other units is far more likely to be next to a treated one than an isolated unit. If those probabilities are related to potential outcomes, simple comparisons are biased. The remedy is the one used for blocked designs: weight each unit by the inverse of its probability of being in the condition it ended up in, and drop units that could never be exposed. (Gerber and Green 2012)

Gerber and Green’s toy example has five villages on a line, at positions A, B, C, D and F (E is empty), and one clinic placed at random. Being next to a treated village has probability 0.2 for the end villages A and D, 0.4 for B and C, and zero for F. With the book’s potential outcomes, the true direct effect is 1 and the true spillover effect is 0.5. (Gerber and Green 2012) Across the five possible placements:

  • the inverse-probability-weighted difference in means averages 0.81 for the direct effect and 0.33 for the spillover: a little biased in so small a sample, but in the right direction;
  • the unweighted difference in means for the spillover averages −1.0. It says spillovers hurt, when they help. An unweighted regression on “treated” and “next to treated” dummies gives the same answer. (Gerber and Green 2012)

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# ---- Left: peer regressions with no true peer effect -------------------------------
rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
J, n, R = 150, 25, 400
g = np.repeat(np.arange(J), n)

def slope(x, y):
    xc = x - x.mean()
    return (xc * (y - y.mean())).sum() / (xc ** 2).sum()

est = np.zeros((R, 3))
for r in range(R):
    shock = rng.normal(0, 0.35, J)[g]          # a good (or bad) principal: shared by the school
    books = rng.normal(0, 1, J * n)             # ex-ante family background
    s = 0.5 * books + shock + rng.normal(0, 1, J * n)   # own background matters; peers do not
    s_sum, b_sum = np.bincount(g, s), np.bincount(g, books)
    mean_incl = (s_sum / n)[g]
    mean_loo = (s_sum[g] - s) / (n - 1)
    books_loo = (b_sum[g] - books) / (n - 1)
    est[r] = slope(mean_incl, s), slope(mean_loo, s), slope(books_loo, s)

# ---- Right: Gerber & Green's clinic example (Tables 8.2-8.3) -------------------------
Y00 = np.array([0, 6, 0, 6, 6.])              # villages at A, B, C, D, F
Y10 = np.array([2, 2, 4, 6, np.nan])          # next to the clinic (F never is)
Y01 = np.array([0, 10, 4, 6, 3.])             # gets the clinic
nbrs = {0: [1], 1: [0, 2], 2: [1, 3], 3: [2], 4: []}
p00 = np.array([.6, .4, .4, .6, .8])
p10 = np.array([.2, .4, .4, .2, 0.])
p01 = np.full(5, .2)

def wmean(y, p):
    return (y / p).sum() / (1 / p).sum()

w_dir, w_spl, u_spl = [], [], []
for t in range(5):
    cond = np.zeros(5, int)                    # 0 = untreated, 1 = next to clinic, 2 = clinic
    cond[t] = 2
    cond[nbrs[t]] = 1
    y = np.where(cond == 2, Y01, np.where(cond == 1, Y10, Y00))
    c, s_, tr = cond == 0, cond == 1, cond == 2
    w_dir.append(wmean(y[tr], p01[tr]) - wmean(y[c], p00[c]))
    if s_.any():                               # spillover contrast exists in 4 of 5 placements
        c_ok = c & (p10 > 0)                   # F can never be exposed: excluded

A larger case: police hotspots

Gerber and Green’s hotspot example has 30 crime hotspots, 10 of them given extra patrols. Untreated crime rises with the number of hotspots within 750 metres. Patrols reduce crime on the spot by 5, push crime up by 5 in untreated hotspots within 500 metres of a patrol, and the two together reduce crime by 7. (Gerber and Green 2012) Across 100,000 simulated experiments:

Estimator Direct effect (true −5) Spillover (true +5) Both (true −7)
Ignore spillovers: treated vs untreated −7.3 — —
Unweighted: exposed vs unexposed untreated — −3.8 —
IPW, 30 hotspots −4.2 7.0 −3.8
IPW, 300 hotspots (100 treated) −4.9 5.2 −6.7

(Gerber and Green 2012)

The 11 hotspots that could never be exposed estimate the direct effect well on their own (−4.9). Weighting fixes most of the problem, but not all of it with 30 hotspots: neighbouring hotspots tend to be exposed together, so assignment to spillover is implicitly clustered, and the weights themselves change from draw to draw. Both biases fade as the number of units grows. (Gerber and Green 2012)

Two practical points complete the picture. First, non-experimental units are free data. 100 nearby locations that could not be treated but could be exposed estimate the spillover at 5.2 (SE 1.1). Not measuring them is “like leaving money on the table.” (Gerber and Green 2012) Second, design helps more than analysis: isolated pairs of hotspots, randomised to 00/01/10/11 like the mail experiment, remove the clustering bias. Simulating the randomisation in advance shows how exposure will fall. Because model choice (the radius, the decay) is itself uncertain, results should be shown under several models. (Gerber and Green 2012)

Interference across time

Following one subject over several periods turns each period into a “unit”, and non-interference becomes two assumptions about time: (Gerber and Green 2012)

  • No anticipation: outcomes are not affected by treatments still to come.
  • No persistence: outcomes are not affected by earlier treatments. Laboratory studies enforce this with washout periods, the time-based version of a buffer zone between villages.

Gerber and Green’s pupil-dilation example has two periods and one dose of eye drops, given in period 1 or 2 by a coin flip. Untreated dilation drifts from 3 mm to 4 mm; treated dilation is 7 mm. The two possible experiments give estimates of 4 and 3, which average to the true 3.5, but only because the example assumes no anticipation and no persistence. (Gerber and Green 2012) Randomising the timing rules out coincidence with trends. It does nothing for the two assumptions that matter most. That may be why within-subject designs are more common in the physical sciences than the social ones. (Gerber and Green 2012)

Waitlist (stepped-wedge) designs combine both approaches. Everyone is treated eventually and chance decides when, which also makes the design easier to defend to participants. Gerber and Green’s TV-advertising example has eight media markets: two start ads in week 1, two in week 2, two in week 3, and two never do. That allows 2,520 possible assignments. (Gerber and Green 2012) Assuming effects last exactly two weeks, IPW gives an immediate effect of 2.72 (95% interval 0.23 to 5.51) and an immediate-plus-lagged effect of 4.13 (0.89 to 7.40). A naive treated-vs-untreated comparison averages 0.58 across all assignments, far below the true effects of 2 and 3. (Gerber and Green 2012) The precision comes largely from the assumption about how long effects last.

Common pitfalls

WarningWhere this goes wrong

  • Regressing an outcome on its own group mean. The slope is 1 by construction and says nothing about peers. (Angrist and Pischke 2009)

  • Trusting a leave-one-out peer mean. Any shock shared by the group (a principal, a local labour market, a teacher) shows up as a “peer effect”. Use peers’ characteristics fixed before the outcome, ideally with as-good-as-random variation in who your peers are. (Angrist and Pischke 2009)

  • Reading a group-mean coefficient as a social return. With own and group values both included, the group coefficient is \phi(\rho_1 - \rho_0): any gap between OLS and grouped IV, including one caused by measurement error. (Angrist and Pischke 2009)

  • Forgetting that an IV exclusion restriction chooses a channel. The exam-school zero is equally a zero for peer ability, peer race or anything else that jumps at the cutoff. The reduced form does not know which channel you meant. (Angrist and Pischke 2015)

  • Defining the estimand without saying what others receive. “The effect of mail” differs depending on whether the housemate got mail. The Salesperson-of-the-Month example shows a 100% overstatement from this alone. (Gerber and Green 2012)

  • Comparing exposed with unexposed units without weights. In spatial and network settings, exposure probability varies with location and is often related to outcomes. Unweighted comparisons can get the sign wrong. (Gerber and Green 2012)

  • Confusing correlated outcomes with interference. Correlation within households or schools is a standard-error problem; interference is a bias problem. Clustering the SEs does not fix spillovers. (Gerber and Green 2012)

  • Calling a “no spillover” finding a mere null. If it holds up, it greatly simplifies every later experiment in that setting. Gerber and Green treat it as a result worth reporting. (Gerber and Green 2012)

Appendix · the mathematics

Why regressing on the group mean gives exactly 1. With n_j members in group j, group means \bar S_j and grand mean \bar S, (Angrist and Pischke 2009)

\frac{\sum_j\sum_i s_{ij}(\bar S_j - \bar S)}{\sum_j n_j(\bar S_j - \bar S)^2} = \frac{\sum_j(\bar S_j - \bar S)\,n_j\bar S_j}{\sum_j n_j(\bar S_j - \bar S)^2} = 1,

because \sum_j n_j(\bar S_j - \bar S)\bar S = 0, so the numerator equals \sum_j n_j(\bar S_j - \bar S)^2.

The social-returns decomposition. Write Y_{ij} = \mu + \pi_0 s_i + \pi_1 \bar S_j + \nu_{ij} and set \tau_i = s_i - \bar S_j. Then Y_{ij} = \mu + \pi_0\tau_i + (\pi_0+\pi_1)\bar S_j + \nu_{ij}, and \tau_i is uncorrelated with \bar S_j by construction, so the slope on \bar S_j alone is \rho_1 = \pi_0 + \pi_1, and (Angrist and Pischke 2009)

\pi_0 = \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(s_i - \bar S_j, Y_{ij})}{V(s_i) - V(\bar S_j)} = \rho_0\,\phi + \rho_1(1-\phi) = \rho_1 + \phi(\rho_0 - \rho_1), \qquad \phi = \frac{V(s_i)}{V(s_i) - V(\bar S_j)},

so \pi_1 = \rho_1 - \pi_0 = \phi(\rho_1 - \rho_0). Since V(\bar S_j)/V(s_i) is the R^2 from regressing s_i on group dummies, \phi = 1/(1-R^2).

The leave-one-out slope under a common shock (our addition). Suppose s_{ij} = a_j + e_{ij} with V(a_j) = \sigma_a^2, V(e_{ij}) = \sigma_e^2 and no peer effect. Then \bar S_{(i)j} = a_j + \bar e_{(i)j}, and for large samples the slope is

\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(s_{ij}, \bar S_{(i)j})}{V(\bar S_{(i)j})} = \frac{\sigma_a^2}{\sigma_a^2 + \sigma_e^2/(n-1)} .

In the figure, \sigma_a^2 = 0.1225, \sigma_e^2 = 1.25 and n = 25, giving about 0.70: the share of the leave-one-out mean’s variance that comes from the shared shock.

Fuzzy RD as a ratio. With qualification D_i as the instrument for peer quality \bar X_{(i)} and a linear control for the running variable, the just-identified 2SLS estimate is the reduced-form jump over the first-stage jump: \lambda = \rho/\phi_{\text{fs}} \approx -0.02/0.80. (See the RD appendix for the general Wald-at-the-cutoff form.)

Inverse-probability-weighted exposure contrasts. For exposure conditions k (here 00, 10, 01) with unit-specific probabilities p_{k,i}, the weighted difference in means for condition k against control is (Gerber and Green 2012)

\widehat{E}[Y_k - Y_{00}] = \frac{\sum_{i\in k} Y_i/p_{k,i}}{\sum_{i\in k} 1/p_{k,i}} - \frac{\sum_{i\in 00} Y_i/p_{00,i}}{\sum_{i\in 00} 1/p_{00,i}},

using only units with p_{k,i} > 0 and p_{00,i} > 0. Because the denominators change from one assignment to the next, it is slightly biased in small samples. Dividing by N instead of the sum of the weights (a difference in weighted totals) is unbiased but imprecise: in the clinic example it gives −1.25 for the draw that gives 0.40, and 1.50 for the draw that gives 1.85. (Gerber and Green 2012)

Counting assignments in the waitlist design. Choosing 2 of 8 markets for week 1, 2 of the remaining 6 for week 2 and 2 of the last 4 for week 3 gives \binom{8}{2}\binom{6}{2}\binom{4}{2} = 28 \times 15 \times 6 = 2{,}520. (Gerber and Green 2012)

Two separate puzzles share one root. **Peer effects** ask whether the people around you change your
outcome. **Spillovers** are what happens when a treatment given to some units reaches others it was not
aimed at. Either way, your outcome depends on *someone else's* treatment. Good students sit together for
many reasons, and a control group that hears about the treatment is no longer a clean control. The fix is the
same in both cases: **randomise, or find as-good-as-random variation in, the thing that reaches you from
others, and say exactly which channels you have allowed for.**

## Why it matters

Parents fight for places at selective schools, and education researchers argue about whether children
should be bussed across a city. Behind both is the same belief: **your classmates' ability affects your
learning**. [@angrist_mastering] Regress students' scores on their classmates' average and you will always
find a strong positive link. Angrist and Pischke call this regression a *"rite of passage"* for social
scientists. [@angrist_mastering] Whether that link is causal decides a lot of school policy.

The same problem turns up in experiments, in reverse. A vaccination trial, a policing experiment, a
get-out-the-vote mailing: each assumes the untreated group is untouched by the treatment. But vaccinated
neighbours protect you, crime moves round the corner, and people talk to their housemates. Gerber and
Green name the assumption that rules all this out, **non-interference**, and spend a whole chapter showing
how much hangs on it. [@gerber_green] The [potential-outcomes](potential-outcomes.qmd) framework states it
as the "no interference" half of SUTVA. This entry is about what to do when it is doubtful.

## Two kinds of peer effect

Angrist and Pischke separate two questions that are often run together: [@angrist_mostly_harmless]

1. **Does a group characteristic affect a different individual outcome?** Acemoglu and Angrist (2000)
   asked whether your wage depends on the *average schooling in your state*, over and above your own
   schooling. The theory is a **social return** to education: an educated workforce makes everyone more
   productive. [@angrist_mostly_harmless]
2. **Does the group average of a variable affect the same variable for an individual?** Are you more likely
   to finish high school when your schoolmates are more likely to finish? This one, they say, is *"even
   more difficult"* to uncover. [@angrist_mostly_harmless]

### The first kind: social returns that appear from nowhere

The obvious threat to a social-returns regression is omitted state-year factors. Public universities may
expand in good times, so states with rising schooling also have rising wages. Acemoglu and Angrist used
historical compulsory-attendance laws as instruments for state average schooling. [@angrist_mostly_harmless]

The subtler problem is mechanical. Put both your own schooling $s_i$ and your state's average $\bar S_j$ in
the same regression. Let $\rho_0$ be the plain OLS return to your own schooling, and $\rho_1$ the slope
of wages on the state average alone. MHE shows that $\rho_1$ is the 2SLS estimate of the private return
using a full set of state dummies as instruments. The coefficient on the state average then turns out to
be [@angrist_mostly_harmless]

$$
\pi_1 = \phi\,(\rho_1 - \rho_0), \qquad \phi = \frac{1}{1-R^2} > 1,
$$

where $R^2$ is the first-stage $R^2$ of own schooling on state dummies. **Any** gap between OLS and that
grouped IV estimate shows up as a "social return". If grouping by state averages away measurement error in
schooling, $\rho_1 > \rho_0$, and you find positive social returns that are not there. If it averages away
ability bias instead, $\rho_1 < \rho_0$, and the social return looks negative. [@angrist_mostly_harmless]
The coefficient on the group mean is really a Hausman test comparing OLS with grouped 2SLS. MHE notes that
Borjas (1992) met the same problem in estimating effects of ethnic background. [@angrist_mostly_harmless]

### The second kind: the regression that always says 1

Now regress each student's graduation status $s_{ij}$ on the graduation rate $\bar S_j$ of their school.
It looks like a sensible causal question. It is, in MHE's word, **nonsense**: the slope is always exactly
1. [@angrist_mostly_harmless] The school average is just the fitted value from regressing $s_{ij}$ on school
dummies, and regressing a variable on its own fitted value returns a slope of one, whatever the world is
like. (The one-line proof is in the appendix.)

Leaving student $i$ out of the average, $\bar S_{(i)j}$, stops the slope being 1 automatically. It is still
a problem. A **shock common to the school**, such as an unusually effective principal, raises everyone's
chances at once. That creates a correlation between you and your peers with no causal link between them.
Angrist and Pischke *"prefer not to see regressions like"* this either. [@angrist_mostly_harmless] Common
shocks are also why group-level regressors need clustered standard errors. Here they do more: they create
the estimate itself.

### What works: peers' characteristics fixed in advance

MHE's recommendation is to use **ex ante peer characteristics**: something about your peers that was set
*before* the outcome, so school-year shocks cannot have moved it. [@angrist_mostly_harmless]

- **Ammermueller and Pischke (2006)** related primary-school achievement in Europe to classmates' family
  background, measured by the number of books at home. Books at home predate the test scores, so a good
  principal this year cannot have changed them. [@angrist_mostly_harmless]
- **Angrist and Lang (2004)** studied how bussed-in, low-achieving newcomers affected the scores of
  high-achieving resident students in Boston (the Metco programme). The number of newcomers is set by
  students outside the estimation sample and by where they come from, not by residents' outcomes. Because
  it is a school-level variable, school effects still matter for inference. [@angrist_mostly_harmless]

Ex-ante measures solve the common-shock problem. They do not solve *sorting*: if families with many books
choose the same schools, peers' books still stand in for your own background. A credible design also needs
the mix of peers to vary for reasons unrelated to you. That is what the next example supplies.

## Worked example: the elite illusion

Boston's three exam schools admit pupils on an entrance test (strictly, a weighted average of the ISEE
score and grades). Fewer than half of applicants win a place. [@angrist_mastering] Comparing exam-school
pupils with everyone else is hopeless, since the schools admit only high achievers. Randomising admission
would not help either: *"if exam schools were to admit students randomly, then they wouldn't be exam
schools after all."* [@angrist_mastering] Instead Angrist and Pischke use the admissions cutoff for Boston
Latin School (BLS), the most selective, as a [regression discontinuity](regression-discontinuity.qmd).

The design is **fuzzy**. Most pupils who just miss the BLS cutoff go to Boston Latin Academy, the next
school down, so the chance of attending *some* exam school barely changes at the cutoff. What does change
sharply is the peer group. [@angrist_mastering]

| Step | What it measures | Estimate |
|---|---|---|
| Naive peer regression | 7th-grade maths score on classmates' 4th-grade average, controlling for own 4th-grade score (all Boston exam-school applicants) | about **.25σ** per 1σ of peer quality |
| First stage | Jump in peers' 4th-grade maths score at the BLS cutoff | **.80σ**, precisely estimated |
| Reduced form | Jump in applicants' own 7th/8th-grade maths scores at the cutoff | **−.02** (SE .10) |
| 2SLS (fuzzy RD) | Effect of peer quality on own score, with qualifying as the instrument | **−.023** (SE .132) |

[@angrist_mastering]

The jump in peer quality, four-fifths of a standard deviation, is about the gap in average peer quality
between inner-city Boston and its wealthy suburbs. Applicants' *own* earlier scores, motivation and family
background show no jump. [@angrist_mastering] So pupils just above and just below the line differ in their
classmates, not in themselves, which is close to the ideal peer experiment. Their later maths scores do not
jump at all. The reduced form divided by the first stage (−.02/.80 ≈ −.025, our arithmetic) is
essentially the reported 2SLS estimate. Peer quality that rises by .8σ produces **no** gain in
achievement, against the .25σ that the naive regression implies. (The standard error is clustered by
applicant, because each pupil contributes a 7th- and an 8th-grade score.) [@angrist_mastering]

Two caveats from the authors keep this honest:

- **The exclusion restriction picks a channel.** 2SLS assumes qualifying affects scores *only* through
  peer quality. Exam schools also have more white classmates, which matters for the bussing debate
  (*"Separate educational facilities are inherently unequal,"* 1954). Swap peer quality for share of white
  peers and you get the same zero, because the reduced form has not changed. [@angrist_mastering]
- **The likely bias is upward.** Other things that change at the cutoff, such as better teachers and more
  advanced courses, probably help. If they are left in the error term, the 2SLS estimate is too *big* for a
  pure peer effect. *"All the more surprising, then, that this estimate turns out to be zero."*
  [@angrist_mastering]

## Spillovers in experiments: non-interference

Gerber and Green write non-interference as $Y_i(\mathbf{d}) = Y_i(d_i)$: subject $i$'s potential outcomes
depend only on $i$'s own treatment, not on the whole vector $\mathbf{d}$ of who got what. The assumption is
also called *individualistic treatment response* (Manski) or SUTVA (Rubin). Gerber and Green prefer to
keep **"no interference between units"** in mind, because the acronym hides the idea. [@gerber_green] They
also distinguish *global* non-interference, which must hold however many units are treated, from a weaker
*local* version for one fixed number treated. The local version may stop holding if a programme is scaled up
or down. [@gerber_green]

### A small example of the bias

A shop will honour one of three sales agents as "Salesperson of the Month", chosen by lottery.
[@gerber_green]

| August sales | Mary honoured | Peter honoured | Limor honoured | Nobody honoured |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mary | 100 | 50 | 70 | 70 |
| Peter | 50 | 50 | 50 | 50 |
| Limor | 90 | 50 | 90 | 90 |

[@gerber_green]

The researcher defines the effect against the "nobody honoured" column: $(30 + 0 + 0)/3 = 10$. But
honouring Peter demoralises the other two. The three lotteries give apparent effects of 30, 0 and 30,
which average **20**: double the intended estimand. Each untreated outcome depends on *who else* was treated.
[@gerber_green]

### Where interference comes from

Gerber and Green list the usual suspects: [@gerber_green]

- **Contagion.** Being vaccinated matters less if everyone around you is vaccinated.
- **Displacement.** Police in one place may push crime next door, which exaggerates the apparent effect.
- **Communication.** Information spreads from the treated to the nominally untreated, which shrinks the
  apparent effect.
- **Social comparison.** Housing help for the treatment group can make the control group rate its own
  housing lower.
- **Deterrence.** Random audits of some local governments may deter corruption in others that hear about
  them.
- **Persistence and memory.** In studies that follow a subject over time, earlier treatments can carry
  forward (see *Interference across time* below).

Interference can also come from the experimenter. A new curriculum may lead teachers to give the treatment
group more time and the control group less. [@gerber_green]

### Design for it: multi-level randomisation

Gerber and Green's mail experiment is set in two-voter households. In 5,000 households both voters get
get-out-the-vote mail, in 5,000 neither does, and in 10,000 exactly one, chosen at random.
[@gerber_green] That gives four groups of 10,000 people and four potential outcomes per voter:
$Y_{00}, Y_{01}, Y_{10}, Y_{11}$, where the first subscript is the *housemate's* treatment and the second
is one's own. Comparing the groups identifies the direct effect (with and without a treated housemate) and
the **spillover** effect (for treated and untreated voters).

Three lessons follow: [@gerber_green]

- **"No unmodelled spillovers."** This design does not violate non-interference, because it allows for the
  spillover within a household. It still assumes nothing spills *between* households. A useful check:
  there should be at least as many randomly assigned groups as columns of potential outcomes.
- **Interference is not clustering.** Housemates may vote alike because they share a background. That
  affects *standard errors*. Interference affects *bias*. Either can occur without the other.
- **Ignoring spillovers biases the direct effect either way.** Pooling everyone who got mail against
  everyone who did not overstates the direct effect if spillovers are larger among the treated, and
  understates it if they are larger among the untreated. Without a multi-level design this is guesswork.

Data from such a design are easy to read. Sinclair, McConnell and Green mailed all, half or none of the
households in each nine-digit zip code. Among single-voter households: [@gerber_green]

| Group | Voted / registered | Turnout (our arithmetic) |
|---|---|---|
| Untreated, zip code untreated | 1,021 / 6,217 | 16.4% |
| Untreated, half the zip code treated | 526 / 3,316 | 15.9% |
| Treated, half the zip code treated | 620 / 2,949 | 21.0% |
| Treated, whole zip code treated | 1,316 / 6,377 | 20.6% |

Our reading (the book leaves it as an exercise): the **direct effect** is about 5 percentage points, in
line with the 4–5 points such social-pressure mail usually produces, while untreated neighbours of treated
households vote at much the same rate as voters in untouched zip codes (−0.6 points). On these numbers, the
effect did not travel between households.

## Spatial spillovers: unequal chances of exposure

Often spillovers are not neatly confined to a household or school. A clinic helps neighbouring villages, a
police patrol affects nearby blocks. Two things then go wrong. [@gerber_green]

**Measuring proximity needs a model.** Walking distance is right for norms passed on foot, wrong for norms
passed by mobile phone. For police, *minutes* to the nearest responder may matter more than miles. A
fixed radius is a common compromise, but choosing it is another source of uncertainty. Flexible models of
how effects decay with distance are often too imprecise to help. [@gerber_green]

**Exposure is not randomly assigned with equal probability, even when treatment is.** A unit in a cluster
of other units is far more likely to be next to a treated one than an isolated unit. If those
probabilities are related to potential outcomes, simple comparisons are biased. The remedy is the one used
for blocked designs: **weight each unit by the inverse of its probability of being in the condition it
ended up in**, and drop units that could never be exposed. [@gerber_green]

Gerber and Green's toy example has five villages on a line, at positions A, B, C, D and F (E is empty),
and one clinic placed at random. Being next to a treated village has probability 0.2 for the end villages
A and D, 0.4 for B and C, and zero for F. With the book's potential outcomes, the true direct effect is 1
and the true spillover effect is 0.5. [@gerber_green] Across the five possible placements:

- the **inverse-probability-weighted** difference in means averages 0.81 for the direct effect and 0.33 for
  the spillover: a little biased in so small a sample, but in the right direction;
- the **unweighted** difference in means for the spillover averages **−1.0**. It says spillovers *hurt*,
  when they help. An unweighted regression on "treated" and "next to treated" dummies gives the same
  answer. [@gerber_green]

The figure re-runs both panels of this story.

```{python}
#| label: fig-peer-spillover
#| fig-cap: "Left: three peer regressions on simulated schools where there is no peer effect of any kind (our own simulation, 400 replications; 150 schools of 25 pupils, a school-wide shock, pupils assigned to schools at random). Regressing pupils on their school's mean gives exactly 1 every time. Leaving the pupil out still gives about 0.7, all of it from the shared school shock. Regressing on peers' ex-ante background (books at home) is centred on the true zero. Right: Gerber and Green's five-village clinic example, enumerated over all five placements. Inverse-probability weighting gives estimates near the true effects; the unweighted spillover estimate (orange) averages −1.0 and gets the sign wrong."
#| code-fold: true
#| code-summary: "Show the figure code"

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# ---- Left: peer regressions with no true peer effect -------------------------------
rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
J, n, R = 150, 25, 400
g = np.repeat(np.arange(J), n)

def slope(x, y):
    xc = x - x.mean()
    return (xc * (y - y.mean())).sum() / (xc ** 2).sum()

est = np.zeros((R, 3))
for r in range(R):
    shock = rng.normal(0, 0.35, J)[g]          # a good (or bad) principal: shared by the school
    books = rng.normal(0, 1, J * n)             # ex-ante family background
    s = 0.5 * books + shock + rng.normal(0, 1, J * n)   # own background matters; peers do not
    s_sum, b_sum = np.bincount(g, s), np.bincount(g, books)
    mean_incl = (s_sum / n)[g]
    mean_loo = (s_sum[g] - s) / (n - 1)
    books_loo = (b_sum[g] - books) / (n - 1)
    est[r] = slope(mean_incl, s), slope(mean_loo, s), slope(books_loo, s)

# ---- Right: Gerber & Green's clinic example (Tables 8.2-8.3) -------------------------
Y00 = np.array([0, 6, 0, 6, 6.])              # villages at A, B, C, D, F
Y10 = np.array([2, 2, 4, 6, np.nan])          # next to the clinic (F never is)
Y01 = np.array([0, 10, 4, 6, 3.])             # gets the clinic
nbrs = {0: [1], 1: [0, 2], 2: [1, 3], 3: [2], 4: []}
p00 = np.array([.6, .4, .4, .6, .8])
p10 = np.array([.2, .4, .4, .2, 0.])
p01 = np.full(5, .2)

def wmean(y, p):
    return (y / p).sum() / (1 / p).sum()

w_dir, w_spl, u_spl = [], [], []
for t in range(5):
    cond = np.zeros(5, int)                    # 0 = untreated, 1 = next to clinic, 2 = clinic
    cond[t] = 2
    cond[nbrs[t]] = 1
    y = np.where(cond == 2, Y01, np.where(cond == 1, Y10, Y00))
    c, s_, tr = cond == 0, cond == 1, cond == 2
    w_dir.append(wmean(y[tr], p01[tr]) - wmean(y[c], p00[c]))
    if s_.any():                               # spillover contrast exists in 4 of 5 placements
        c_ok = c & (p10 > 0)                   # F can never be exposed: excluded
        w_spl.append(wmean(y[s_], p10[s_]) - wmean(y[c_ok], p00[c_ok]))
        u_spl.append(y[s_].mean() - y[c].mean())

truth = [np.mean(Y01 - Y00), np.nanmean(Y10 - Y00)]
weighted = [np.mean(w_dir), np.mean(w_spl)]

fig, (ax1, ax2) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(10, 4.4), gridspec_kw={"width_ratios": [1.15, 1]})

labels = ["own score on\nschool mean\n(incl. self)", "own score on\npeers' mean\n(leave-one-out)",
          "own score on\npeers' books\n(ex ante)"]
cols = ["#161613", "#5e5e54", "#8a8a7e"]
for k in range(3):
    jitter = rng.uniform(-0.18, 0.18, R)
    ax1.scatter(est[:, k], k + jitter, s=7, color=cols[k], alpha=0.45)
    ax1.plot([est[:, k].mean()] * 2, [k - 0.3, k + 0.3], color=cols[k], lw=2.4)
    ax1.text(est[:, k].mean() + 0.03, k + 0.36, f"mean {est[:, k].mean():.2f}", ha="left",
             fontsize=8, color=cols[k])
ax1.axvline(0, color="#161613", lw=1, ls=":")
ax1.text(0.02, 2.62, "true peer effect = 0", fontsize=8, color="#5e5e54")
ax1.set_yticks(range(3))
ax1.set_yticklabels(labels, fontsize=8)
ax1.set_ylim(-0.6, 2.85)
ax1.set_xlim(-0.5, 1.25)
ax1.invert_yaxis()
ax1.set_xlabel("estimated 'peer effect'")
ax1.set_title("Peer regressions when peers do not matter")

x = np.arange(2)
wdt = 0.26
ax2.bar(x - wdt, truth, wdt, color="#161613", label="true effect")
ax2.bar(x, weighted, wdt, color="#b7b6a8", label="IPW difference in means (average)")
ax2.bar([1 + wdt], [np.mean(u_spl)], wdt, color="#e8531d", label="unweighted difference in means")
for xi, v in zip([-wdt, 0, 1 - wdt, 1, 1 + wdt], [truth[0], weighted[0], truth[1], weighted[1],
                                                   np.mean(u_spl)]):
    ax2.text(xi, v + (0.06 if v >= 0 else -0.16), f"{v:.2f}", ha="center", fontsize=8)
ax2.axhline(0, color="#161613", lw=0.8)
ax2.set_xticks(x)
ax2.set_xticklabels(["direct effect\n(own clinic)", "spillover effect\n(clinic next door)"])
ax2.set_ylim(-1.35, 1.35)
ax2.set_ylabel("effect on village health")
ax2.set_title("Five villages, one clinic: weight by exposure")
ax2.legend(fontsize=8, frameon=False, loc="lower left")

fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

In the left panel, the first regression is not wrong by a bit: it cannot give any answer except 1. The
second one is a real estimate of the wrong thing, the strength of the school shock relative to pupil-level
noise. Only the third, which uses something about peers that the shock could not touch, is centred on the
truth. The right panel is the spatial version of the same lesson: the unweighted comparison is dominated
by *which* villages happen to be exposed, and those villages differ in their untreated health.

### A larger case: police hotspots

Gerber and Green's hotspot example has 30 crime hotspots, 10 of them given extra patrols. Untreated crime
rises with the number of hotspots within 750 metres. Patrols reduce crime on the spot by 5, push crime up
by 5 in untreated hotspots within 500 metres of a patrol, and the two together reduce crime by 7.
[@gerber_green] Across 100,000 simulated experiments:

| Estimator | Direct effect (true −5) | Spillover (true +5) | Both (true −7) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ignore spillovers: treated vs untreated | −7.3 | — | — |
| Unweighted: exposed vs unexposed untreated | — | −3.8 | — |
| IPW, 30 hotspots | −4.2 | 7.0 | −3.8 |
| IPW, 300 hotspots (100 treated) | −4.9 | 5.2 | −6.7 |

[@gerber_green]

The 11 hotspots that could never be exposed estimate the direct effect well on their own (−4.9).
Weighting fixes most of the problem, but not all of it with 30 hotspots: neighbouring hotspots tend to be
exposed together, so assignment to spillover is *implicitly clustered*, and the weights themselves change
from draw to draw. Both biases fade as the number of units grows. [@gerber_green]

Two practical points complete the picture. First, **non-experimental units are free data**. 100 nearby
locations that could not be treated but could be exposed estimate the spillover at 5.2 (SE 1.1). Not
measuring them is *"like leaving money on the table."* [@gerber_green] Second, **design helps more than
analysis**: isolated *pairs* of hotspots, randomised to 00/01/10/11 like the mail experiment, remove the
clustering bias. Simulating the randomisation in advance shows how exposure will fall. Because model choice
(the radius, the decay) is itself uncertain, results should be shown under several models. [@gerber_green]

## Interference across time

Following one subject over several periods turns each period into a "unit", and non-interference becomes
two assumptions about time: [@gerber_green]

- **No anticipation:** outcomes are not affected by treatments still to come.
- **No persistence:** outcomes are not affected by earlier treatments. Laboratory studies enforce this
  with *washout periods*, the time-based version of a buffer zone between villages.

Gerber and Green's pupil-dilation example has two periods and one dose of eye drops, given in period 1 or
2 by a coin flip. Untreated dilation drifts from 3 mm to 4 mm; treated dilation is 7 mm. The two possible
experiments give estimates of 4 and 3, which average to the true 3.5, *but only because* the example
assumes no anticipation and no persistence. [@gerber_green] Randomising the timing rules out coincidence
with trends. It does nothing for the two assumptions that matter most. That may be why within-subject
designs are more common in the physical sciences than the social ones. [@gerber_green]

**Waitlist (stepped-wedge) designs** combine both approaches. Everyone is treated eventually and chance
decides *when*, which also makes the design easier to defend to participants. Gerber and Green's TV-advertising
example has eight media markets: two start ads in week 1, two in week 2, two in week 3, and two never do.
That allows 2,520 possible assignments. [@gerber_green] Assuming effects last exactly two weeks, IPW gives
an immediate effect of 2.72 (95% interval 0.23 to 5.51) and an immediate-plus-lagged effect of 4.13 (0.89
to 7.40). A naive treated-vs-untreated comparison averages 0.58 across all assignments, far below the true
effects of 2 and 3. [@gerber_green] The precision comes largely from the assumption about how long effects
last.

## Common pitfalls

::: {.callout-warning title="Where this goes wrong"}

- **Regressing an outcome on its own group mean.** The slope is 1 by construction and says nothing about
  peers. [@angrist_mostly_harmless]

- **Trusting a leave-one-out peer mean.** Any shock shared by the group (a principal, a local labour
  market, a teacher) shows up as a "peer effect". Use peers' characteristics fixed before the outcome,
  ideally with as-good-as-random variation in who your peers are. [@angrist_mostly_harmless]

- **Reading a group-mean coefficient as a social return.** With own and group values both included, the
  group coefficient is $\phi(\rho_1 - \rho_0)$: any gap between OLS and grouped IV, including one caused by
  measurement error. [@angrist_mostly_harmless]

- **Forgetting that an IV exclusion restriction chooses a channel.** The exam-school zero is equally a zero
  for peer ability, peer race or anything else that jumps at the cutoff. The reduced form does not know
  which channel you meant. [@angrist_mastering]

- **Defining the estimand without saying what others receive.** "The effect of mail" differs depending on
  whether the housemate got mail. The Salesperson-of-the-Month example shows a 100% overstatement from this
  alone. [@gerber_green]

- **Comparing exposed with unexposed units without weights.** In spatial and network settings, exposure
  probability varies with location and is often related to outcomes. Unweighted comparisons can get the
  sign wrong. [@gerber_green]

- **Confusing correlated outcomes with interference.** Correlation within households or schools is a
  standard-error problem; interference is a bias problem. Clustering the SEs does not fix spillovers.
  [@gerber_green]

- **Calling a "no spillover" finding a mere null.** If it holds up, it greatly simplifies every later
  experiment in that setting. Gerber and Green treat it as a result worth reporting. [@gerber_green]

:::

## Appendix · the mathematics {.unnumbered}

::: {.math-appendix}

**Why regressing on the group mean gives exactly 1.** With $n_j$ members in group $j$, group means
$\bar S_j$ and grand mean $\bar S$, [@angrist_mostly_harmless]

$$
\frac{\sum_j\sum_i s_{ij}(\bar S_j - \bar S)}{\sum_j n_j(\bar S_j - \bar S)^2}
= \frac{\sum_j(\bar S_j - \bar S)\,n_j\bar S_j}{\sum_j n_j(\bar S_j - \bar S)^2} = 1,
$$

because $\sum_j n_j(\bar S_j - \bar S)\bar S = 0$, so the numerator equals
$\sum_j n_j(\bar S_j - \bar S)^2$.

**The social-returns decomposition.** Write $Y_{ij} = \mu + \pi_0 s_i + \pi_1 \bar S_j + \nu_{ij}$ and set
$\tau_i = s_i - \bar S_j$. Then $Y_{ij} = \mu + \pi_0\tau_i + (\pi_0+\pi_1)\bar S_j + \nu_{ij}$, and
$\tau_i$ is uncorrelated with $\bar S_j$ by construction, so the slope on $\bar S_j$ alone is
$\rho_1 = \pi_0 + \pi_1$, and [@angrist_mostly_harmless]

$$
\pi_0 = \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(s_i - \bar S_j, Y_{ij})}{V(s_i) - V(\bar S_j)}
= \rho_0\,\phi + \rho_1(1-\phi) = \rho_1 + \phi(\rho_0 - \rho_1),
\qquad \phi = \frac{V(s_i)}{V(s_i) - V(\bar S_j)},
$$

so $\pi_1 = \rho_1 - \pi_0 = \phi(\rho_1 - \rho_0)$. Since $V(\bar S_j)/V(s_i)$ is the $R^2$ from
regressing $s_i$ on group dummies, $\phi = 1/(1-R^2)$.

**The leave-one-out slope under a common shock (our addition).** Suppose
$s_{ij} = a_j + e_{ij}$ with $V(a_j) = \sigma_a^2$, $V(e_{ij}) = \sigma_e^2$ and no peer effect. Then
$\bar S_{(i)j} = a_j + \bar e_{(i)j}$, and for large samples the slope is

$$
\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(s_{ij}, \bar S_{(i)j})}{V(\bar S_{(i)j})}
= \frac{\sigma_a^2}{\sigma_a^2 + \sigma_e^2/(n-1)} .
$$

In the figure, $\sigma_a^2 = 0.1225$, $\sigma_e^2 = 1.25$ and $n = 25$, giving about 0.70: the share of
the leave-one-out mean's variance that comes from the shared shock.

**Fuzzy RD as a ratio.** With qualification $D_i$ as the instrument for peer quality $\bar X_{(i)}$ and a
linear control for the running variable, the just-identified 2SLS estimate is the reduced-form jump over
the first-stage jump: $\lambda = \rho/\phi_{\text{fs}} \approx -0.02/0.80$. (See the [RD
appendix](regression-discontinuity.qmd) for the general Wald-at-the-cutoff form.)

**Inverse-probability-weighted exposure contrasts.** For exposure conditions $k$ (here 00, 10, 01) with
unit-specific probabilities $p_{k,i}$, the weighted difference in means for condition $k$ against control
is [@gerber_green]

$$
\widehat{E}[Y_k - Y_{00}] = \frac{\sum_{i\in k} Y_i/p_{k,i}}{\sum_{i\in k} 1/p_{k,i}}
- \frac{\sum_{i\in 00} Y_i/p_{00,i}}{\sum_{i\in 00} 1/p_{00,i}},
$$

using only units with $p_{k,i} > 0$ and $p_{00,i} > 0$. Because the denominators change from one
assignment to the next, it is slightly biased in small samples. Dividing by $N$ instead of the sum of the
weights (a difference in weighted *totals*) is unbiased but imprecise: in the clinic example it gives
−1.25 for the draw that gives 0.40, and 1.50 for the draw that gives 1.85. [@gerber_green]

**Counting assignments in the waitlist design.** Choosing 2 of 8 markets for week 1, 2 of the remaining 6
for week 2 and 2 of the last 4 for week 3 gives
$\binom{8}{2}\binom{6}{2}\binom{4}{2} = 28 \times 15 \times 6 = 2{,}520$. [@gerber_green]
