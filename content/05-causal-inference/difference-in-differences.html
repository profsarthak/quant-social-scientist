Difference-in-Differences

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source

Let each group be its own control through time — subtract away the fixed gaps and the common shocks, and what’s left is the policy.

When a policy hits one group and not another, you can’t just compare the two — they differed to begin with. And you can’t just track the treated group over time — everything else moved too. Difference-in- differences does both subtractions at once: the treated group’s change minus the control group’s change. The first difference erases fixed group gaps; the second erases shocks common to all. What survives is the treatment effect — if the groups would have moved in parallel anyway.

Why it matters

RD needs a sharp cutoff; IV needs a clever instrument. DiD needs only a policy change, a comparison group, and data before and after — which is why it is the most widely used quasi-experimental design in economics and policy. (Cunningham 2021) It exploits a different kind of as-good-as-random variation: the timing of a treatment that some units get and others don’t. And it is the design where the field has most recently discovered it was doing things wrong — making this entry both a how-to and a cautionary tale. (Baker et al. 2026)

The canonical 2×2

The simplest DiD has two groups — treated (D=1) and control (D=0) — and two periods, before and after. The estimator double-differences four means: (Baker et al. 2026) \hat\delta_{DD}=\big(\bar Y_{\text{treat,post}}-\bar Y_{\text{treat,pre}}\big) -\big(\bar Y_{\text{ctrl,post}}-\bar Y_{\text{ctrl,pre}}\big). Read the two subtractions: (Baker et al. 2026)

  • The first difference (post − pre, within each group) wipes out every time-invariant group trait — the treated group can be richer, sicker, bigger; if it doesn’t change, it cancels.
  • The second difference (treated change − control change) wipes out any shock common to both groups in that period — a recession, a seasonal swing, a nationwide trend.

Two confounding layers removed by arithmetic, no controls required. What remains is attributed to the treatment.

NoteCard & Krueger and the minimum wage

The study that made DiD famous — and overturned a textbook prediction. In 1992 New Jersey raised its minimum wage from $4.25 to $5.05; neighbouring Pennsylvania held at $4.25. Card & Krueger surveyed ~400 fast-food restaurants before and after, measuring full-time-equivalent employment: (Cunningham 2021)

Pre Post Change
New Jersey (treated) 20.44 21.03 +0.59
Pennsylvania (control) 23.33 21.17 −2.16

DiD = (+0.59)-(-2.16) = \mathbf{+2.76}. Employment fell in Pennsylvania (a common regional shock) but rose relative to it in New Jersey — the opposite of the competitive prediction that a higher minimum wage destroys jobs. Note the logic: NJ’s raw +0.59 alone would be misleading; only by netting out PA’s -2.16 regional slump does the policy’s relative effect appear. (Cunningham 2021)

The one assumption: parallel trends

DiD’s entire credibility rests on parallel trends: absent the treatment, the treated and control groups would have moved in parallel. (Baker et al. 2026) The control group’s change is used as the treated group’s missing counterfactual change — valid only if their paths would have stayed parallel. Two things to internalise: (Baker et al. 2026)

  • It is fundamentally untestable. It concerns the treated group’s unobserved no-treatment path after the intervention — a counterfactual we never see.
  • Pre-trends are the evidence. If the groups tracked each other before treatment, it’s more plausible they’d have continued. So researchers plot the pre-period and check for parallel paths — necessary support, not proof. (Levels may differ wildly; it is the slopes that must match.)

TWFE and the event study

In practice DiD is run as a two-way fixed effects (TWFE) regression, which generalises the four means to many units and periods: (Baker et al. 2026) Y_{it}=\eta_i+\theta_t+\beta D_{it}+\epsilon_{it}. The unit fixed effects \eta_i absorb all time-invariant differences between units (the first difference); the time fixed effects \theta_t absorb shocks common to all units in a period (the second difference); \beta is the DiD effect. (Baker et al. 2026) It is the panel/fixed-effects machinery of the next entry — DiD is a fixed-effects design.

The event study enriches this by replacing the single D_{it} with dummies for periods relative to treatment: (Baker et al. 2026) Y_{it}=\eta_i+\theta_t+\sum_{\tau=-q}^{m}\delta_\tau D_{i,\tau}+\epsilon_{it}. The leads (\tau<0) should be near zero — a visual parallel-trends check; the lags (\tau\ge0) trace how the effect grows, fades, or plateaus after treatment. (Baker et al. 2026) An event study is the single most persuasive DiD graphic: flat before, a clear break after.

From four numbers to a state–year panel

Angrist and Pischke teach DiD through two studies that show how the design grows from a 2×2 into a full regression. (Angrist and Pischke 2015)

Mississippi, 1930–31. After the Caldwell banking collapse, Mississippi’s two Federal Reserve districts responded in opposite ways. Within four weeks the Atlanta Fed (Sixth District) had increased lending to troubled banks by about 40%. Over the same period, St. Louis (Eighth District) lending fell almost 10%. A district border running through the state turned this into a natural experiment. (Angrist and Pischke 2015)

Banks open, 1 July 1930 1931 Change
Sixth District (easy money) 135 121 −14
Eighth District (tight money) 165 132 −33

In 1931 the Sixth District had fewer banks open, which on its face looks like easy money backfiring. The comparison in changes reverses that reading: (-14)-(-33)=\mathbf{19} banks saved, more than 10% of those operating in the Sixth District in 1930. Fitting the same comparison as a regression on twelve district–year points gives about 21 banks, with a standard error of about 11. With only two districts, though, there are only two clusters, which Angrist and Pischke flag as an important caution (see Inference below). (Angrist and Pischke 2015)

The minimum legal drinking age, 51 states × 14 years. The MLDA follow-up shows what a real panel adds. (Angrist and Pischke 2015)

  • Continuous treatment intensity. States differed in how much they liberalised drinking. So instead of a single treated/post dummy, the regressor is \text{LEGAL}_{st}, the share of 18–20-year-olds allowed to drink in state s and year t. It is scaled for mid-year changes: Alabama’s age-19 law took effect in July 1975, so it counts for half that year. The regression then has state effects, year effects and \text{LEGAL}_{st}, estimated on 714 observations.
  • The result. Legal access caused about 11 extra deaths per 100,000 18–20-year-olds, seven or eight of them from motor-vehicle accidents (standard error about 2.5). There is little effect on deaths from internal causes, the placebo outcome. This agrees with the RD estimates from the same book.
  • State-specific trends. With many periods you can relax common trends by giving each state its own linear trend, \theta_k\cdot t. Effects are then identified from sharp deviations from each state’s own smooth path. The book uses two invented states to show why this matters. Allatsea lowers its MLDA in 1975 and neighbouring Alabaster does not. If Allatsea’s death rate was already rising faster, a plain DiD credits the MLDA with a gap that predates it. The trend model credits only the extra jump in 1975. Adding trends barely moved the MLDA estimates, which supports a causal reading.
  • The limit of trend controls. “If treatment effects emerge only gradually”, a state trend can absorb the effect itself, and the trend model returns an imprecise, inconclusive answer. The same book’s child-labour-law study shows the opposite danger. After state trends are added, its first stage and reduced form collapse, the 2SLS estimate jumps to an implausible 0.399 with a standard error almost as large, and the authors call it “a failed research design”. (Angrist and Pischke 2015)
  • Time-varying controls. States might raise beer taxes at the same time as they tighten the MLDA. Controlling for state beer taxes leaves the MLDA estimates essentially unchanged. (Angrist and Pischke 2015)

The DiD revolution: when TWFE lies

Here is the cautionary tale, and why this entry cites a 2020s practitioner’s guide. When units are treated at different times (staggered adoption), the trusty TWFE regression can become biased — even sign-flipped. (Baker et al. 2026)

  • Forbidden comparisons. With staggering, TWFE implicitly uses already-treated units as controls for later-treated ones. If effects change over time (dynamic effects), those already-treated “controls” are themselves still moving because of their own treatment, contaminating the comparison. (Baker et al. 2026)
  • Goodman-Bacon decomposition. TWFE’s \hat\beta is a weighted average of all possible 2×2 DiDs, with weights that are always positive (bigger for larger groups and for groups treated nearer the middle of the panel). The trouble is which 2×2s are in the average: many use already-treated units as controls. Translate those comparisons back into true effects and some cohort-period effects enter with negative weight. The estimate can come out the wrong sign even if every unit’s true effect is positive. (Baker et al. 2026)
  • New estimators. “Forward-engineered” methods fix it by using only clean controls: Callaway–Sant’Anna (compare each treated cohort only to never-treated or not-yet-treated units), Sun–Abraham (a saturated cohort-by-time interaction), and de Chaisemartin–D’Haultfœuille (robust when treatment switches on and off). (Baker et al. 2026)

The lesson is humbling and current: a method everyone trusted for decades was quietly mis-estimating staggered rollouts, and the corpus’s most recent source exists to set it right.

Inference: why your DiD standard errors are too small

Getting the point estimate right is half the job. The other half is being honest about how sure you are, and here DiD has a trap that sat unnoticed in applied work for years.

The problem is that the policy varies by group, not by person. A minimum wage changes by state and year, but the data are thousands of individual workers or restaurants. Angrist and Pischke split the error into two parts: a state–year shock shared by everyone in that state that year (a regional business cycle), and an individual component. (Angrist and Pischke 2009) Those shared shocks are bad news in two ways.

  • With only two groups, more data does not help. In the Card–Krueger 2×2, the DiD estimate converges not to the true effect but to the effect plus the difference-in-differences of the regional shocks. Surveying more restaurants in New Jersey and Pennsylvania cannot tell a minimum-wage effect apart from “New Jersey’s economy held steady while Pennsylvania’s dipped.” (Angrist and Pischke 2009) The only fix is more groups or more periods. For example, Card (1992) used all 51 states, so that the shocks could average out. (Angrist and Pischke 2009)
  • The shocks are serially correlated. If Pennsylvania is doing badly this month, it will probably be doing badly next month too. Bertrand, Duflo and Mullainathan (2004) showed that with many years of group-level data and serially correlated outcomes, conventional standard errors badly understate the estimator’s true variability. Placebo “policies” then come out “significant” far too often. (Cunningham 2021; Angrist and Pischke 2009)

The standard remedies are: (Cunningham 2021)

  1. Cluster at the group level. Angrist and Pischke call this passing the clustering buck one level higher: cluster by state, not state×year. Clustering allows any correlation among a state’s residuals, including correlation across its years, so it absorbs the serial correlation that state fixed effects leave behind. (Angrist and Pischke 2009) This is by far the most common choice.
  2. Block bootstrap. Resample whole states, with all their years, rather than individual observations (see the bootstrap).
  3. Collapse the time dimension. Average into one pre-period and one post-period so there is no serial correlation left to mishandle. With staggered adoption, regress the outcome on state and year effects (plus covariates), then compare the treated states’ residuals before and after treatment.

The catch: clustering needs many clusters. Clustering fixes the correlation problem by treating the group as the unit of information, so the effective sample size becomes the number of groups. In Card–Krueger that number is two. (Cunningham 2021) Angrist and Pischke, borrowing Douglas Adams’s “answer to everything,” frame the question as are 42 clusters enough? With few clusters, the clustered formula itself underestimates the correlation it is meant to correct. (Angrist and Pischke 2009) Their small-cluster options:

  • judge significance against a t distribution with g-k degrees of freedom rather than the normal (g = clusters, k = regressors). This widens intervals when g is small;
  • use bias-reduced (HC2-style) cluster corrections, though these break down in the standard DiD setup with a full set of state dummies;
  • use a block bootstrap, which simulations show tends to beat plain clustering with few groups;
  • above all, show that the conclusions survive an analysis of group averages, a conservative and transparent check.

Their rough guidance is that 51 US states are reasonably safe for ordinary clustering, while Canada’s ten provinces are well below 42, though one study still finds clustering does reasonably well even there. (Angrist and Pischke 2009) The general lesson is humbling: if your regressor varies only across a few states or years, your real sample size is the number of states or years, however many individual records you hold. (Angrist and Pischke 2009) This is the DiD form of the Moulton problem.

Threats beyond the pre-trend plot

Cunningham lists four threats to a DiD design: non-parallel trends, compositional change (the people in the groups change over time), the trade-off between long-run effects and reliability (the further out you look, the more else has changed), and dependence on functional form (parallel in levels need not mean parallel in logs). (Cunningham 2021) The first is the most dangerous, and it often comes from who chose to be treated:

  • The Ashenfelter dip. People who enter job-training programmes typically see their earnings dip just before enrolling, which is often why they enrol. Earnings tend to revert to the mean, so they would have recovered anyway. A DiD of participants against non-participants credits that rebound to the programme and is biased upward. (Cunningham 2021)
  • Targeted rollouts. An NGO that picks the villages that look most promising, or the most distressed, has chosen treated units by their trajectory. That is selection bias in the trends, and it breaks parallel trends by construction. (Cunningham 2021)

The defences are all forms of placebo analysis. Check that the event-study leads are near zero. Use a second control group; carried through, this becomes the triple difference in combining & triangulating designs. Or run the same model on an outcome that should not respond. Cunningham’s example: if Craigslist’s erotic-services section affected only female sex workers, the same model should show no effect on manslaughters or male murders. An “effect” there would point to a general violence trend, not the policy. (Cunningham 2021)

Covariates and weights: where a simple DiD quietly changes its question

Two routine DiD choices are where practice drifts furthest from what the estimate means: whether to weight, and how to add control variables. The Practitioner’s Guide works through both with one running example, the 2014 Affordable Care Act Medicaid expansion and county mortality among adults aged 20–64 (deaths per 100,000). (Baker et al. 2026)

Weights pick the estimand. The comparison is 2014 expansion counties against counties in states that had not expanded by 2019, from 2013 to 2014: (Baker et al. 2026)

Expansion: 2013 → 2014 No expansion: 2013 → 2014 DiD
Unweighted (average county) 419.2 → 428.5 (+9.3) 474.0 → 483.1 (+9.1) +0.1
Population-weighted (average adult) 322.7 → 326.5 (+3.7) 376.4 → 382.7 (+6.3) −2.6

These two rows answer different questions; they are not two versions of one estimate. The unweighted DiD is about the average expansion county. The weighted DiD is about the average adult living in one, which gives California much more say: 27.7% of the adults in the 2014-expansion states but only 5.4% of the counties. The guide is blunt that comparing the two “does not show whether weighting matters”, because they “reflect different target parameters”. Decide which question you are asking before you look at the numbers. (Baker et al. 2026) Angrist and Pischke take a softer line on their MLDA panel. Weighting can buy precision when small states’ death rates are noisy, but “you should hope … that regression estimates from your state-year panel are not highly sensitive to weighting”. (Angrist and Pischke 2015) The two views fit together. If the effect is the same everywhere, weighting changes only precision, and stable estimates are reassuring. If the effect varies, weighting changes the question, and a gap between the two is a finding about heterogeneity to report, not noise.

Covariates make the assumption conditional. At baseline, expansion and non-expansion counties differ a lot in share White, poverty and median income. Each of these has a normalized difference (the gap in means divided by a pooled standard deviation) well above the usual 0.25 warning line. (Baker et al. 2026) If those traits also predict mortality trends, unconditional parallel trends is doubtful. The natural fallback is conditional parallel trends: among counties with the same covariates, treated and untreated would have followed the same trend. This also needs overlap: every treated county must have comparable untreated counties. (Baker et al. 2026)

Why you can’t just add X to the TWFE regression. It looks like the obvious move. The guide explains four ways it goes wrong: (Baker et al. 2026)

  1. Fixed traits drop out. The county fixed effect absorbs a county’s 2013 poverty rate. That rate can only adjust for trends if you interact it with the post-period dummy.
  2. Changes are not levels. A time-varying X_{it} means you are controlling for changes. In the guide’s words, “areas that are poor are not the same as areas that are becoming poor”.
  3. Bad controls. Suppose the expansion itself lowered poverty. Then controlling for 2014 poverty (or its change) partly controls away the effect. This is the bad-control problem in DiD form.
  4. Wrong weights even when specified right. Caetano and Callaway show that the TWFE coefficient averages the covariate-specific effects with weights that need not be convex. It can come out negative even when the effect is positive at every covariate value. The coefficient is only clean if the effect is the same in every stratum, and research on Medicaid effects by income says it is not.

The data show how fragile this is. Adding covariates to TWFE moves the unweighted estimate from 0.12 to −2.35 or to −0.49. The only difference between those two is whether the covariates enter as 2013 levels or as yearly values. (Baker et al. 2026)

The forward-engineered fix. Start from the target, the \text{ATT} under conditional parallel trends, and build an estimator for it directly. The guide offers three, all close relatives of matching and propensity scores: (Baker et al. 2026)

  • Regression adjustment (RA). Model how untreated counties’ mortality changed as a function of their baseline covariates. Predict that change for each treated county, then subtract the prediction from what the county actually did. In the weighted Medicaid data:
    • the model predicts an untreated change of +7.2 for the average expansion county (the raw untreated change was +6.3);
    • expansion counties actually changed by +3.7;
    • so the estimate is 3.7-7.2=\mathbf{-3.5}.
  • Inverse probability weighting (IPW). Model each county’s probability of expanding from its covariates. Reweight the untreated counties to look like the treated ones, then compare changes. Standard software trims untreated units with scores above 0.995 so the weights stay sane.
  • Doubly robust (DR). Combine the two. The estimate is consistent if either the outcome model or the propensity model is right. The guide recommends DR because it adds “additional ‘protection’”.
Medicaid ATT(2014), 2013 covariates RA IPW DR
Unweighted −1.62 (4.68) −0.86 (4.61) −1.23 (4.94)
Population-weighted −3.46 (2.39) −3.84 (3.39) −3.76 (3.24)

The weighted estimates agree closely across the three methods. Every interval is wide enough to include zero. The honest reading is “probably a small reduction, imprecisely measured”. (Baker et al. 2026) The staggered-adoption entry follows the same example through all four expansion waves.

Common pitfalls

WarningWhere this goes wrong

  • Asserting parallel trends from a good story. It’s untestable; show pre-trends / event-study leads near zero, and reason about confounders that could break parallelism. (Baker et al. 2026)

  • A control group on its own trajectory. If the control was already diverging (a differential trend), the second difference doesn’t cancel it and DiD is biased. (Baker et al. 2026)

  • Default TWFE under staggered adoption. Negative weights and forbidden comparisons can flip the sign; use Callaway–Sant’Anna / Sun–Abraham / dCDH and report a Goodman-Bacon decomposition. (Baker et al. 2026)

  • Dropping covariates into the TWFE regression. Time-invariant covariates fall out entirely, and time-varying ones mean controlling for changes, not levels. Covariates the policy itself moves are bad controls. Use regression-adjustment, IPW or doubly robust DiD instead. (Baker et al. 2026)

  • Reading weighted vs unweighted as a robustness check. Population weights change the question (the average county versus the average person), so the two estimates answer different questions. (Baker et al. 2026)

  • A shock that hits only one group. Parallel trends fails if something else changed for the treated group at the same time (a concurrent policy). (Cunningham 2021)

  • Anticipation. If units change behaviour before treatment (expecting it), pre-periods are contaminated and leads aren’t a clean test. (Baker et al. 2026)

  • Default (unclustered) standard errors on group-level policy. With serially correlated state–year shocks, they are far too small, and placebo laws come out “significant.” Cluster by the group the policy varies over, and remember that the number of clusters is your real sample size. With a handful of groups, use t_{g-k} critical values, a block bootstrap, or group averages. (Angrist and Pischke 2009; Cunningham 2021)

  • Choosing treated units because of their recent dip. The Ashenfelter dip and trajectory-based targeting build mean reversion into the “effect.” (Cunningham 2021)

Appendix · the mathematics

Why double-differencing identifies the effect. Write potential outcomes with additive group and time components: Y_{it}(0)=\gamma_i+\lambda_t+\varepsilon_{it}, and Y_{it}(1)=Y_{it}(0)+\delta. Parallel trends is the statement that \lambda_t is common across groups (the no-treatment trend is the same). Taking each group’s post−pre difference removes \gamma_i; subtracting the control’s difference removes the common \lambda_{\text{post}}-\lambda_{\text{pre}}, leaving exactly \delta. The assumption is precisely “the \lambda_t path is shared,” which is why differing levels \gamma_i are harmless but differing trends are fatal.

TWFE recovers the 2×2. In the saturated two-period, two-group case, the OLS coefficient on D_{it} in Y_{it}=\eta_i+\theta_t+\beta D_{it}+\epsilon_{it} equals \hat\delta_{DD} exactly — the unit dummies are the first difference, the time dummies the second.

Goodman-Bacon weights. With staggered timing, \hat\beta_{TWFE}=\sum_k s_k\,\hat\beta_k over all 2×2 sub-experiments k. The weights s_k are strictly positive, proportional to subsample size and the variance of D_{it} conditional on the fixed effects. (Baker et al. 2026) The negative weights live one level down, in the estimand. Take one group treated in period 1 and one treated in period 2. The 2×2 that uses the early group as a control for the late one estimates \text{ATT}(2,2)-[\text{ATT}(1,2)-\text{ATT}(1,1)], so \beta_{TWFE}=\text{ATT}(2,2)+w_1\,\text{ATT}(1,1)-w_1\,\text{ATT}(1,2). \text{ATT}(1,2) enters negatively unless effects are static (\text{ATT}(1,1)=\text{ATT}(1,2)). That is how a positive-everywhere effect can aggregate to a negative \hat\beta_{TWFE}. (Baker et al. 2026) The new estimators use only clean (never- or not-yet-treated) controls, so every building block gets a non-negative weight.

Regression adjustment and doubly robust DiD. With conditional parallel trends, the regression-adjustment estimator fits \mu_0(X)=\mathbb E[\Delta Y\mid X,D=0] on the untreated units and averages \Delta Y_i-\hat\mu_0(X_i) over the treated. The IPW estimator instead reweights untreated changes by \hat p(X)/(1-\hat p(X)). The doubly robust estimator applies the IPW weights to the residuals \Delta Y_i-\hat\mu_0(X_i): \widehat{\text{ATT}}_{dr}=\frac1n\sum_i\big(\hat w_{1}(D_i)-\hat w_{0}(D_i,X_i)\big)\big(\Delta Y_i-\hat\mu_0(X_i)\big), which is consistent if either the outcome model or the propensity model is right. (Baker et al. 2026)

Why group shocks make the 2×2 inconsistent. Following Angrist and Pischke, write the individual outcome as y_{ist}=\gamma_s+\lambda_t+\delta d_{st}+v_{st}+\eta_{ist}, where v_{st} is a state–year shock with E[v_{st}]=0. The Card–Krueger estimator is unbiased, but as the number of individuals per cell grows with states and periods held fixed, \operatorname{plim}\hat\delta_{CK}=\delta+\big[(v_{NJ,\text{Nov}}-v_{NJ,\text{Feb}})-(v_{PA,\text{Nov}}-v_{PA,\text{Feb}})\big]. The bracket does not vanish, because averaging more people within a state–period cannot average away that state–period’s shock. (Angrist and Pischke 2009) Consistency requires the number of groups or periods to grow. The state fixed effect removes each state’s mean shock \bar v_s, but v_{st}-\bar v_s is still serially correlated. The cluster-robust variance \hat V=(X'X)^{-1}\big(\sum_g X_g'\hat e_g\hat e_g'X_g\big)(X'X)^{-1}, with clusters g = states, leaves the within-state covariance unrestricted and so absorbs that correlation. Its justification is asymptotic in g, which is why few clusters bias it downward. (Angrist and Pischke 2009)

::: {.lead-kernel}
When a policy hits one group and not another, you can't just compare the two — they differed to begin
with. And you can't just track the treated group over time — everything else moved too. Difference-in-
differences does **both subtractions at once**: the treated group's *change* minus the control group's
*change*. The first difference erases fixed group gaps; the second erases shocks common to all. What
survives is the treatment effect — *if* the groups would have moved in parallel anyway.
:::

## Why it matters

[RD](regression-discontinuity.qmd) needs a sharp cutoff; [IV](instrumental-variables.qmd) needs a
clever instrument. DiD needs only a **policy change**, a **comparison group**, and **data before and
after** — which is why it is the most widely used quasi-experimental design in economics and policy.
[@cunningham_mixtape] It exploits a different kind of as-good-as-random variation: the *timing* of a
treatment that some units get and others don't. And it is the design where the field has most recently
discovered it was doing things *wrong* — making this entry both a how-to and a cautionary tale.
[@did_guide]

## The canonical 2×2

The simplest DiD has two groups — **treated** ($D=1$) and **control** ($D=0$) — and two periods,
**before** and **after**. The estimator double-differences four means: [@did_guide]
$$
\hat\delta_{DD}=\big(\bar Y_{\text{treat,post}}-\bar Y_{\text{treat,pre}}\big)
-\big(\bar Y_{\text{ctrl,post}}-\bar Y_{\text{ctrl,pre}}\big).
$$
Read the two subtractions: [@did_guide]

- The **first difference** (post − pre, *within* each group) wipes out every **time-invariant** group
  trait — the treated group can be richer, sicker, bigger; if it doesn't change, it cancels.
- The **second difference** (treated change − control change) wipes out any **shock common to both
  groups** in that period — a recession, a seasonal swing, a nationwide trend.

Two confounding layers removed by arithmetic, no controls required. What remains is attributed to the
treatment.

::: {.callout-note title="Card & Krueger and the minimum wage"}
The study that made DiD famous — and overturned a textbook prediction. In 1992 **New Jersey** raised
its minimum wage from \$4.25 to \$5.05; neighbouring **Pennsylvania** held at \$4.25. Card & Krueger
surveyed ~400 fast-food restaurants before and after, measuring full-time-equivalent employment:
[@cunningham_mixtape]

| | Pre | Post | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| **New Jersey** (treated) | 20.44 | 21.03 | **+0.59** |
| **Pennsylvania** (control) | 23.33 | 21.17 | **−2.16** |

DiD $= (+0.59)-(-2.16) = \mathbf{+2.76}$. Employment fell in Pennsylvania (a common regional shock) but
*rose* relative to it in New Jersey — the opposite of the competitive prediction that a higher minimum
wage destroys jobs. Note the logic: NJ's raw $+0.59$ alone would be misleading; only by netting out
PA's $-2.16$ regional slump does the policy's *relative* effect appear. [@cunningham_mixtape]
:::

## The one assumption: parallel trends

DiD's entire credibility rests on **parallel trends**: *absent the treatment, the treated and control
groups would have moved in parallel.* [@did_guide] The control group's change is used as the treated
group's **missing counterfactual change** — valid only if their paths would have stayed parallel. Two
things to internalise: [@did_guide]

- **It is fundamentally untestable.** It concerns the treated group's *unobserved* no-treatment path
  after the intervention — a counterfactual we never see.
- **Pre-trends are the evidence.** If the groups tracked each other *before* treatment, it's more
  plausible they'd have continued. So researchers plot the pre-period and check for parallel paths —
  necessary support, not proof. (Levels may differ wildly; it is the *slopes* that must match.)

## TWFE and the event study

In practice DiD is run as a **two-way fixed effects (TWFE)** regression, which generalises the four
means to many units and periods: [@did_guide]
$$
Y_{it}=\eta_i+\theta_t+\beta D_{it}+\epsilon_{it}.
$$
The **unit fixed effects** $\eta_i$ absorb all time-invariant differences between units (the first
difference); the **time fixed effects** $\theta_t$ absorb shocks common to all units in a period (the
second difference); $\beta$ is the DiD effect. [@did_guide] It is the
[panel/fixed-effects](panel-and-fixed-effects.qmd) machinery of the next entry — **DiD is a
fixed-effects design.**

The **event study** enriches this by replacing the single $D_{it}$ with dummies for periods *relative
to treatment*: [@did_guide]
$$
Y_{it}=\eta_i+\theta_t+\sum_{\tau=-q}^{m}\delta_\tau D_{i,\tau}+\epsilon_{it}.
$$
The **leads** ($\tau<0$) should be near zero — a visual **parallel-trends check**; the **lags**
($\tau\ge0$) trace how the effect **grows, fades, or plateaus** after treatment. [@did_guide] An event
study is the single most persuasive DiD graphic: flat before, a clear break after.

## From four numbers to a state–year panel

Angrist and Pischke teach DiD through two studies that show how the design grows from a 2×2 into a
full regression. [@angrist_mastering]

**Mississippi, 1930–31.** After the Caldwell banking collapse, Mississippi's two Federal Reserve
districts responded in opposite ways. Within four weeks the Atlanta Fed (Sixth District) had increased
lending to troubled banks by about 40%. Over the same period, St. Louis (Eighth District) lending fell
almost 10%. A district border running through the state turned this into a natural experiment.
[@angrist_mastering]

| Banks open, 1 July | 1930 | 1931 | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sixth District (easy money) | 135 | 121 | −14 |
| Eighth District (tight money) | 165 | 132 | −33 |

In 1931 the Sixth District had *fewer* banks open, which on its face looks like easy money backfiring.
The comparison in changes reverses that reading: $(-14)-(-33)=\mathbf{19}$ banks saved, more than 10%
of those operating in the Sixth District in 1930. Fitting the same comparison as a regression on
twelve district–year points gives about **21 banks**, with a standard error of about 11. With only two
districts, though, there are only two clusters, which Angrist and Pischke flag as an important caution
(see [Inference](#inference-why-your-did-standard-errors-are-too-small) below). [@angrist_mastering]

**The minimum legal drinking age, 51 states × 14 years.** The MLDA follow-up shows what a real panel
adds. [@angrist_mastering]

- **Continuous treatment intensity.** States differed in how much they liberalised drinking. So instead
  of a single treated/post dummy, the regressor is $\text{LEGAL}_{st}$, the share of 18–20-year-olds
  allowed to drink in state $s$ and year $t$. It is scaled for mid-year changes: Alabama's age-19 law
  took effect in July 1975, so it counts for half that year. The regression then has state effects,
  year effects and $\text{LEGAL}_{st}$, estimated on 714 observations.
- **The result.** Legal access caused about **11 extra deaths per 100,000** 18–20-year-olds, seven or
  eight of them from motor-vehicle accidents (standard error about 2.5). There is little effect on
  deaths from internal causes, the placebo outcome. This agrees with the
  [RD estimates](regression-discontinuity.qmd) from the same book.
- **State-specific trends.** With many periods you can relax common trends by giving each state its own
  linear trend, $\theta_k\cdot t$. Effects are then identified from *sharp deviations from each state's
  own smooth path*. The book uses two invented states to show why this matters. Allatsea lowers its
  MLDA in 1975 and neighbouring Alabaster does not. If Allatsea's death rate was already rising faster,
  a plain DiD credits the MLDA with a gap that predates it. The trend model credits only the extra jump
  in 1975. Adding trends barely moved the MLDA estimates, which supports a causal reading.
- **The limit of trend controls.** "If treatment effects emerge only gradually", a state trend can
  absorb the effect itself, and the trend model returns an imprecise, inconclusive answer. The same
  book's child-labour-law study shows the opposite danger. After state trends are added, its first
  stage and reduced form collapse, the 2SLS estimate jumps to an implausible 0.399 with a standard
  error almost as large, and the authors call it "a failed research design". [@angrist_mastering]
- **Time-varying controls.** States might raise beer taxes at the same time as they tighten the MLDA.
  Controlling for state beer taxes leaves the MLDA estimates essentially unchanged.
  [@angrist_mastering]

## The DiD revolution: when TWFE lies

Here is the cautionary tale, and why this entry cites a 2020s practitioner's guide. When units are
treated at **different times** (**staggered adoption**), the trusty TWFE regression can become
**biased — even sign-flipped.** [@did_guide]

- **Forbidden comparisons.** With staggering, TWFE implicitly uses **already-treated units as controls**
  for later-treated ones. If effects **change over time** (dynamic effects), those already-treated
  "controls" are themselves still moving *because of their own treatment*, contaminating the
  comparison. [@did_guide]
- **Goodman-Bacon decomposition.** TWFE's $\hat\beta$ is a **weighted average of all possible 2×2 DiDs**,
  with weights that are always **positive** (bigger for larger groups and for groups treated nearer the
  middle of the panel). The trouble is *which* 2×2s are in the average: many use already-treated units
  as controls. Translate those comparisons back into true effects and some cohort-period effects enter
  with **negative weight**. The estimate can come out the **wrong sign** *even if every unit's true
  effect is positive*. [@did_guide]
- **New estimators.** "Forward-engineered" methods fix it by using only **clean controls**:
  **Callaway–Sant'Anna** (compare each treated cohort only to **never-treated or not-yet-treated**
  units), **Sun–Abraham** (a saturated cohort-by-time interaction), and
  **de Chaisemartin–D'Haultfœuille** (robust when treatment switches on and off). [@did_guide]

The lesson is humbling and current: a method everyone trusted for decades was quietly mis-estimating
staggered rollouts, and the corpus's most recent source exists to set it right.

## Inference: why your DiD standard errors are too small

Getting the *point estimate* right is half the job. The other half is being honest about how sure you are,
and here DiD has a trap that sat unnoticed in applied work for years.

**The problem is that the policy varies by group, not by person.** A minimum wage changes by *state* and
*year*, but the data are thousands of individual workers or restaurants. Angrist and Pischke split the
error into two parts: a **state–year shock** shared by everyone in that state that year (a regional
business cycle), and an individual component. [@angrist_mostly_harmless] Those shared shocks are
bad news in two ways.

- **With only two groups, more data does not help.** In the Card–Krueger 2×2, the DiD estimate converges
  not to the true effect but to the effect *plus* the difference-in-differences of the regional shocks.
  Surveying more restaurants in New Jersey and Pennsylvania cannot tell a minimum-wage effect apart from
  "New Jersey's economy held steady while Pennsylvania's dipped." [@angrist_mostly_harmless] The only
  fix is **more groups or more periods**. For example, Card (1992) used all 51 states, so that the
  shocks could average out. [@angrist_mostly_harmless]
- **The shocks are serially correlated.** If Pennsylvania is doing badly this month, it will probably be
  doing badly next month too. Bertrand, Duflo and Mullainathan (2004) showed that with many years of
  group-level data and serially correlated outcomes, conventional standard errors **badly understate**
  the estimator's true variability. Placebo "policies" then come out "significant" far too often.
  [@cunningham_mixtape; @angrist_mostly_harmless]

The standard remedies are: [@cunningham_mixtape]

1. **Cluster at the group level.** Angrist and Pischke call this *passing the clustering buck one level
   higher*: cluster by **state**, not state×year. Clustering allows any correlation among a state's
   residuals, including correlation across its years, so it absorbs the serial correlation that state
   fixed effects leave behind. [@angrist_mostly_harmless] This is by far the most common choice.
2. **Block bootstrap.** Resample whole states, with all their years, rather than individual
   observations (see [the bootstrap](../02-inference/the-bootstrap.qmd)).
3. **Collapse the time dimension.** Average into one pre-period and one post-period so there is no serial
   correlation left to mishandle. With staggered adoption, regress the outcome on state and year effects
   (plus covariates), then compare the treated states' residuals before and after treatment.

**The catch: clustering needs many clusters.** Clustering fixes the correlation problem by treating the
*group* as the unit of information, so the effective sample size becomes the number of groups. In
Card–Krueger that number is **two**. [@cunningham_mixtape] Angrist and Pischke, borrowing Douglas
Adams's "answer to everything," frame the question as *are 42 clusters enough?* With few clusters, the
clustered formula itself **underestimates** the correlation it is meant to correct.
[@angrist_mostly_harmless] Their small-cluster options:

- judge significance against a **$t$ distribution with $g-k$ degrees of freedom** rather than the normal
  ($g$ = clusters, $k$ = regressors). This widens intervals when $g$ is small;
- use bias-reduced (HC2-style) cluster corrections, though these break down in the standard DiD setup
  with a full set of state dummies;
- use a **block bootstrap**, which simulations show tends to beat plain clustering with few groups;
- above all, show that the conclusions survive an **analysis of group averages**, a conservative and
  transparent check.

Their rough guidance is that 51 US states are reasonably safe for ordinary clustering, while
Canada's ten provinces are well below 42, though one study still finds clustering does reasonably well
even there. [@angrist_mostly_harmless] The general lesson is humbling: if your regressor varies only
across a few states or years, your real sample size is the number of states or years, however many
individual records you hold. [@angrist_mostly_harmless] This is the DiD form of the
[Moulton problem](../03-regression/regression-diagnostics.qmd).

## Threats beyond the pre-trend plot

Cunningham lists four threats to a DiD design: non-parallel trends, **compositional change** (the people
in the groups change over time), the trade-off between **long-run effects and reliability** (the further
out you look, the more else has changed), and **dependence on functional form** (parallel in levels need
not mean parallel in logs). [@cunningham_mixtape] The first is the most dangerous, and it often comes
from **who chose to be treated**:

- **The Ashenfelter dip.** People who enter job-training programmes typically see their earnings **dip
  just before enrolling**, which is often why they enrol. Earnings tend to revert to the mean, so they
  would have recovered anyway. A DiD of participants against non-participants credits that rebound to
  the programme and is **biased upward**. [@cunningham_mixtape]
- **Targeted rollouts.** An NGO that picks the villages that look most promising, or the most
  distressed, has chosen treated units by their trajectory. That is selection bias in the trends, and
  it breaks parallel trends by construction. [@cunningham_mixtape]

The defences are all forms of **placebo analysis**. Check that the event-study leads are near zero.
Use a second control group; carried through, this becomes the triple difference
in [combining & triangulating designs](combining-and-triangulating-designs.qmd). Or run the same model
on an **outcome that should not respond**. Cunningham's example: if Craigslist's erotic-services section
affected only female sex workers, the same model should show no effect on manslaughters or male
murders. An "effect" there would point to a general violence trend, not the policy.
[@cunningham_mixtape]

## Covariates and weights: where a simple DiD quietly changes its question

Two routine DiD choices are where practice drifts furthest from what the estimate means: whether to
weight, and how to add control variables. The Practitioner's Guide works through both with one running
example, the 2014 **Affordable Care Act Medicaid expansion** and county mortality among adults aged
20–64 (deaths per 100,000). [@did_guide]

**Weights pick the estimand.** The comparison is 2014 expansion counties against counties in states
that had not expanded by 2019, from 2013 to 2014: [@did_guide]

| | Expansion: 2013 → 2014 | No expansion: 2013 → 2014 | DiD |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Unweighted** (average county) | 419.2 → 428.5 (+9.3) | 474.0 → 483.1 (+9.1) | **+0.1** |
| **Population-weighted** (average adult) | 322.7 → 326.5 (+3.7) | 376.4 → 382.7 (+6.3) | **−2.6** |

These two rows answer different questions; they are not two versions of one estimate. The unweighted
DiD is about the average expansion *county*. The weighted DiD is about the average *adult* living in
one, which gives California much more say: 27.7% of the adults in the 2014-expansion states but only
5.4% of the counties. The guide is blunt that comparing the two "does not show whether weighting
matters", because they "reflect different target parameters". Decide which question you are asking
*before* you look at the numbers. [@did_guide] Angrist and Pischke take a softer line on their MLDA
panel. Weighting can buy precision when small states' death rates are noisy, but "you should hope …
that regression estimates from your state-year panel are not highly sensitive to weighting".
[@angrist_mastering] The two views fit together. If the effect is the same everywhere, weighting
changes only precision, and stable estimates are reassuring. If the effect varies, weighting changes
the question, and a gap between the two is a finding about heterogeneity to report, not noise.

**Covariates make the assumption conditional.** At baseline, expansion and non-expansion counties
differ a lot in share White, poverty and median income. Each of these has a **normalized difference**
(the gap in means divided by a pooled standard deviation) well above the usual 0.25 warning line.
[@did_guide] If those traits also predict mortality *trends*, unconditional parallel trends is
doubtful. The natural fallback is **conditional parallel trends**: among counties with the same
covariates, treated and untreated would have followed the same trend. This also needs **overlap**:
every treated county must have comparable untreated counties. [@did_guide]

**Why you can't just add $X$ to the TWFE regression.** It looks like the obvious move. The guide
explains four ways it goes wrong: [@did_guide]

1. **Fixed traits drop out.** The county fixed effect absorbs a county's 2013 poverty rate. That rate
   can only adjust for trends if you interact it with the post-period dummy.
2. **Changes are not levels.** A time-varying $X_{it}$ means you are controlling for *changes*. In the
   guide's words, "areas that are poor are not the same as areas that are becoming poor".
3. **Bad controls.** Suppose the expansion itself lowered poverty. Then controlling for 2014 poverty
   (or its change) partly controls away the effect. This is the
   [bad-control problem](../03-regression/regression-and-causality.qmd) in DiD form.
4. **Wrong weights even when specified right.** Caetano and Callaway show that the TWFE coefficient
   averages the covariate-specific effects with weights that need not be convex. It can come out
   negative even when the effect is positive at every covariate value. The coefficient is only clean
   if the effect is the same in every stratum, and research on Medicaid effects by income says it is
   not.

The data show how fragile this is. Adding covariates to TWFE moves the unweighted estimate from 0.12
to −2.35 or to −0.49. The only difference between those two is whether the covariates enter as 2013
levels or as yearly values. [@did_guide]

**The forward-engineered fix.** Start from the target, the $\text{ATT}$ under conditional parallel
trends, and build an estimator for it directly. The guide offers three, all close relatives of
[matching and propensity scores](matching-and-propensity-scores.qmd): [@did_guide]

- **Regression adjustment (RA).** Model how untreated counties' mortality *changed* as a function of
  their baseline covariates. Predict that change for each treated county, then subtract the prediction
  from what the county actually did. In the weighted Medicaid data:
  - the model predicts an untreated change of **+7.2** for the average expansion county (the raw
    untreated change was +6.3);
  - expansion counties actually changed by +3.7;
  - so the estimate is $3.7-7.2=\mathbf{-3.5}$.
- **Inverse probability weighting (IPW).** Model each county's probability of expanding from its
  covariates. Reweight the untreated counties to look like the treated ones, then compare changes.
  Standard software trims untreated units with scores above 0.995 so the weights stay sane.
- **Doubly robust (DR).** Combine the two. The estimate is consistent if *either* the outcome model or
  the propensity model is right. The guide recommends DR because it adds "additional 'protection'".

| Medicaid ATT(2014), 2013 covariates | RA | IPW | DR |
|---|---|---|---|
| Unweighted | −1.62 (4.68) | −0.86 (4.61) | −1.23 (4.94) |
| Population-weighted | −3.46 (2.39) | −3.84 (3.39) | −3.76 (3.24) |

The weighted estimates agree closely across the three methods. Every interval is wide enough to
include zero. The honest reading is "probably a small reduction, imprecisely measured". [@did_guide]
The [staggered-adoption entry](staggered-did.qmd) follows the same example through all four expansion
waves.

## Common pitfalls

::: {.callout-warning title="Where this goes wrong"}

- **Asserting parallel trends from a good story.** It's untestable; show pre-trends / event-study leads
  near zero, and reason about confounders that could break parallelism. [@did_guide]

- **A control group on its own trajectory.** If the control was already diverging (a differential
  trend), the second difference doesn't cancel it and DiD is biased. [@did_guide]

- **Default TWFE under staggered adoption.** Negative weights and forbidden comparisons can flip the
  sign; use Callaway–Sant'Anna / Sun–Abraham / dCDH and report a Goodman-Bacon decomposition.
  [@did_guide]

- **Dropping covariates into the TWFE regression.** Time-invariant covariates fall out entirely, and
  time-varying ones mean controlling for *changes*, not levels. Covariates the policy itself moves are
  bad controls. Use regression-adjustment, IPW or doubly robust DiD instead. [@did_guide]

- **Reading weighted vs unweighted as a robustness check.** Population weights change the *question*
  (the average county versus the average person), so the two estimates answer different questions.
  [@did_guide]

- **A shock that hits only one group.** Parallel trends fails if something *else* changed for the
  treated group at the same time (a concurrent policy). [@cunningham_mixtape]

- **Anticipation.** If units change behaviour *before* treatment (expecting it), pre-periods are
  contaminated and leads aren't a clean test. [@did_guide]

- **Default (unclustered) standard errors on group-level policy.** With serially correlated state–year
  shocks, they are far too small, and placebo laws come out "significant." Cluster by the group the
  policy varies over, and remember that *the number of clusters is your real sample size*. With a
  handful of groups, use $t_{g-k}$ critical values, a block bootstrap, or group averages.
  [@angrist_mostly_harmless; @cunningham_mixtape]

- **Choosing treated units because of their recent dip.** The Ashenfelter dip and trajectory-based
  targeting build mean reversion into the "effect." [@cunningham_mixtape]

:::

## Appendix · the mathematics {.unnumbered}

::: {.math-appendix}

**Why double-differencing identifies the effect.** Write potential outcomes with additive group and
time components: $Y_{it}(0)=\gamma_i+\lambda_t+\varepsilon_{it}$, and $Y_{it}(1)=Y_{it}(0)+\delta$.
**Parallel trends** is the statement that $\lambda_t$ is *common* across groups (the no-treatment trend
is the same). Taking each group's post−pre difference removes $\gamma_i$; subtracting the control's
difference removes the common $\lambda_{\text{post}}-\lambda_{\text{pre}}$, leaving exactly $\delta$.
The assumption is precisely "the $\lambda_t$ path is shared," which is why differing *levels*
$\gamma_i$ are harmless but differing *trends* are fatal.

**TWFE recovers the 2×2.** In the saturated two-period, two-group case, the OLS coefficient on
$D_{it}$ in $Y_{it}=\eta_i+\theta_t+\beta D_{it}+\epsilon_{it}$ equals $\hat\delta_{DD}$ exactly — the
unit dummies are the first difference, the time dummies the second.

**Goodman-Bacon weights.** With staggered timing, $\hat\beta_{TWFE}=\sum_k s_k\,\hat\beta_k$ over all
2×2 sub-experiments $k$. The weights $s_k$ are **strictly positive**, proportional to subsample size
**and the variance of $D_{it}$ conditional on the fixed effects**. [@did_guide] The negative weights
live one level down, in the *estimand*. Take one group treated in period 1 and one treated in period 2.
The 2×2 that uses the early group as a control for the late one estimates
$\text{ATT}(2,2)-[\text{ATT}(1,2)-\text{ATT}(1,1)]$, so
$$
\beta_{TWFE}=\text{ATT}(2,2)+w_1\,\text{ATT}(1,1)-w_1\,\text{ATT}(1,2).
$$
$\text{ATT}(1,2)$ enters negatively unless effects are static ($\text{ATT}(1,1)=\text{ATT}(1,2)$). That
is how a positive-everywhere effect can aggregate to a negative $\hat\beta_{TWFE}$. [@did_guide] The new
estimators use only clean (never- or not-yet-treated) controls, so every building block gets a
non-negative weight.

**Regression adjustment and doubly robust DiD.** With conditional parallel trends, the
regression-adjustment estimator fits $\mu_0(X)=\mathbb E[\Delta Y\mid X,D=0]$ on the untreated units and
averages $\Delta Y_i-\hat\mu_0(X_i)$ over the treated. The IPW estimator instead reweights untreated
changes by $\hat p(X)/(1-\hat p(X))$. The doubly robust estimator applies the IPW weights to the
*residuals* $\Delta Y_i-\hat\mu_0(X_i)$:
$$
\widehat{\text{ATT}}_{dr}=\frac1n\sum_i\big(\hat w_{1}(D_i)-\hat w_{0}(D_i,X_i)\big)\big(\Delta Y_i-\hat\mu_0(X_i)\big),
$$
which is consistent if *either* the outcome model or the propensity model is right. [@did_guide]

**Why group shocks make the 2×2 inconsistent.** Following Angrist and Pischke, write the individual
outcome as $y_{ist}=\gamma_s+\lambda_t+\delta d_{st}+v_{st}+\eta_{ist}$, where $v_{st}$ is a state–year
shock with $E[v_{st}]=0$. The Card–Krueger estimator is unbiased, but as the number of individuals per
cell grows with states and periods held fixed,
$$
\operatorname{plim}\hat\delta_{CK}=\delta+\big[(v_{NJ,\text{Nov}}-v_{NJ,\text{Feb}})-(v_{PA,\text{Nov}}-v_{PA,\text{Feb}})\big].
$$
The bracket does not vanish, because averaging more people within a state–period cannot average away that
state–period's shock. [@angrist_mostly_harmless] Consistency requires the number of **groups or periods**
to grow. The state fixed effect removes each state's mean shock $\bar v_s$, but $v_{st}-\bar v_s$ is
still serially correlated. The cluster-robust variance
$\hat V=(X'X)^{-1}\big(\sum_g X_g'\hat e_g\hat e_g'X_g\big)(X'X)^{-1}$, with clusters $g$ = states,
leaves the within-state covariance unrestricted and so absorbs that correlation. Its justification is
asymptotic in $g$, which is why few clusters bias it downward. [@angrist_mostly_harmless]

:::
