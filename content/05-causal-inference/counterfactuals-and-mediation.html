Counterfactuals, Attribution & Mediation

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source

The top rung of the ladder: not what happens on average if we act, but what would have happened to this person had things been different — and through which channel a cause does its work. Blame, regret, legal liability, and mechanism all live here, and none of them can be written with the do-operator alone.

An experiment tells you what a treatment does to a population; a counterfactual asks what it did — or would have done — to this unit, given everything you already saw happen. Answering that means using the evidence to infer who the unit is, changing one thing about the world, and replaying the model. The same idea defines responsibility (was the drug necessary for this death?) and mechanism (how much of the effect flows through the mediator?). And it shows why the popular recipes — impute from a regression, “control for the mediator” — answer a different question from the one being asked.

Why it matters

Everything on rung two of Pearl’s ladder of causation is about averages under intervention: \mathbb E[Y\mid do(X=x)], the effect of a policy on a population. That is the right target for a lot of decisions, but many of the questions researchers, courts, and people actually ask are of a different kind: (Pearl and Mackenzie 2018)

  • “I took Sepulveda and it took an hour. I should have taken the freeway.” That is a claim about the same evening, under the same conditions, after seeing how it turned out. (Pearl et al. 2016)
  • “Do I owe my remission to the irradiation?” This asks about one patient’s history, not the trial average. (Pearl et al. 2016)
  • “Would the employer have taken the same action had the employee been of a different race … and everything else had been the same?” That is how U.S. courts define employment discrimination (Carson v. Bethlehem Steel, 1996). It is a counterfactual about an individual case. (Pearl et al. 2016; Pearl and Mackenzie 2018)
  • “Would gender-blind hiring close the pay gap, or do we need to equalise training first?” This is a question about which pathway carries the effect: a mediation question. (Pearl et al. 2016)

Pearl frames these as the third rung. They need more than data plus a do-operator. They need a model of how each variable gets its value, because the answer depends on reasoning about a world that contradicts what we observed. (Pearl and Mackenzie 2018) Pearl and Mackenzie call responsibility and blame, regret and credit “the currency of a causal mind.” (Pearl and Mackenzie 2018) This entry builds on the DAG and potential-outcomes entries. It shows how counterfactuals are computed, what they can and can’t be estimated from, and how they give mediation analysis a precise meaning.

The clash of worlds

Try to write the freeway regret with the do-operator: \mathbb E[\text{time}\mid do(\text{freeway}),\ \text{time}=1\text{ hour}]. It is self-contradictory. The one hour was observed on Sepulveda, but the expression puts it in the freeway world. (Pearl et al. 2016) The do-operator can tell P(Y\mid do(\text{freeway})) apart from P(Y\mid do(\text{Sepulveda})). It cannot tell apart the variables “my actual time” and “my would-be freeway time,” and the regret claim needs to use the first to inform the second. (Pearl et al. 2016)

The fix is the subscript notation you already know from potential outcomes: Y_{x} is the value Y would have taken had X been x. The regret question becomes \mathbb E[Y_{X=1}\mid X=0,\ Y=1\text{ hour}]. That expression mixes one hypothetical variable with two observed ones from a different world. (Pearl et al. 2016) No randomized experiment can estimate it, because nobody can take both roads on the same night. A trial gives you \mathbb E[Y_1] and \mathbb E[Y_0] for the population, never the freeway time for this driver on the night Sepulveda was jammed. (Pearl et al. 2016)

Two traditions reach the same notation from opposite directions. In Rubin’s (and before him Neyman’s 1923) potential-outcomes framework, Y_x(u) is a primitive: each unit simply has a potential outcome for each treatment. (Pearl and Mackenzie 2018) Pearl derives it from a structural causal model (SCM). Replace the equation for X with the constant X=x (the same graph surgery as do(x)), then solve for Y with unit u’s background factors held fixed:

Y_x(u) \;=\; Y_{M_x}(u).

The Primer calls this the fundamental law of counterfactuals: every structural model assigns a definite value to every counterfactual you can pose about it. (Pearl et al. 2016) It comes with a consistency rule. If X actually was x, then Y_x is just the observed Y (Y = XY_1+(1-X)Y_0 for binary X), because setting a variable to the value it already has changes nothing. (Pearl et al. 2016) The difference between the two rungs is a difference of level. do(x) describes how a population behaves under intervention. Y_x(u) describes a specific individual, and that is what lets it express credit, blame, and regret. (Pearl et al. 2016)

Computing a counterfactual: abduction, action, prediction

With a fully specified model, any unit-level counterfactual takes three steps: (Pearl et al. 2016)

  1. Abduction — use the evidence to work out the unit’s background factors U (explain the past).
  2. Action — replace the equation for X with X=x (bend history, minimally).
  3. Prediction — solve the modified model with those same U’s (replay the future).

The Primer’s student. Encouragement X raises homework H, and both raise the exam score Y (all standardised), with H = 0.5X + U_H and Y = 0.7X + 0.4H + U_Y. Joe is observed at X=0.5, H=1, Y=1.5. What would Joe have scored had he doubled his homework? Abduction recovers Joe’s idiosyncrasies, U_H = 0.75 and U_Y = 0.75. Action sets H=2. Prediction gives Y_{H=2} = 0.7(0.5)+0.4(2)+0.75 = 1.90, up from 1.5. (Pearl et al. 2016) The U’s are what make it Joe’s counterfactual rather than an average student’s. They carry “everything that is unique” about him into the other world.

The Book of Why’s Alice. Here the stakes are higher, because the obvious data-driven answers are wrong. (Pearl and Mackenzie 2018) Salary S depends on experience EX and education ED, and education takes time away from experience. The model is S = 65{,}000 + 2{,}500\,EX + 5{,}000\,ED + U_S and EX = 10 - 4\,ED + U_{EX}. Alice has a high-school education and 6 years’ experience. What would she earn with a college degree?

  • Regression imputation plugs ED=1, EX=6 into the fitted salary equation and says $85,000. It holds her experience fixed, as if the extra years in college cost her nothing. (Pearl and Mackenzie 2018)
  • The three steps. Abduction gives U_S = \$1{,}000 and U_{EX}=-4. Action sets ED=1. Prediction first updates her experience, EX_{ED=1} = 10-4-4 = 2 years, and then her salary, S_{ED=1} = 65{,}000 + 2{,}500(2) + 5{,}000 + 1{,}000 = $76,000. (Pearl and Mackenzie 2018)

The $9,000 gap comes from a classic error: experience is a mediator, not a confounder. Matching Alice to a college graduate with the same experience compares her to someone who would have had more experience had they stopped at high school. Equal experience, often treated as the ideal match, is actually evidence that the two people differ in the way that matters. (Pearl and Mackenzie 2018) Pearl calls “mistaking a mediator for a confounder … one of the deadliest sins in causal inference”: the second invites adjustment and the first forbids it. (Pearl and Mackenzie 2018) There is no free lunch here. The model supplied the answer, and with only a partially specified model you get bounds rather than a number, just as in partial identification. (Pearl and Mackenzie 2018)

Effects on the treated, and interventions do() can’t write

Counterfactuals are also the natural language for several policy quantities.

Effect of treatment on the treated (ETT). A job-training pilot works in a randomized trial. Once it is opened to volunteers, hiring among graduates is even higher. Critics object that self-enrollees are more resourceful and might have found jobs anyway. The question they are asking is \text{ETT}=\mathbb E[Y_1 - Y_0\mid X=1], the ATT, and its \mathbb E[Y_0\mid X=1] term is another clash of worlds. (Pearl et al. 2016) It is still often estimable. If covariates Z satisfy the backdoor criterion, the ETT uses the ordinary adjustment formula with one change: average over P(Z=z\mid X=1), the treated’s covariate mix, rather than P(Z=z). (Pearl et al. 2016) ETT is also identified for binary X when experimental and observational data are both available, and whenever a front-door mediator exists. (Pearl et al. 2016)

Additive interventions. “Add 5 mg/l of insulin to every patient’s current dose” does not set everyone to the same level, so it is not a do(x). Its effect is \sum_{x'}\mathbb E[Y_{x'+q}\mid X=x']P(x'), an ETT-type quantity. (Pearl et al. 2016) It does not equal the average of dose-response effects over the population, because people who chose dose x' may respond differently from people assigned it. The two coincide only without confounding. (Pearl et al. 2016) Scientific findings are reported as do-expressions, which carry over between populations. A policy question like “add q to everyone” depends on who chose what in this population, and counterfactual reasoning is how you translate between the two. (Pearl et al. 2016)

Policies targeted on a downstream variable. “Send students with little homework (H\le H_0) to the program” cannot be written as setting X, because X itself causes H. The target is \mathbb E[Y_{X=1}\mid H\le H_0], a counterfactual restricted to a subgroup defined by a post-treatment variable. (Pearl et al. 2016)

Attribution: necessity, sufficiency, and “but for”

The law’s test of causation is but-for: the harm would not have occurred but for the defendant’s act. (Pearl and Mackenzie 2018) Written as a counterfactual, that is the probability of necessity:

PN = P(Y_{0}=0 \mid X=1,\ Y=1),

the probability the outcome would not have happened without the act, given that the act and the outcome both happened. (Pearl et al. 2016) A civil court finds for the plaintiff when this is “more probable than not,” so PN > 1/2. (Pearl et al. 2016; Pearl and Mackenzie 2018) Its mirror image is the probability of sufficiency, PS = P(Y_1 = 1\mid X=0,\ Y=0): would the act not taken have produced the outcome? And PNS = P(Y_1=1,\ Y_0=0) is the probability that the act is both necessary and sufficient. (Pearl et al. 2016)

Three patients (the Primer, using a real trial). Irradiation after lumpectomy cut breast-cancer recurrence from 39% to 14% (Fisher et al., 2002). (Pearl et al. 2016) Ms Jones had irradiation and is in remission. Asking whether she owes her life to it is asking for PN. Mrs Smith skipped it and relapsed. Her regret is justified in proportion to PS. Ms Daily is still deciding. She only wants irradiation if her tumour is the kind that would remit with it and recur without it, so she needs PNS. (Pearl et al. 2016) None of these is identified by the trial alone. But under monotonicity (irradiation never causes a recurrence that would otherwise have remitted), PNS reduces to the experimental risk difference, 0.39-0.14 = 0.25. Ms Daily has about a one-in-four chance of being a patient the treatment actually saves. (Pearl et al. 2016)

Why hindsight matters. PN cannot be written as a do-expression. Conditioning on “the outcome happened” changes our beliefs about the unit’s background factors, for example about how strong the fire was that killed the victim behind the blocked exit. Pearl and Mackenzie treat this as proof that rung three sits strictly above rung two. (Pearl and Mackenzie 2018)

Combining experiments and observation can settle a case. Suppose a drug trial shows a death rate of 1.6% under the drug and 1.4% without it, a “minor” effect. But among people who chose the drug themselves, 0.2% died, against 2.8% among those who chose not to. (Pearl et al. 2016) The observational excess risk ratio alone is -13, which looks strongly protective. The confounding correction, which is what the experiment contributes, is +14. Together they give PN = 1. Even without assuming monotonicity, the lower bound hits 1: barring sampling error, the drug caused the death. (Pearl et al. 2016) In general PN is only bounded, like any partially identified quantity, and the width of the bounds depends on a single observable ratio. (Pearl et al. 2016)

Necessity ≠ sufficiency. A fire needs both a struck match and oxygen, and each is equally necessary. We still blame the match. For the match both PN and PS are high, while for oxygen PN is high but PS is low, because oxygen is almost always present. (Pearl and Mackenzie 2018) Two riflemen in a firing squad give the opposite pattern. Each shot alone is sufficient, so for each rifleman PS=1 but PN=0: the prisoner would have died anyway. (Pearl and Mackenzie 2018)

Climate attribution is the best-known large-scale use. Climatologists’ fraction of attributable risk equals PN under no confounding and monotonicity (greenhouse gases never prevent a heat wave). (Pearl and Mackenzie 2018) Allen and Stott (2004) concluded it was “very likely that over half the risk” of a 2003-scale European heat wave was attributable to human influence. The heat wave was blamed for nearly 15,000 deaths in France. (Pearl and Mackenzie 2018) Hannart’s counterfactual analysis put PN at 0.9 but PS at only about 0.0072 for that particular summer. Climate change was very likely necessary for it, but nothing made that year predictable. Over a 200-year horizon, the probability that climate change is sufficient for another such heat wave rises to 80%. (Pearl and Mackenzie 2018) Pearl traces two conflicting published studies of the 2010 Russian heat wave to the same split: one argued from PN, the other from PS. (Pearl and Mackenzie 2018)

Mediation, done right

Mediation asks the second kind of why: not whether X affects Y, but how. The scurvy story shows why this matters. The British navy believed citrus worked through acidity, switched to West Indian limes (with only a quarter of the vitamin C), then “purified” the juice by cooking it. In Pearl’s words, they were “disabling the mediator”, and scurvy came back on polar expeditions. (Pearl and Mackenzie 2018) Getting the mechanism wrong can undo a cure.

The DAG entry warned that “regress Y on X and M” does not deliver a direct effect. Counterfactuals say what a direct effect is. With treatment T, mediator M, and outcome Y, there are four distinct quantities: (Pearl et al. 2016)

  • Total effect (TE) — switch T from 0 to 1 and let the mediator respond naturally.
  • Controlled direct effect, CDE(m) — switch T with the mediator forced to the same level m for everyone. This is a rung-two quantity: do(T,\,M=m).
  • Natural direct effect (NDE) — switch T while each unit’s mediator stays at whatever value it would have taken without treatment: \mathbb E[Y_{1,M_0} - Y_{0,M_0}]. In Pearl’s words, freeze the mediator where it would have been and “wiggle the treatment.” (Pearl and Mackenzie 2018)
  • Natural indirect effect (NIE) — hold treatment at 0 but move each unit’s mediator to the value it would take under treatment: \mathbb E[Y_{0,M_1} - Y_{0,M_0}]. This is the part of the effect the mediator can deliver on its own, with Y “blinded” to T. (Pearl et al. 2016)

The natural effects are nested counterfactuals. Y_{1,M_0} combines treatment from one world with a mediator value from another, which is why they cannot be written with do(\cdot). (Pearl and Mackenzie 2018) The distinction has practical bite. Gender-blind hiring would disable the direct path while leaving qualifications as they are, and that is an NDE-type intervention, not “set qualification to q for everyone.” (Pearl et al. 2016) It is also the legal definition: under Carson, discrimination is the direct effect. (Pearl and Mackenzie 2018)

The mediation formula

If nothing confounds the mediator–outcome relationship (and treatment is randomized or deconfounded), the natural effects reduce to ordinary conditional probabilities, whatever the functional form: (Pearl et al. 2016)

\text{NDE} = \sum_m \big[\mathbb E(Y\mid T{=}1,m) - \mathbb E(Y\mid T{=}0,m)\big]\,P(m\mid T{=}0), \qquad \text{NIE} = \sum_m \mathbb E(Y\mid T{=}0,m)\,\big[P(m\mid T{=}1) - P(m\mid T{=}0)\big].

The NDE is a CDE averaged over the untreated mediator distribution. The NIE is a product of two nonlinear effects and has no CDE interpretation. (Pearl et al. 2016; Pearl and Mackenzie 2018) Robins and Greenland had described the natural effects in words in 1992 but concluded they could not be estimated, partly because they combine information from two “cross-worlds” that no experiment can replicate. Pearl’s 2001 formula turned the idea into something estimable. Pearl and Mackenzie count almost no papers titled “mediation analysis” before 2004 and more than a hundred a year now. (Pearl and Mackenzie 2018)

Worked example (the Primer’s homework data, randomized with no mediator–outcome confounding). An enhanced training program (T) raises the share of students doing more than 3 hours of homework (M) from 40% to 75%. Exam pass rates are 0.80 / 0.40 for trained students with / without heavy homework and 0.30 / 0.20 for untrained students. (Pearl et al. 2016) The formula gives TE = 0.46, NDE = 0.32 and NIE = 0.035. So about 30% of the effect (1-\text{NDE}/\text{TE}) needs the homework channel, but only 7% (\text{NIE}/\text{TE}) could be produced by the homework boost alone. The rest comes from the curriculum and homework working together. (Pearl et al. 2016) Figure 1 computes these quantities both ways.

Why the classic recipe breaks

The Baron–Kenny approach (1986, one of the most-cited papers in all of science) runs two regressions, with and without the mediator, and reads the indirect effect off the difference in treatment coefficients or the product of path coefficients. (Pearl and Mackenzie 2018) Both shortcuts assume that effects along distinct paths add and coefficients along a path multiply. That is true in a linear model without interactions and false as soon as the mediator and treatment interact. (Pearl and Mackenzie 2018) Pearl’s threshold example: a job offer comes if salary exceeds 10, where salary = 7\cdot\text{education} + 3\cdot\text{skill}. The total effect of education is 1 (the offer arrives), but the NIE is 0, because the skill gained alone lifts salary only to 6. TE is no longer NDE + NIE. In general \text{TE} = \text{NDE} - \text{NIE}_{r}, where \text{NIE}_r is the indirect effect of the reverse transition. (Pearl and Mackenzie 2018; Pearl et al. 2016) Asked whether the difference or the product method is right, Pearl answers “Neither!” Both mistake the estimation procedure for the meaning. (Pearl and Mackenzie 2018)

A related trap is the mediation fallacy: conditioning on the mediator instead of holding it constant by intervention. It does no harm when nothing confounds M and Y. When something does, it can reverse the conclusion. That is the collider mechanism from the DAG entry. Kruskal’s counterexample to the Berkeley-admissions analysis, where state of residence confounds department choice and admission, shows it happening. (Pearl and Mackenzie 2018) Pearl and Mackenzie note that Fisher made the same mistake in 1936. (Pearl and Mackenzie 2018)

Mediation in the field

  • Algebra for All (Chicago, 1997). Guanglei Hong split the reform’s effect on test scores into a direct effect of about +2.7 points (statistically significant) and an indirect effect of about −2.3 points through a worse classroom environment: lower-achieving students, now in mixed-ability classes, could not keep up. The side effect almost cancelled the curriculum’s gain. The 2003 “Double-Dose Algebra” reform gave those students a second daily class closer to their old environment, repairing the channel the analysis had exposed. (Pearl and Mackenzie 2018)
  • The “smoking gene.” Tyler VanderWeele asked whether a nicotine-receptor variant raises lung-cancer risk by making people smoke more. The indirect effect was only 1–3%, about one extra cigarette a day. CDE(0) was roughly zero: a non-smoker with the gene is not at extra risk. The effect showed up in smokers’ bodies. “The interaction between the gene and the subject’s behavior is everything.” (Pearl and Mackenzie 2018)
  • Tourniquets. A study of wounded soldiers who reached hospital found no survival benefit from battlefield tourniquets. Selecting on arrival alive conditions on the mediator (pre-admission survival), so the study estimated a direct effect. Any life-saving indirect effect is invisible in data of that kind. (Pearl and Mackenzie 2018)

Common pitfalls

WarningWhere this goes wrong

  • Answering a rung-three question with rung-one tools. Imputing “what she would have earned” from a fitted regression, or matching on a post-treatment variable, gives a confident wrong number ($85,000 against $76,000 for Alice). Methods driven only by data cannot answer counterfactual questions. (Pearl and Mackenzie 2018)

  • Treating a mediator as a confounder. Adjusting for (or matching on) something the treatment causes blocks part of the effect and compares unlike units. (Pearl and Mackenzie 2018)

  • Reading an average effect as an individual one. A trial’s risk difference is not the probability that this patient was helped. That is PN or PNS, which need extra assumptions (monotonicity) or extra data (observational + experimental) and are generally only bounded. (Pearl et al. 2016)

  • Confusing “necessary” with “sufficient.” Attribution of a past event (PN) and prediction of a future one (PS) can differ enormously, as in PN 0.9 against PS 0.0072 for the 2003 heat wave. Conflicting “attribution” headlines often come from using different measures. (Pearl and Mackenzie 2018)

  • Baron–Kenny by default. The difference and product methods are correct only in linear models without interactions. With a treatment–mediator interaction the parts don’t sum to the total, and the two methods disagree. Define NDE/NIE first, then estimate them. (Pearl and Mackenzie 2018; Pearl et al. 2016)

  • Conditioning on the mediator instead of holding it fixed. This is the mediation fallacy. It is harmless only if nothing confounds M and Y, and that assumption is separate from, and not guaranteed by, the randomization of T. (Pearl and Mackenzie 2018; Gerber and Green 2012)

  • Selecting on a mediator. Studying only those who survived to hospital, stayed in the program, or got the job conditions on a post-treatment variable and estimates, at most, a direct effect. (Pearl and Mackenzie 2018)

Appendix · the mathematics

Counterfactuals from a structural model. Let M be an SCM with exogenous U and structural equations V_i = f_i(\text{pa}_i, U_i). For unit u, Y_x(u) is the solution for Y in M_x, the model with X’s equation replaced by X=x. With a distribution P(u), a counterfactual query given evidence e is answered by (i) P(u)\to P(u\mid e), (ii) forming M_x, (iii) computing \mathbb E[Y_x\mid e]=\sum_u Y_x(u)P(u\mid e). Consistency: X=x \Rightarrow Y_x = Y.

ETT under the backdoor criterion. If Z satisfies the backdoor criterion for X\to Y, then Y_x \perp X \mid Z, so P(Y_x=y\mid X=x') = \sum_z P(Y=y\mid X=x, Z=z)\,P(Z=z\mid X=x'), compared with P(y\mid do(x)) = \sum_z P(y\mid x,z)P(z). Hence \text{ETT}=\mathbb E[Y\mid X{=}1] - \sum_z \mathbb E[Y\mid X{=}0,z]P(z\mid X{=}1).

Probability of necessity. If Y is monotone in X (Y_1(u)\ge Y_0(u) for all u), then PN = \frac{P(y)-P(y\mid do(x'))}{P(x,y)} = \underbrace{\frac{P(y\mid x)-P(y\mid x')}{P(y\mid x)}}_{\text{ERR}} + \underbrace{\frac{P(y\mid x')-P(y\mid do(x'))}{P(x,y)}}_{\text{CF}} . Without monotonicity, \max\{0,\ \tfrac{P(y)-P(y\mid do(x'))}{P(x,y)}\}\le PN\le \min\{1,\ \tfrac{P(y'\mid do(x'))-P(x',y')}{P(x,y)}\}. The width of the interval is q = P(y'\mid x)/P(y\mid x), whatever the confounding. Under monotonicity, PNS = P(y\mid do(x)) - P(y\mid do(x')).

Natural effects. For t=f_T(u_T), m=f_M(t,u_M), y=f_Y(t,m,u_Y): \text{TE}=\mathbb E[Y_1-Y_0], \text{CDE}(m)=\mathbb E[Y_{1,m}-Y_{0,m}], \text{NDE}=\mathbb E[Y_{1,M_0}-Y_{0,M_0}], \text{NIE}=\mathbb E[Y_{0,M_1}-Y_{0,M_0}], and in general \text{TE}=\text{NDE}-\text{NIE}_r. Sufficient conditions for identification: a covariate set W with no descendants of T that blocks every backdoor path from M to Y (after removing T\to M and T\to Y), with the W-specific effects of T on M and of \{T,M\} on Y identifiable. When W also deconfounds those, \text{NDE} = \sum_{m,w}\big[\mathbb E(Y\mid 1,m,w)-\mathbb E(Y\mid 0,m,w)\big]P(m\mid 0,w)P(w).

Why linear models hide the problem. Take y=\beta_1 m+\beta_2 t+\beta_3 tm+u_y and m=\gamma_1 t+u_m (with \mathbb E[u_m]=0). Then \text{NDE}=\beta_2, \text{NIE}=\beta_1\gamma_1, but \text{TE}=\beta_2+\gamma_1(\beta_1+\beta_3). The portion that needs mediation, \text{TE}-\text{NDE}= \gamma_1(\beta_1+\beta_3), exceeds the portion mediation suffices for by \beta_3\gamma_1. Only when \beta_3=0 do we recover \text{TE}=\text{NDE}+\text{NIE} and the Baron–Kenny product \beta_1\gamma_1. (Specialisation of the Primer’s Study question 4.5.3.)

::: {.lead-kernel}
An experiment tells you what a treatment does to a *population*; a counterfactual asks what it did — or would
have done — to *this* unit, given everything you already saw happen. Answering that means using the evidence to
infer who the unit is, changing one thing about the world, and replaying the model. The same idea defines
responsibility (*was the drug necessary for this death?*) and mechanism (*how much of the effect flows through
the mediator?*). And it shows why the popular recipes — impute from a regression, "control for the mediator" —
answer a different question from the one being asked.

## Why it matters

Everything on rung two of Pearl's [ladder of causation](index.qmd) is about **averages under intervention**:
$\mathbb E[Y\mid do(X=x)]$, the effect of a policy on a population. That is the right target for a lot of
decisions, but many of the questions researchers, courts, and people actually ask are of a different kind:
[@pearl_why]

- *"I took Sepulveda and it took an hour. I should have taken the freeway."* That is a claim about **the same
  evening, under the same conditions**, after seeing how it turned out. [@pearl_primer]
- *"Do I owe my remission to the irradiation?"* This asks about **one patient's** history, not the trial average.
  [@pearl_primer]
- *"Would the employer have taken the same action had the employee been of a different race … and everything
  else had been the same?"* That is how U.S. courts define employment discrimination (*Carson v. Bethlehem
  Steel*, 1996). It is a counterfactual about an individual case. [@pearl_primer; @pearl_why]
- *"Would gender-blind hiring close the pay gap, or do we need to equalise training first?"* This is a question
  about **which pathway** carries the effect: a mediation question. [@pearl_primer]

Pearl frames these as the **third rung**. They need more than data plus a do-operator. They need a model of
*how each variable gets its value*, because the answer depends on reasoning about a world that
contradicts what we observed. [@pearl_why] Pearl and Mackenzie call responsibility and blame, regret and
credit "the currency of a causal mind." [@pearl_why] This entry builds on the
[DAG](directed-acyclic-graphs.qmd) and [potential-outcomes](potential-outcomes.qmd) entries. It shows how
counterfactuals are *computed*, what they can and can't be estimated from, and how they give mediation analysis
a precise meaning.

## The clash of worlds

Try to write the freeway regret with the do-operator: $\mathbb E[\text{time}\mid do(\text{freeway}),\
\text{time}=1\text{ hour}]$. It is self-contradictory. The one hour was observed on Sepulveda, but the
expression puts it in the freeway world. [@pearl_primer] The do-operator can tell $P(Y\mid do(\text{freeway}))$
apart from $P(Y\mid do(\text{Sepulveda}))$. It **cannot** tell apart the *variables* "my actual time" and "my
would-be freeway time," and the regret claim needs to use the first to inform the second. [@pearl_primer]

The fix is the subscript notation you already know from [potential outcomes](potential-outcomes.qmd): $Y_{x}$ is
the value $Y$ would have taken had $X$ been $x$. The regret question becomes
$\mathbb E[Y_{X=1}\mid X=0,\ Y=1\text{ hour}]$. That expression mixes one hypothetical variable with two
observed ones from a *different* world. [@pearl_primer] No randomized experiment can estimate it, because nobody
can take both roads on the same night. A trial gives you $\mathbb E[Y_1]$ and $\mathbb E[Y_0]$ for the
population, never the freeway time for *this* driver *on the night Sepulveda was jammed*. [@pearl_primer]

Two traditions reach the same notation from opposite directions. In Rubin's (and before him Neyman's 1923)
potential-outcomes framework, $Y_x(u)$ is a **primitive**: each unit simply *has* a potential outcome for each
treatment. [@pearl_why] Pearl **derives** it from a structural causal model (SCM). Replace the equation for $X$
with the constant $X=x$ (the same graph surgery as $do(x)$), then solve for $Y$ with unit $u$'s background
factors held fixed:

$$
Y_x(u) \;=\; Y_{M_x}(u).
$$

The Primer calls this the fundamental law of counterfactuals: every structural model assigns a definite value
to every counterfactual you can pose about it. [@pearl_primer] It comes with a **consistency** rule. If $X$ actually
was $x$, then $Y_x$ is just the observed $Y$ ($Y = XY_1+(1-X)Y_0$ for binary $X$), because setting a variable to
the value it already has changes nothing. [@pearl_primer] The difference between the two rungs is a difference
of *level*. $do(x)$ describes how a **population** behaves under intervention. $Y_x(u)$ describes a **specific
individual**, and that is what lets it express credit, blame, and regret. [@pearl_primer]

## Computing a counterfactual: abduction, action, prediction

With a fully specified model, any unit-level counterfactual takes three steps: [@pearl_primer]

1. **Abduction** — use the evidence to work out the unit's background factors $U$ (*explain the past*).
2. **Action** — replace the equation for $X$ with $X=x$ (*bend history, minimally*).
3. **Prediction** — solve the modified model with those same $U$'s (*replay the future*).

**The Primer's student.** Encouragement $X$ raises homework $H$, and both raise the exam score $Y$ (all
standardised), with $H = 0.5X + U_H$ and $Y = 0.7X + 0.4H + U_Y$. Joe is observed at $X=0.5$, $H=1$, $Y=1.5$.
*What would Joe have scored had he doubled his homework?* Abduction recovers Joe's idiosyncrasies,
$U_H = 0.75$ and $U_Y = 0.75$. Action sets $H=2$. Prediction gives $Y_{H=2} = 0.7(0.5)+0.4(2)+0.75 = 1.90$, up
from 1.5. [@pearl_primer] The $U$'s are what make it *Joe's* counterfactual rather than an average student's.
They carry "everything that is unique" about him into the other world.

**The Book of Why's Alice.** Here the stakes are higher, because the obvious data-driven answers are *wrong*.
[@pearl_why] Salary $S$ depends on experience $EX$ and education $ED$, and education *takes time away from*
experience. The model is $S = 65{,}000 + 2{,}500\,EX + 5{,}000\,ED + U_S$ and $EX = 10 - 4\,ED + U_{EX}$. Alice
has a high-school education and 6 years' experience. *What would she earn with a college degree?*

- **Regression imputation** plugs $ED=1$, $EX=6$ into the fitted salary equation and says **\$85,000**. It
  holds her experience fixed, as if the extra years in college cost her nothing. [@pearl_why]
- **The three steps.** Abduction gives $U_S = \$1{,}000$ and $U_{EX}=-4$. Action sets $ED=1$. Prediction
  first updates her experience, $EX_{ED=1} = 10-4-4 = 2$ years, and then her salary,
  $S_{ED=1} = 65{,}000 + 2{,}500(2) + 5{,}000 + 1{,}000 =$ **\$76,000**. [@pearl_why]

The \$9,000 gap comes from a classic error: **experience is a mediator, not a confounder**. Matching Alice to a
college graduate with the *same* experience compares her to someone who would have had *more* experience had
they stopped at high school. Equal experience, often treated as the ideal match, is actually evidence that
the two people differ in the way that matters. [@pearl_why] Pearl calls "mistaking a mediator for a confounder
... one of the deadliest sins in causal inference": the second invites adjustment and the first forbids it.
[@pearl_why] There is no free lunch here. The model supplied the answer, and with only a *partially* specified
model you get bounds rather than a number, just as in [partial identification](partial-identification.qmd).
[@pearl_why]

## Effects on the treated, and interventions do() can't write

Counterfactuals are also the natural language for several policy quantities.

**Effect of treatment on the treated (ETT).** A job-training pilot works in a randomized trial. Once it is
opened to volunteers, hiring among graduates is even higher. Critics object that self-enrollees are more
resourceful and might have found jobs anyway. The question they are asking is
$\text{ETT}=\mathbb E[Y_1 - Y_0\mid X=1]$, the [ATT](potential-outcomes.qmd), and its $\mathbb E[Y_0\mid X=1]$
term is another clash of worlds. [@pearl_primer] It is still often estimable. If covariates $Z$ satisfy the
[backdoor criterion](directed-acyclic-graphs.qmd), the ETT uses the ordinary adjustment formula with one change:
average over $P(Z=z\mid X=1)$, the *treated's* covariate mix, rather than $P(Z=z)$. [@pearl_primer] ETT is
also identified for binary $X$ when experimental and observational data are *both* available, and whenever a
[front-door](directed-acyclic-graphs.qmd) mediator exists. [@pearl_primer]

**Additive interventions.** "Add 5 mg/l of insulin to every patient's current dose" does **not** set everyone
to the same level, so it is not a $do(x)$. Its effect is $\sum_{x'}\mathbb E[Y_{x'+q}\mid X=x']P(x')$, an ETT-type
quantity. [@pearl_primer] It does *not* equal the average of dose-response effects over the population,
because people who *chose* dose $x'$ may respond differently from people *assigned* it. The two coincide only
without confounding. [@pearl_primer] Scientific findings are reported as do-expressions, which carry over
between populations. A policy question like "add $q$ to everyone" depends on who chose what in *this*
population, and counterfactual reasoning is how you translate between the two. [@pearl_primer]

**Policies targeted on a downstream variable.** "Send students with little homework ($H\le H_0$) to the program"
cannot be written as setting $X$, because $X$ itself causes $H$. The target is
$\mathbb E[Y_{X=1}\mid H\le H_0]$, a counterfactual restricted to a subgroup defined by a post-treatment variable.
[@pearl_primer]

## Attribution: necessity, sufficiency, and "but for"

The law's test of causation is **but-for**: the harm would not have occurred *but for* the defendant's act.
[@pearl_why] Written as a counterfactual, that is the **probability of necessity**:

$$
PN = P(Y_{0}=0 \mid X=1,\ Y=1),
$$

*the probability the outcome would not have happened without the act, given that the act and the outcome both
happened.* [@pearl_primer] A civil court finds for the plaintiff when this is "more probable than not," so
$PN > 1/2$. [@pearl_primer; @pearl_why] Its mirror image is the **probability of sufficiency**,
$PS = P(Y_1 = 1\mid X=0,\ Y=0)$: would the act *not* taken have produced the outcome? And $PNS = P(Y_1=1,\ Y_0=0)$
is the probability that the act is both necessary and sufficient. [@pearl_primer]

**Three patients** (the Primer, using a real trial). Irradiation after lumpectomy cut breast-cancer recurrence from
39% to 14% (Fisher et al., 2002). [@pearl_primer] Ms Jones had irradiation and is in remission. Asking whether she
owes her life to it is asking for **PN**. Mrs Smith skipped it and relapsed. Her regret is justified in proportion
to **PS**. Ms Daily is still deciding. She only wants irradiation if her tumour is the kind that would remit
*with* it and recur *without* it, so she needs **PNS**. [@pearl_primer] None of these is identified by the trial
alone. But under **monotonicity** (irradiation never causes a recurrence that would otherwise have remitted), PNS
reduces to the experimental risk difference, $0.39-0.14 = 0.25$. Ms Daily has about a one-in-four chance of
being a patient the treatment actually saves. [@pearl_primer]

**Why hindsight matters.** PN cannot be written as a do-expression. Conditioning on "the outcome happened"
changes our beliefs about the unit's background factors, for example about how strong the fire was that
killed the victim behind the blocked exit. Pearl and Mackenzie treat this as proof that rung three sits
strictly above rung two. [@pearl_why]

**Combining experiments and observation can settle a case.** Suppose a drug trial shows a death rate of 1.6%
under the drug and 1.4% without it, a "minor" effect. But among people who chose the drug themselves, 0.2%
died, against 2.8% among those who chose not to. [@pearl_primer] The observational **excess risk ratio** alone
is $-13$, which looks strongly *protective*. The **confounding correction**, which is what the experiment
contributes, is $+14$. Together they give $PN = 1$. Even without assuming monotonicity, the lower bound hits 1:
barring sampling error, the drug caused the death. [@pearl_primer] In general PN is only *bounded*, like any
[partially identified](partial-identification.qmd) quantity, and the width of the bounds depends on a single
observable ratio. [@pearl_primer]

**Necessity ≠ sufficiency.** A fire needs both a struck match and oxygen, and each is equally necessary. We
still blame the match. For the match both PN and PS are high, while for oxygen PN is high but PS is low, because
oxygen is almost always present. [@pearl_why] Two riflemen in a firing squad give the opposite pattern. Each
shot alone is sufficient, so for each rifleman $PS=1$ but $PN=0$: the prisoner would have died anyway.
[@pearl_why]

**Climate attribution** is the best-known large-scale use. Climatologists' *fraction of attributable risk* equals
PN under no confounding and monotonicity (greenhouse gases never *prevent* a heat wave). [@pearl_why] Allen and
Stott (2004) concluded it was "very likely that over half the risk" of a 2003-scale European heat wave was
attributable to human influence. The heat wave was blamed for nearly 15,000 deaths in France. [@pearl_why]
Hannart's counterfactual analysis put **PN at 0.9 but PS at only about 0.0072** for that particular summer.
Climate change was very likely *necessary* for it, but nothing made *that year* predictable. Over a 200-year
horizon, the probability that climate change is *sufficient* for another such heat wave rises to 80%.
[@pearl_why] Pearl traces two conflicting published studies of the 2010 Russian heat wave to the same split:
one argued from PN, the other from PS. [@pearl_why]

## Mediation, done right

Mediation asks the second kind of *why*: not whether $X$ affects $Y$, but *how*. The scurvy story shows why
this matters. The British navy believed citrus worked through **acidity**, switched to West Indian limes (with
only a quarter of the vitamin C), then "purified" the juice by cooking it. In Pearl's words, they were
"disabling the mediator", and scurvy came back
on polar expeditions. [@pearl_why] Getting the mechanism wrong can undo a cure.

The [DAG entry](directed-acyclic-graphs.qmd#mediation-when-the-mechanism-is-the-question) warned that "regress
$Y$ on $X$ and $M$" does not deliver a direct effect. Counterfactuals say what a direct effect *is*. With
treatment $T$, mediator $M$, and outcome $Y$, there are four distinct quantities: [@pearl_primer]

- **Total effect (TE)** — switch $T$ from 0 to 1 and let the mediator respond naturally.
- **Controlled direct effect, CDE($m$)** — switch $T$ with the mediator **forced** to the same level $m$ for
  everyone. This is a rung-two quantity: $do(T,\,M=m)$.
- **Natural direct effect (NDE)** — switch $T$ while each unit's mediator stays at *whatever value it would
  have taken without treatment*: $\mathbb E[Y_{1,M_0} - Y_{0,M_0}]$. In Pearl's words, freeze the mediator
  where it would have been and "wiggle the treatment." [@pearl_why]
- **Natural indirect effect (NIE)** — hold treatment at 0 but move each unit's mediator to the value it
  *would* take under treatment: $\mathbb E[Y_{0,M_1} - Y_{0,M_0}]$. This is the part of the effect the
  mediator can deliver on its own, with $Y$ "blinded" to $T$. [@pearl_primer]

The natural effects are **nested counterfactuals**. $Y_{1,M_0}$ combines treatment from one world with a
mediator value from another, which is why they cannot be written with $do(\cdot)$. [@pearl_why] The
distinction has practical bite. Gender-blind hiring would *disable* the direct path while leaving
qualifications as they are, and that is an NDE-type intervention, not "set qualification to $q$ for everyone."
[@pearl_primer] It is also the legal definition: under *Carson*, **discrimination is the direct effect**.
[@pearl_why]

### The mediation formula

If nothing confounds the mediator–outcome relationship (and treatment is randomized or deconfounded), the natural
effects reduce to ordinary conditional probabilities, whatever the functional form: [@pearl_primer]

$$
\text{NDE} = \sum_m \big[\mathbb E(Y\mid T{=}1,m) - \mathbb E(Y\mid T{=}0,m)\big]\,P(m\mid T{=}0),
\qquad
\text{NIE} = \sum_m \mathbb E(Y\mid T{=}0,m)\,\big[P(m\mid T{=}1) - P(m\mid T{=}0)\big].
$$

The NDE is a CDE averaged over the untreated mediator distribution. The NIE is a *product of two nonlinear
effects* and has no CDE interpretation. [@pearl_primer; @pearl_why] Robins and Greenland had described the
natural effects in words in 1992 but concluded they could not be estimated, partly because they combine
information from two "cross-worlds" that no experiment can replicate. Pearl's 2001 formula turned the
idea into something estimable. Pearl and Mackenzie count almost no papers titled "mediation analysis" before
2004 and more than a hundred a year now. [@pearl_why]

**Worked example** (the Primer's homework data, randomized with no mediator–outcome confounding). An enhanced
training program ($T$) raises the share of students doing more than 3 hours of homework ($M$) from 40% to 75%.
Exam pass rates are 0.80 / 0.40 for trained students with / without heavy homework and 0.30 / 0.20 for untrained
students. [@pearl_primer] The formula gives **TE = 0.46**, **NDE = 0.32** and **NIE = 0.035**. So about 30%
of the effect ($1-\text{NDE}/\text{TE}$) *needs* the homework channel, but only 7% ($\text{NIE}/\text{TE}$) could
be produced by the homework boost *alone*. The rest comes from the curriculum and homework **working together**.
[@pearl_primer] @fig-mediation-decomposition computes these quantities both ways.

### Why the classic recipe breaks

The **Baron–Kenny** approach (1986, one of the most-cited papers in all of science) runs two regressions, with and
without the mediator, and reads the indirect effect off the *difference* in treatment coefficients or the
*product* of path coefficients. [@pearl_why] Both shortcuts assume that **effects along distinct paths add and
coefficients along a path multiply**. That is true in a linear model without interactions and false as soon as
the mediator and treatment interact. [@pearl_why] Pearl's threshold example: a job offer comes if salary exceeds 10,
where salary $= 7\cdot\text{education} + 3\cdot\text{skill}$. The total effect of education is 1 (the offer
arrives), but the NIE is 0, because the skill gained alone lifts salary only to 6. TE is no longer NDE + NIE.
In general $\text{TE} = \text{NDE} - \text{NIE}_{r}$, where $\text{NIE}_r$ is the indirect effect of the
*reverse* transition. [@pearl_why; @pearl_primer] Asked whether the difference or the product method is right,
Pearl answers "Neither!" Both mistake the estimation procedure for the meaning. [@pearl_why]

A related trap is the **mediation fallacy**: *conditioning* on the mediator instead of *holding it constant*
by intervention. It does no harm when nothing confounds $M$ and $Y$. When something does, it can reverse
the conclusion. That is the collider mechanism from the DAG entry. Kruskal's counterexample to the
Berkeley-admissions analysis, where state of residence confounds department choice and admission, shows it
happening. [@pearl_why] Pearl and Mackenzie note that Fisher made the same mistake in 1936. [@pearl_why]

### Mediation in the field

- **Algebra for All (Chicago, 1997).** Guanglei Hong split the reform's effect on test scores into a **direct
  effect of about +2.7 points** (statistically significant) and an **indirect effect of about −2.3 points** through
  a worse classroom environment: lower-achieving students, now in mixed-ability classes, could not keep up.
  The side effect almost cancelled the curriculum's gain. The 2003 "Double-Dose Algebra" reform gave those
  students a second daily class closer to their old environment, repairing the channel the analysis had exposed. [@pearl_why]
- **The "smoking gene."** Tyler VanderWeele asked whether a nicotine-receptor variant raises lung-cancer risk by
  making people smoke more. The **indirect effect was only 1–3%**, about one extra cigarette a day. CDE(0) was
  roughly zero: a non-smoker with the gene is not at extra risk. The effect showed up in *smokers'* bodies.
  "The interaction between the gene and the subject's behavior is everything." [@pearl_why]
- **Tourniquets.** A study of wounded soldiers who *reached hospital* found no survival benefit from
  battlefield tourniquets. Selecting on arrival alive conditions on the mediator (pre-admission survival), so
  the study estimated a **direct** effect. Any life-saving indirect effect is invisible in data of that kind.
  [@pearl_why]

## Figure: decomposing an effect by its channels

```{python}
#| label: fig-mediation-decomposition
#| fig-cap: "Mediation in the Primer's homework example. Bars: the mediation formula applied to the published conditional probabilities (pass rates 0.80/0.40 for trained students with/without heavy homework, 0.30/0.20 for untrained; heavy-homework rates 0.75 trained vs 0.40 untrained). Dots: the same effects computed the 'impossible' way, by simulating 200,000 students from a structural model with those probabilities and evaluating each student's cross-world counterfactuals (such as Y with training but homework left at its untrained level) with the student's own background factors held fixed. The two agree. The orange bar is the share of the total effect that needs the mediator (TE − NDE ≈ 0.14), and it is four times the natural indirect effect (≈ 0.035) that the mediator produces on its own. The dashed line marks NDE + NIE ≈ 0.355, short of TE = 0.46. Under the treatment–mediator interaction the parts do not add up, which is why the regression 'difference' and 'product' methods disagree."
#| code-fold: true
#| code-summary: "Show the figure code (mediation formula + cross-world simulation by hand)"

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# Published inputs (Primer, Tables 4.6–4.7): E[Y | T, M] and E[M | T]
pY = {(1, 1): 0.80, (1, 0): 0.40, (0, 1): 0.30, (0, 0): 0.20}
pM = {0: 0.40, 1: 0.75}
pm = lambda m, t: pM[t] if m == 1 else 1 - pM[t]

# The mediation formulas (no mediator-outcome confounding)
TE  = sum(pY[(1, m)] * pm(m, 1) for m in (0, 1)) - sum(pY[(0, m)] * pm(m, 0) for m in (0, 1))
NDE = sum((pY[(1, m)] - pY[(0, m)]) * pm(m, 0) for m in (0, 1))
NIE = sum(pY[(0, m)] * (pm(m, 1) - pm(m, 0)) for m in (0, 1))

# Cross-world check: one structural model, each student keeps their own U's in every world
rng = np.random.default_rng(4)
N = 200_000
U_M, U_Y = rng.random(N), rng.random(N)
M = {t: (U_M < pM[t]).astype(int) for t in (0, 1)}                 # M_0, M_1 for every student
Y = lambda t, m: (U_Y < np.where(m == 1, pY[(t, 1)], pY[(t, 0)])).astype(int)
sim = {
    "TE": (Y(1, M[1]) - Y(0, M[0])).mean(),
    "NDE": (Y(1, M[0]) - Y(0, M[0])).mean(),   # treatment switched, mediator frozen at M_0
    "NIE": (Y(0, M[1]) - Y(0, M[0])).mean(),   # treatment held at 0, mediator moved to M_1
}
sim["TE − NDE"] = sim["TE"] - sim["NDE"]

labels = ["TE", "NDE", "NIE", "TE − NDE"]
vals = [TE, NDE, NIE, TE - NDE]
cols = ["#161613", "#5e5e54", "#b7b6a8", "#e8531d"]
desc = ["total effect", "natural direct (mediator frozen)",
        "natural indirect (mediator alone)", "needs the mediator"]

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(8, 3.9))
y = np.arange(len(labels))[::-1]
ax.barh(y, vals, color=cols, height=0.6, edgecolor="#f4f3ec")
ax.scatter([sim[l] for l in labels], y, s=46, zorder=3,
           facecolor="#f4f3ec", edgecolor="#161613", linewidth=1.4, label="cross-world simulation")
for yi, v, d in zip(y, vals, desc):
    ax.text(v + 0.012, yi, f"{v:.3f}  · {d}", va="center", fontsize=9)
ax.axvline(NDE + NIE, ls="--", lw=1.2, color="#8a8a7e")
ax.text(NDE + NIE + 0.004, 3.45, "NDE + NIE", fontsize=8.5, color="#5e5e54")
ax.set_yticks(y)
ax.set_yticklabels(labels)
ax.set_xlim(0, 0.75)
ax.set_ylim(-0.6, 3.7)
ax.set_xlabel("effect on the probability of passing the exam")
ax.set_title("One effect, four questions: the mediation formula vs. cross-world simulation", loc="left")
ax.legend(loc="lower right", frameon=False, fontsize=9)
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

## Common pitfalls

::: {.callout-warning title="Where this goes wrong"}

- **Answering a rung-three question with rung-one tools.** Imputing "what she would have earned" from a fitted
  regression, or matching on a post-treatment variable, gives a confident wrong number (\$85,000 against
  \$76,000 for Alice). Methods driven only by data cannot answer counterfactual questions. [@pearl_why]

- **Treating a mediator as a confounder.** Adjusting for (or matching on) something the treatment *causes*
  blocks part of the effect and compares unlike units. [@pearl_why]

- **Reading an average effect as an individual one.** A trial's risk difference is not the probability that
  *this* patient was helped. That is PN or PNS, which need extra assumptions (monotonicity) or extra data
  (observational + experimental) and are generally only bounded. [@pearl_primer]

- **Confusing "necessary" with "sufficient."** Attribution of a past event (PN) and prediction of a future one
  (PS) can differ enormously, as in PN 0.9 against PS 0.0072 for the 2003 heat wave. Conflicting "attribution"
  headlines often come from using different measures. [@pearl_why]

- **Baron–Kenny by default.** The difference and product methods are correct only in linear models without
  interactions. With a treatment–mediator interaction the parts don't sum to the total, and the two methods
  disagree. Define NDE/NIE first, then estimate them. [@pearl_why; @pearl_primer]

- **Conditioning on the mediator instead of holding it fixed.** This is the mediation fallacy. It is harmless
  only if nothing confounds $M$ and $Y$, and that assumption is *separate* from, and not guaranteed by, the
  randomization of $T$. [@pearl_why; @gerber_green]

- **Selecting on a mediator.** Studying only those who survived to hospital, stayed in the program, or got the
  job conditions on a post-treatment variable and estimates, at most, a direct effect. [@pearl_why]

:::

## Appendix · the mathematics {.unnumbered}

::: {.math-appendix}

**Counterfactuals from a structural model.** Let $M$ be an SCM with exogenous $U$ and structural equations
$V_i = f_i(\text{pa}_i, U_i)$. For unit $u$, $Y_x(u)$ is the solution for $Y$ in $M_x$, the model with $X$'s
equation replaced by $X=x$. With a distribution $P(u)$, a counterfactual query given evidence $e$ is answered by
(i) $P(u)\to P(u\mid e)$, (ii) forming $M_x$, (iii) computing $\mathbb E[Y_x\mid e]=\sum_u Y_x(u)P(u\mid e)$.
**Consistency:** $X=x \Rightarrow Y_x = Y$.

**ETT under the backdoor criterion.** If $Z$ satisfies the backdoor criterion for $X\to Y$, then
$Y_x \perp X \mid Z$, so
$$
P(Y_x=y\mid X=x') = \sum_z P(Y=y\mid X=x, Z=z)\,P(Z=z\mid X=x'),
$$
compared with $P(y\mid do(x)) = \sum_z P(y\mid x,z)P(z)$. Hence
$\text{ETT}=\mathbb E[Y\mid X{=}1] - \sum_z \mathbb E[Y\mid X{=}0,z]P(z\mid X{=}1)$.

**Probability of necessity.** If $Y$ is monotone in $X$ ($Y_1(u)\ge Y_0(u)$ for all $u$), then
$$
PN = \frac{P(y)-P(y\mid do(x'))}{P(x,y)}
   = \underbrace{\frac{P(y\mid x)-P(y\mid x')}{P(y\mid x)}}_{\text{ERR}}
   + \underbrace{\frac{P(y\mid x')-P(y\mid do(x'))}{P(x,y)}}_{\text{CF}} .
$$
Without monotonicity,
$\max\{0,\ \tfrac{P(y)-P(y\mid do(x'))}{P(x,y)}\}\le PN\le \min\{1,\ \tfrac{P(y'\mid do(x'))-P(x',y')}{P(x,y)}\}$.
The width of the interval is $q = P(y'\mid x)/P(y\mid x)$, whatever the confounding. Under monotonicity,
$PNS = P(y\mid do(x)) - P(y\mid do(x'))$.

**Natural effects.** For $t=f_T(u_T)$, $m=f_M(t,u_M)$, $y=f_Y(t,m,u_Y)$:
$\text{TE}=\mathbb E[Y_1-Y_0]$, $\text{CDE}(m)=\mathbb E[Y_{1,m}-Y_{0,m}]$,
$\text{NDE}=\mathbb E[Y_{1,M_0}-Y_{0,M_0}]$, $\text{NIE}=\mathbb E[Y_{0,M_1}-Y_{0,M_0}]$, and in general
$\text{TE}=\text{NDE}-\text{NIE}_r$. Sufficient conditions for identification: a covariate set $W$ with no
descendants of $T$ that blocks every backdoor path from $M$ to $Y$ (after removing $T\to M$ and $T\to Y$), with
the $W$-specific effects of $T$ on $M$ and of $\{T,M\}$ on $Y$ identifiable. When $W$ also deconfounds those,
$$
\text{NDE} = \sum_{m,w}\big[\mathbb E(Y\mid 1,m,w)-\mathbb E(Y\mid 0,m,w)\big]P(m\mid 0,w)P(w).
$$

**Why linear models hide the problem.** Take $y=\beta_1 m+\beta_2 t+\beta_3 tm+u_y$ and
$m=\gamma_1 t+u_m$ (with $\mathbb E[u_m]=0$). Then $\text{NDE}=\beta_2$, $\text{NIE}=\beta_1\gamma_1$, but
$\text{TE}=\beta_2+\gamma_1(\beta_1+\beta_3)$. The portion that *needs* mediation, $\text{TE}-\text{NDE}=
\gamma_1(\beta_1+\beta_3)$, exceeds the portion mediation *suffices* for by $\beta_3\gamma_1$. Only when
$\beta_3=0$ do we recover $\text{TE}=\text{NDE}+\text{NIE}$ and the Baron–Kenny product $\beta_1\gamma_1$.
(Specialisation of the Primer's Study question 4.5.3.)
