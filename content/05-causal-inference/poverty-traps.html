Poverty Traps: Testing a Theory’s Shape

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source

Whether the poor can be stuck depends on the shape of one curve. Theory can draw both shapes, so only evidence, gathered case by case, can say which one a given life is on.

A poverty trap is a claim about shape. If what you have today buys very little of tomorrow when you have almost nothing, and a lot once you clear some threshold, the curve linking today to tomorrow is an S, and the poorest drift down to a low point and stay there. If the poorest gain the most from each extra dollar, the curve is an inverted L, and no one is stuck. Both curves are easy to believe, so the question is empirical: find the slope at the bottom, one mechanism at a time.

Why it matters

The biggest argument in development economics is, underneath, an argument about a curve. Jeffrey Sachs holds that poor countries are poor because they are poor: hot, malarial, landlocked and unable to afford the investments that would make them productive. A large push of aid would then start a virtuous spiral. William Easterly answers that many poor countries have become rich and many rich ones poor, so poverty is not permanent and the idea of a trap is “bogus”. (Banerjee and Duflo 2011) The same split runs through policy for individuals. If people can be trapped, a single well-aimed transfer can change a life for good. If they cannot, the same transfer only speeds people a little along a path they were already on.

Banerjee & Duflo’s contribution is to turn this into a question you can test. They do not ask whether poverty traps exist in general. They ask when and where they do, and their answer, built from experiments, is that they “find them in some areas, but not in others.” They also note how often policy goes wrong because someone “thought there was a poverty trap somewhere and there was none, or they were ignoring another one that was right in front of them.” (Banerjee and Duflo 2011) This entry is about how that question is put to the data. It is also a lesson that applies far beyond development: a theory’s prediction often lives in the shape of a relationship, and a shape has to be measured, not assumed.

The S-curve and the inverted L

Plot income (or wealth) today on the horizontal axis and income tomorrow on the vertical. Tomorrow can mean next month or the next generation. What you have today decides how well you eat, whether you can treat an illness, buy fertiliser or keep a child in school, and those decide what you will have next. (Banerjee and Duflo 2011) Add the 45° diagonal, where tomorrow equals today. Wherever the curve sits above the diagonal, people get richer over time. Wherever it sits below, they get poorer.

Banerjee & Duflo state the general rule in one sentence. There is a trap “whenever the scope for growing income or wealth at a very fast rate is limited for those who have too little to invest, but expands dramatically for those who can invest a bit more.” If, instead, growth potential is highest among the poor and tapers off as people get richer, there is no trap. (Banerjee and Duflo 2011)

  • The S-shape. The curve is flat at the bottom, rises steeply, then flattens again. It cuts the diagonal three times. Below the middle crossing, the poverty-trap zone, the curve lies under the diagonal, so each period leaves people a little poorer until they settle at the low point. Above it, they climb towards a much higher resting point. Two people who start a short distance apart, either side of the threshold, end up in completely different places. (Banerjee and Duflo 2011)
  • The inverted L. The curve rises fastest at the start and then flattens, so it crosses the diagonal once. The poorest earn more than they started with and grow until they reach that single resting point. A one-time gift here moves someone along the path faster, “but it cannot change where they are eventually headed.” (Banerjee and Duflo 2011)

The two diagrams carry opposite policy advice. Under the S, a large one-off push that lifts someone over the threshold is permanent and worth almost any price. Under the inverted L, the same push is a temporary boost, and what the poor need is help along a road they are already on. That is why the shape matters so much, and why Banerjee & Duflo insist that “theory is not enough”: the debate has to be settled “case by case”. (Banerjee and Duflo 2011)

Show the figure code 
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

S = lambda y: 0.04 + 0.92 / (1 + np.exp(-9 * (y - 0.5)))   # S-shape: three fixed points
L = lambda y: 0.95 * y ** 0.45                              # inverted L: one fixed point

def staircase(f, y0, steps):
    xs, ys = [y0], [y0]
    y = y0
    for _ in range(steps):
        y_next = f(y)
        xs += [y, y_next]; ys += [y_next, y_next]
        y = y_next
    return np.array(xs), np.array(ys)

grid = np.linspace(0, 1.0, 400)
fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(9.6, 4.4), sharey=True)

# Left: S-curve with a trap
ax = axes[0]
ax.plot(grid, grid, color="#b7b6a8", lw=1.2, label="tomorrow = today")
ax.plot(grid, S(grid), color="#161613", lw=2.2, label="S-shaped curve")
x, y = staircase(S, 0.46, 12)
ax.plot(x, y, color="#e8531d", lw=1.4, label="start just below threshold")
x, y = staircase(S, 0.54, 12)
ax.plot(x, y, color="#5e5e54", lw=1.4, ls="--", label="start just above threshold")
for yp, name, fill in [(0.0567, "N", True), (0.5, "threshold", False), (0.9433, "high", True)]:
    ax.plot(yp, yp, "o", ms=6, color="#161613", mfc="#161613" if fill else "#f4f3ec", zorder=5)
ax.annotate("N (trap)", (0.0567, 0.0567), xytext=(0.12, 0.02), fontsize=9, color="#161613")
ax.annotate("unstable\nthreshold", (0.5, 0.5), xytext=(0.58, 0.36), fontsize=9, color="#5e5e54")
ax.set_title("S-shape: a poverty trap")
ax.set_xlabel("income today")
ax.set_ylabel("income tomorrow")
ax.legend(fontsize=8, loc="upper left", frameon=False)

# Right: inverted L, no trap
ax = axes[1]
ax.plot(grid, grid, color="#b7b6a8", lw=1.2, label="tomorrow = today")
ax.plot(grid, L(grid), color="#161613", lw=2.2, label="inverted-L curve")
x, y = staircase(L, 0.05, 10)
ax.plot(x, y, color="#5e5e54", lw=1.4, label="start at A1")
x, y = staircase(L, 0.30, 10)
ax.plot(x, y, color="#5e5e54", lw=1.4, ls="--", label="one-time transfer to A2")

Testing the shape, one mechanism at a time

There is no single “poverty curve” to estimate. Each possible trap runs through a specific channel, such as food, health, schooling or business capital, and each channel has its own curve. The test is always about the same feature: is the return to a little more very low at the bottom, or very high? Banerjee & Duflo work through the channels one by one, and the verdicts differ.

Nutrition: the classic S, mostly rejected for adults. The story is intuitive. Below some intake the body can barely sustain itself, so there is nothing left for hard work. Above it, extra food becomes strength, strength becomes earnings, and earnings buy more food. That is an S. (Banerjee and Duflo 2011) John Strauss went looking for the clearest case, self-employed farmers in Sierra Leone whose work is physically hard. A 10% increase in calories raised a worker’s productivity by at most 4%, and the shape of the relationship was an inverted L: the largest gains came at the lowest intake, with no jump once people ate enough. (Banerjee and Duflo 2011) Banerjee & Duflo conclude that most adults, even the very poor, are outside the nutrition trap zone. They keep the logic for groups who do not choose what they eat: babies in the womb and young children. There, small investments such as deworming pills at $1.36 a year, which over two years rather than one were linked to a lifetime income gain of $3,269, compound over a lifetime, so the S may be real. (Banerjee and Duflo 2011)

Health: ladders exist, but demand does not climb them. Bednets could, by Banerjee & Duflo’s estimate, raise incomes by about 15% by preventing malaria. For a health trap to unwind by itself, that extra income would have to make the next generation reliably buy nets. It does not. People who are 15% richer are only 5% more likely to buy a net, so a single free distribution would raise the share of children sleeping under one in the next generation from about 47% to 52%. (Banerjee and Duflo 2011) The curve has the right shape for a ladder, but the income gradient is too flat to drive people up it.

Small business: high marginal returns, low ceilings. Here the evidence is a lottery. In Sri Lanka, owners of tiny businesses entered a draw for a grant of $250 or $500 (PPP). The return on the first $250 was over 60% a year, and a similar experiment in Mexico found 10–15% a month. (Banerjee and Duflo 2011) That is the opposite of a trap at the bottom: the poorest firms have high marginal returns. But businesses that got $500 did not earn more than those that got $250. Owners invested only about half the larger grant, probably because tripling a tiny firm’s capital would have meant hiring a worker or finding new space. (Banerjee and Duflo 2011) Banerjee & Duflo read this as an inverted L that flattens fast. Growing a little is easy; growing a lot runs into a ceiling. That rules out a capital trap at the bottom, but it also explains why microcredit rarely turns a shop into a firm.

Assets and shocks: why a trap matters even for the non-poor. Ibu Tina and her husband ran a profitable garment business with four employees, comfortably above the threshold. A theft wiped out their assets and left them below it. With too little capital to run at a profitable scale, they kept getting poorer. (Banerjee and Duflo 2011) On an S-curve, one bad break can be permanent, which is why Banerjee & Duflo tell her story in their chapter on risk: when the curve is S-shaped, protection against shocks matters more than average incomes suggest. (Banerjee and Duflo 2011)

A trap made of beliefs

The most striking case in the book is a trap that is not in the world at all. Parents in Madagascar believed that each year of primary school would raise a child’s income by 6%, junior secondary by 12% and senior secondary by 20%. In Morocco, parents valued a year of primary school for girls at almost nothing, 0.4%, and a year of secondary at 17%. (Banerjee and Duflo 2011) The real estimates show earnings rising “more or less proportionally” with each year of schooling. In Banerjee & Duflo’s words, “parents see an S-shape where there really isn’t one.” (Banerjee and Duflo 2011)

The perceived S changes behaviour. If only the later years pay off, a family that cannot afford to educate every child should pick a winner and concentrate on that child. A Bogotá conditional-cash-transfer lottery caught this happening: in families that entered two children and won for one, the child who lost was less likely to be enrolled than children in families where both lost, even though the family’s income had risen. (Banerjee and Duflo 2011) So a mistaken belief that the curve is S-shaped creates the very trap it imagines: “Education is valuable at every level”, but parents “behave as if there were a poverty trap, and thereby inadvertently to create one.” (Banerjee and Duflo 2011)

For a researcher, this adds a second curve to measure. Besides the true return, there is the return people believe in, and behaviour responds to the second. In Madagascar, simply telling parents the average income gain from another year of school raised test scores, and the gain was twice as large for parents who learned they had underestimated the benefits. Banerjee & Duflo call it “so far the cheapest known way to improve test scores” among the interventions evaluated. (Banerjee and Duflo 2011)

Common pitfalls

WarningWhere this goes wrong
  • Arguing about traps in the aggregate. “Do poverty traps exist?” has no general answer. The shape differs by channel (food, health, schooling, capital) and by group (adults vs young children). Ask which mechanism, for whom. (Banerjee and Duflo 2011)
  • Reading a trap off cross-country correlations. That rich countries grew and poor ones did not is consistent with both curves. Separating them needs evidence on the slope at the bottom, typically from experiments that change one input for some people and not others. (Banerjee and Duflo 2011)
  • Confusing marginal and average returns. A tiny shop can earn 60% a year on its next $250 and still barely cover its owner’s time. A high marginal return at the bottom rules out a capital trap, but it does not mean the business will grow; the curve can flatten quickly. (Banerjee and Duflo 2011)
  • Judging a one-time transfer by its first-year effect. On an S-curve a transfer that clears the threshold keeps paying; on an inverted L it fades as everyone converges. A short follow-up cannot distinguish the two. Durable effects are the signature of a trap.
  • Ignoring beliefs. If people act as if the curve is S-shaped, they can create a trap the technology does not have. Measure perceived returns alongside real ones. (Banerjee and Duflo 2011)

From the sources

NoteWho says what
  • Banerjee & Duflo, Poor Economics — the Sachs vs Easterly debate recast as a question about the shape of the curve linking income today to income tomorrow; the general rule for a trap; the S-shape (poverty-trap zone, low point N, trajectories A1→A2→A3 and B1→B2→B3) vs the inverted L (a one-time gift cannot change where people are headed); “theory is not enough” and “case by case”; traps found “in some areas, but not in others”. Channel by channel: the nutrition S and Strauss’s Sierra Leone result (10% more calories → at most 4% more productivity; an inverted L); micronutrients and early childhood (deworming $1.36/yr; $3,269 lifetime gain); bednets (15% richer → 5% more likely to buy; 47% → 52%); the Sri Lanka grant lottery (over 60%/yr on the first $250; $500 no better) and Mexico (10–15%/month); marginal vs overall return and the OP vs OZ technologies; Ibu Tina pushed into the trap zone by a single shock; and the illusory S-shape of schooling returns (Madagascar 6/12/20%, Morocco 0.4% vs 17% for girls; Bogotá sibling lottery).
  • Strogatz, Nonlinear Dynamics and Chaos — the stability condition for a fixed point of a map, used in the appendix.

Appendix · the mathematics

The model. Let y_t be income (or wealth) in period t and y_{t+1}=f(y_t) the curve in the figure. A resting point is a fixed point, y^\ast=f(y^\ast): a crossing of the 45° line.

Stability. Near y^\ast, write y_t=y^\ast+\varepsilon_t. A first-order expansion gives \varepsilon_{t+1}\approx f'(y^\ast)\,\varepsilon_t, so a small deviation shrinks when |f'(y^\ast)|<1 and grows when |f'(y^\ast)|>1. This is the same condition as for the logistic map. (Strogatz 2015)

  • An S-curve that starts above the diagonal at zero, dips below it, and ends below it at high incomes crosses three times. At the outer two crossings the curve is flatter than the diagonal (f'<1): both are stable, the trap N and the high point. At the middle crossing it is steeper (f'>1): that point is unstable. It is the threshold, and it separates two basins of attraction.
  • An inverted L, such as f(y)=Ay^{\alpha} with 0<\alpha<1, crosses once (at y^\ast=A^{1/(1-\alpha)} for y>0), and there f'(y^\ast)=\alpha<1. Every positive starting point converges to y^\ast.

What a one-time transfer does. A transfer moves y_0 to y_0+\tau without changing f. On the inverted L the limit is y^\ast either way, so the transfer only shortens the path. On the S-curve the limit jumps from N to the high point if and only if y_0+\tau clears the threshold. The policy-relevant quantity is therefore the threshold and the distance to it, not the average return.

Marginal vs average return. For a business with output f(k) from capital k, the marginal return is f'(k) and the overall return is f(k)/k. For a concave curve, f'(k)<f(k)/k beyond the origin, but both can be large at small k, and f'(k) can be high at the scale a microenterprise operates while f(k) itself stays small. The Sri Lanka lottery measures f'(k) directly: random assignment of $250 vs $0 gives the slope at the bottom, and $500 vs $250 shows how fast it falls. (Banerjee and Duflo 2011)

Connections

  • Builds on: randomized experiments — the lotteries and field trials that measure the slope at the bottom; the fertiliser sequence there is another case of narrowing a mechanism experiment by experiment. Also nonlinear dynamics — fixed points, stability and basins of attraction.
  • Contrasts with: structural estimation, which would write down f and estimate it whole. Banerjee & Duflo instead test one feature of f at a time with design-based evidence.
  • Leads to: sensitivity & external validity — a curve measured in Sierra Leone or Sri Lanka need not hold elsewhere, which is why the answer is “case by case” — and systems traps & leverage points, the systems-thinking view of self-reinforcing loops.

investments that would make them productive. A large push of aid would then start a virtuous spiral. William
Easterly answers that many poor countries have become rich and many rich ones poor, so poverty is not
permanent and the idea of a trap is "bogus". [@banerjee_duflo] The same split runs through policy for
individuals. If people can be trapped, a single well-aimed transfer can change a life for good. If they
cannot, the same transfer only speeds people a little along a path they were already on.

Banerjee & Duflo's contribution is to turn this into a question you can test. They do not ask whether poverty
traps exist in general. They ask *when and where* they do, and their answer, built from experiments, is
that they *"find them in some areas, but not in others."* They also note how often policy goes wrong because
someone *"thought there was a poverty trap somewhere and there was none, or they were ignoring another one
that was right in front of them."* [@banerjee_duflo] This entry is about how that question is put to the
data. It is also a lesson that applies far beyond development: a theory's prediction often lives in the
*shape* of a relationship, and a shape has to be measured, not assumed.

## The S-curve and the inverted L

Plot **income (or wealth) today** on the horizontal axis and **income tomorrow** on the vertical. Tomorrow
can mean next month or the next generation. What you have today decides how well you eat, whether you can
treat an illness, buy fertiliser or keep a child in school, and those decide what you will have next.
[@banerjee_duflo] Add the 45° diagonal, where tomorrow equals today. Wherever the curve sits *above* the
diagonal, people get richer over time. Wherever it sits *below*, they get poorer.

Banerjee & Duflo state the general rule in one sentence. There is a trap *"whenever the scope for growing
income or wealth at a very fast rate is limited for those who have too little to invest, but expands
dramatically for those who can invest a bit more."* If, instead, growth potential is highest among the
poor and tapers off as people get richer, there is no trap. [@banerjee_duflo]

- **The S-shape.** The curve is flat at the bottom, rises steeply, then flattens again. It cuts the
  diagonal three times. Below the middle crossing, the *poverty-trap zone*, the curve lies under the
  diagonal, so each period leaves people a little poorer until they settle at the low point. Above it, they
  climb towards a much higher resting point. Two people who start a short distance apart, either side of the
  threshold, end up in completely different places. [@banerjee_duflo]
- **The inverted L.** The curve rises fastest at the start and then flattens, so it crosses the diagonal
  once. The poorest earn more than they started with and grow until they reach that single resting point.
  A one-time gift here moves someone *along* the path faster, *"but it cannot change where they are
  eventually headed."* [@banerjee_duflo]

The two diagrams carry opposite policy advice. Under the S, a large one-off push that lifts someone over the
threshold is permanent and worth almost any price. Under the inverted L, the same push is a temporary boost,
and what the poor need is help along a road they are already on. That is why the shape matters so much,
and why Banerjee & Duflo insist that *"theory is not enough"*: the debate has to be settled *"case by
case"*. [@banerjee_duflo]

```{python}
#| label: fig-poverty-trap-shapes
#| fig-cap: "The two shapes, with the 45° line where tomorrow equals today. Left: an S-shaped curve crosses the diagonal three times. Starting just below the middle (unstable) crossing, the staircase path (orange) slides down to the low resting point N. Starting just above it, the path climbs to the high resting point. The two starts are almost the same; their destinations are not. Right: an inverted-L curve crosses once. A household starting very poor (A1) and one given a one-time transfer to A2 both converge to the same resting point; the gift only speeds the trip."
#| code-fold: true
#| code-summary: "Show the figure code"
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

S = lambda y: 0.04 + 0.92 / (1 + np.exp(-9 * (y - 0.5)))   # S-shape: three fixed points
L = lambda y: 0.95 * y ** 0.45                              # inverted L: one fixed point

def staircase(f, y0, steps):
    xs, ys = [y0], [y0]
    y = y0
    for _ in range(steps):
        y_next = f(y)
        xs += [y, y_next]; ys += [y_next, y_next]
        y = y_next
    return np.array(xs), np.array(ys)

grid = np.linspace(0, 1.0, 400)
fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(9.6, 4.4), sharey=True)

# Left: S-curve with a trap
ax = axes[0]
ax.plot(grid, grid, color="#b7b6a8", lw=1.2, label="tomorrow = today")
ax.plot(grid, S(grid), color="#161613", lw=2.2, label="S-shaped curve")
x, y = staircase(S, 0.46, 12)
ax.plot(x, y, color="#e8531d", lw=1.4, label="start just below threshold")
x, y = staircase(S, 0.54, 12)
ax.plot(x, y, color="#5e5e54", lw=1.4, ls="--", label="start just above threshold")
for yp, name, fill in [(0.0567, "N", True), (0.5, "threshold", False), (0.9433, "high", True)]:
    ax.plot(yp, yp, "o", ms=6, color="#161613", mfc="#161613" if fill else "#f4f3ec", zorder=5)
ax.annotate("N (trap)", (0.0567, 0.0567), xytext=(0.12, 0.02), fontsize=9, color="#161613")
ax.annotate("unstable\nthreshold", (0.5, 0.5), xytext=(0.58, 0.36), fontsize=9, color="#5e5e54")
ax.set_title("S-shape: a poverty trap")
ax.set_xlabel("income today")
ax.set_ylabel("income tomorrow")
ax.legend(fontsize=8, loc="upper left", frameon=False)

# Right: inverted L, no trap
ax = axes[1]
ax.plot(grid, grid, color="#b7b6a8", lw=1.2, label="tomorrow = today")
ax.plot(grid, L(grid), color="#161613", lw=2.2, label="inverted-L curve")
x, y = staircase(L, 0.05, 10)
ax.plot(x, y, color="#5e5e54", lw=1.4, label="start at A1")
x, y = staircase(L, 0.30, 10)
ax.plot(x, y, color="#5e5e54", lw=1.4, ls="--", label="one-time transfer to A2")
ystar = 0.95 ** (1 / 0.55)
ax.plot(ystar, ystar, "o", ms=6, color="#161613", zorder=5)
ax.annotate("A1", (0.05, 0.05), xytext=(0.08, 0.07), fontsize=9, color="#161613")
ax.annotate("A2", (0.30, 0.30), xytext=(0.32, 0.24), fontsize=9, color="#161613")
ax.annotate("same destination", (ystar, ystar), xytext=(0.22, 0.90), fontsize=9, color="#161613",
            arrowprops=dict(arrowstyle="->", color="#5e5e54", lw=0.8))
ax.set_title("Inverted L: no trap")
ax.set_xlabel("income today")
ax.legend(fontsize=8, loc="lower right", frameon=False)

for ax in axes:
    ax.set_xlim(0, 1.0); ax.set_ylim(0, 1.0); ax.set_aspect("equal")
fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

## Testing the shape, one mechanism at a time

There is no single "poverty curve" to estimate. Each possible trap runs through a specific channel, such as
food, health, schooling or business capital, and each channel has its own curve. The test is always about
the same feature: **is the return to a little more very low at the bottom, or very high?** Banerjee & Duflo
work through the channels one by one, and the verdicts differ.

**Nutrition: the classic S, mostly rejected for adults.** The story is intuitive. Below some intake the body
can barely sustain itself, so there is nothing left for hard work. Above it, extra food becomes strength,
strength becomes earnings, and earnings buy more food. That is an S. [@banerjee_duflo] John Strauss went looking
for the clearest case, self-employed farmers in Sierra Leone whose work is physically hard. A **10%** increase
in calories raised a worker's productivity by **at most 4%**, and the shape of the relationship was an
**inverted L**: the largest gains came at the *lowest* intake, with no jump once people ate enough.
[@banerjee_duflo] Banerjee & Duflo conclude that most adults, even the very poor, are *outside* the nutrition
trap zone. They keep the logic for groups who do not choose what they eat: babies in the womb and young
children. There, small investments such as deworming pills at **\$1.36** a year, which over two years
rather than one were linked to a lifetime income gain of **\$3,269**, compound over a lifetime, so the S
may be real. [@banerjee_duflo]

**Health: ladders exist, but demand does not climb them.** Bednets could, by Banerjee & Duflo's
estimate, raise incomes by about **15%** by preventing malaria. For a health trap to unwind by itself, that
extra income would have to make the next generation reliably buy nets. It does not. People who are **15%
richer** are only **5% more likely** to buy a net, so a single free distribution would raise the share of
children sleeping under one in the next generation from about **47% to 52%**. [@banerjee_duflo] The curve has
the right shape for a ladder, but the income gradient is too flat to drive people up it.

**Small business: high marginal returns, low ceilings.** Here the evidence is a lottery. In Sri Lanka, owners
of tiny businesses entered a draw for a grant of **\$250** or **\$500** (PPP). The return on the first \$250
was **over 60% a year**, and a similar experiment in Mexico found **10–15% a month**. [@banerjee_duflo] That is
the opposite of a trap at the bottom: the poorest firms have *high* marginal returns. But businesses that
got \$500 did not earn more than those that got \$250. Owners invested only about half the larger grant,
probably because tripling a tiny firm's capital would have meant hiring a worker or finding new space.
[@banerjee_duflo] Banerjee & Duflo read this as an inverted L that flattens fast. Growing a little is easy;
growing a lot runs into a ceiling. That rules out a capital trap at the bottom, but it also explains why
microcredit rarely turns a shop into a firm.

**Assets and shocks: why a trap matters even for the non-poor.** Ibu Tina and her husband ran a profitable
garment business with four employees, comfortably above the threshold. A theft wiped out their assets and
left them below it. With too little capital to run at a profitable scale, they kept getting poorer.
[@banerjee_duflo] On an S-curve, one bad break can be permanent, which is why Banerjee & Duflo tell her
story in their chapter on risk: when the curve is S-shaped, protection against shocks matters more than
average incomes suggest. [@banerjee_duflo]

## A trap made of beliefs

The most striking case in the book is a trap that is not in the world at all. Parents in Madagascar believed
that each year of primary school would raise a child's income by **6%**, junior secondary by **12%** and
senior secondary by **20%**. In Morocco, parents valued a year of primary school for girls at almost
nothing, **0.4%**, and a year of secondary at **17%**. [@banerjee_duflo] The real estimates show earnings rising
*"more or less proportionally"* with each year of schooling. In Banerjee & Duflo's words, *"parents see an
S-shape where there really isn't one."* [@banerjee_duflo]

The perceived S changes behaviour. If only the later years pay off, a family that cannot afford to educate
every child should pick a winner and concentrate on that child. A Bogotá conditional-cash-transfer lottery
caught this happening: in families that entered two children and won for one, the child who lost was *less*
likely to be enrolled than children in families where both lost, even though the family's income had risen.
[@banerjee_duflo] So a mistaken belief that the curve is S-shaped creates the very trap it imagines:
*"Education is valuable at every level"*, but parents *"behave as if there were a poverty trap, and thereby
inadvertently to create one."* [@banerjee_duflo]

For a researcher, this adds a second curve to measure. Besides the true return, there is the return people
*believe* in, and behaviour responds to the second. In Madagascar, simply telling parents the average
income gain from another year of school raised test scores, and the gain was twice as large for parents
who learned they had underestimated the benefits. Banerjee & Duflo call it *"so far the cheapest known way
to improve test scores"* among the interventions evaluated. [@banerjee_duflo]

## Common pitfalls

::: {.callout-warning title="Where this goes wrong"}
- **Arguing about traps in the aggregate.** "Do poverty traps exist?" has no general answer. The shape
  differs by channel (food, health, schooling, capital) and by group (adults vs young children). Ask which
  mechanism, for whom. [@banerjee_duflo]
- **Reading a trap off cross-country correlations.** That rich countries grew and poor ones did not is
  consistent with both curves. Separating them needs evidence on the *slope at the bottom*, typically from
  experiments that change one input for some people and not others. [@banerjee_duflo]
- **Confusing marginal and average returns.** A tiny shop can earn 60% a year on its next \$250 and still
  barely cover its owner's time. A high marginal return at the bottom rules out a capital trap, but it does
  not mean the business will grow; the curve can flatten quickly. [@banerjee_duflo]
- **Judging a one-time transfer by its first-year effect.** On an S-curve a transfer that clears the
  threshold keeps paying; on an inverted L it fades as everyone converges. A short follow-up cannot
  distinguish the two. Durable effects are the signature of a trap.
- **Ignoring beliefs.** If people *act* as if the curve is S-shaped, they can create a trap the technology
  does not have. Measure perceived returns alongside real ones. [@banerjee_duflo]
:::

## Appendix · the mathematics {.unnumbered}

::: {.math-appendix}

**The model.** Let $y_t$ be income (or wealth) in period $t$ and $y_{t+1}=f(y_t)$ the curve in the figure.
A **resting point** is a fixed point, $y^\ast=f(y^\ast)$: a crossing of the 45° line.

**Stability.** Near $y^\ast$, write $y_t=y^\ast+\varepsilon_t$. A first-order expansion gives
$\varepsilon_{t+1}\approx f'(y^\ast)\,\varepsilon_t$, so a small deviation shrinks when $|f'(y^\ast)|<1$
and grows when $|f'(y^\ast)|>1$. This is the same condition as for the
[logistic map](../complexity/nonlinear-dynamics-and-chaos.qmd). [@strogatz_nonlinear]

- An **S-curve** that starts above the diagonal at zero, dips below it, and ends below it at high incomes
  crosses three times. At the outer two crossings the curve is flatter than the diagonal ($f'<1$): both are
  stable, the trap $N$ and the high point. At the middle crossing it is steeper ($f'>1$): that point is
  unstable. It is the **threshold**, and it separates two *basins of attraction*.
- An **inverted L**, such as $f(y)=Ay^{\alpha}$ with $0<\alpha<1$, crosses once (at $y^\ast=A^{1/(1-\alpha)}$
  for $y>0$), and there $f'(y^\ast)=\alpha<1$. Every positive starting point converges to $y^\ast$.

**What a one-time transfer does.** A transfer moves $y_0$ to $y_0+\tau$ without changing $f$. On the inverted
L the limit is $y^\ast$ either way, so the transfer only shortens the path. On the S-curve the limit jumps
from $N$ to the high point if and only if $y_0+\tau$ clears the threshold. The policy-relevant quantity is
therefore the **threshold** and the **distance to it**, not the average return.

**Marginal vs average return.** For a business with output $f(k)$ from capital $k$, the marginal return is
$f'(k)$ and the overall return is $f(k)/k$. For a concave curve, $f'(k)<f(k)/k$ beyond the origin, but both
can be large at small $k$, and $f'(k)$ can be high at the scale a microenterprise operates while $f(k)$
itself stays small. The Sri Lanka lottery measures $f'(k)$ directly: random assignment of \$250 vs \$0 gives
the slope at the bottom, and \$500 vs \$250 shows how fast it falls. [@banerjee_duflo]

:::
