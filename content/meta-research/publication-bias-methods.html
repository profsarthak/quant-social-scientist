Publication Bias: Detecting and Correcting a Filtered Literature

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source

A meta-analysis can only pool the studies it can find, and the ones it can’t find are often the disappointing ones. Funnel plots, regression tests, trim-and-fill, PET-PEESE, p-curve and selection models each guess at what is missing. They often disagree, and that disagreement is worth reporting.

If significant results are more likely to be published, the studies a meta-analysis collects are a filtered sample, and pooling them precisely gives a precise answer for the filtered sample. No method can see the missing studies. Each one assumes a story about how studies went missing and corrects for that story. Small-study methods assume the missing studies are small ones with small effects. p-curve assumes they are the ones with p \ge 0.05. Selection models let you write the story down explicitly. On the same data these methods can give anything from “no effect” to “no bias”, so the honest practice is to run several, report the range, and remember that a thorough search for unpublished studies does more than any of them.

Why it matters

By the 1990s it was taken as settled that antidepressants such as SSRIs beat placebo, and most of that evidence came from meta-analyses of published trials. In The Emperor’s New Drugs (2002), Irving Kirsch and colleagues used the US Freedom of Information Act to get the trial data that drug companies had submitted to the FDA, including trials that were never published. With the unpublished trials added, the advantage over placebo was at best minimal (Harrer et al. 2022). Kirsch argued that companies had published the favourable trials and held back the disappointing ones. The claim is still disputed, and Harrer et al. use it without taking sides, as an illustration of how much the missing studies can matter (Harrer et al. 2022).

Their analogy is an iceberg. If you can only see the tip, you can measure it perfectly and still get its size wrong (Harrer et al. 2022). Every model in pooling studies estimates the average effect of the studies it is given. If the studies it is given are a biased selection, the estimate inherits the bias, however careful the statistics. The consequences Harrer et al. list are overestimated treatment effects, missed side-effects, and continued belief in theories that are wrong.

The gateway entry introduces the funnel plot and the file drawer. This entry covers what to do about them: how to look for the bias, the main ways of correcting for it, and why the results of those corrections need careful reading.

What counts as publication bias

Publication bias exists when a study’s chance of being published depends on its results (Harrer et al. 2022). There is good evidence that significant results, and results that confirm the hypothesis, are more likely to be published. It belongs to a wider family of non-reporting biases, each of which keeps some results out of a meta-analysis (Harrer et al. 2022):

  • Citation bias: negative or inconclusive studies are cited less, so reference-list searches miss them.
  • Time-lag bias: positive results come out sooner, so a recent literature over-represents them.
  • Multiple-publication bias: successful studies are reported in several articles (“salami slicing”), which makes them easier to find.
  • Language bias: studies not published in English are harder to find, and may differ systematically from those that are.
  • Outcome-reporting bias: a study measures several outcomes and reports the ones that worked. The paper is published, but the unfavourable result is still missing.

Even a complete set of studies can be biased by questionable research practices inside them (Harrer et al. 2022). The two Harrer et al. name are p-hacking (adjusting outlier rules, subgroups or missing-data handling until p < 0.05) and HARKing, hypothesising after the results are known: presenting an exploratory finding as if it had been predicted, or quietly dropping hypotheses the data did not support. HARKing inflates the false-discovery rate and, in its second form, produces outcome-reporting bias.

There are two lines of defence (Harrer et al. 2022). The first is the search. Look for grey literature (dissertations, preprints, government reports, conference proceedings), search trial registries such as the ICTRP and OSF Registries for studies that were registered but never reported, and ask authors for unpublished data. One large study found that including grey and unpublished literature helps prevent overestimated effects (McAuley et al. 2000). The second line is statistical, and the rest of this entry is about it. None of these methods detects publication bias directly. Each looks for a pattern in the data that publication bias would produce.

Small-study effects: the theory behind the funnel

The oldest family of methods looks for small-study effects, and it rests on a simple story about which studies go missing (Harrer et al., following Borenstein et al.) (Harrer et al. 2022):

  • Large studies cost so much time and money that they get published whatever they find.
  • Medium-sized studies risk non-publication, but they usually have enough power to reach significance, so only some of them disappear.
  • Small studies are most likely to end up in the file drawer. They only reach significance if their effect is very large, so the small studies that are published are the ones with very large effects.

The mechanism is “only significant results get published”. Because significance is easier to reach in large samples, the damage falls mainly on small studies. In a meta-analysis this produces a relationship between a study’s precision and its effect size, which shouldn’t otherwise exist.

The funnel plot

A funnel plot shows each study’s effect size (horizontal) against its standard error (vertical, with the axis reversed so the most precise studies are at the top) (Harrer et al. 2022). Without bias, the points form a symmetric, upside-down funnel: precise studies cluster near the pooled effect, and imprecise ones spread out evenly to both sides, because a large standard error means the observed effect can land far from the true one in either direction.

In Harrer et al.’s eighteen trials of third-wave psychotherapies (the running example in pooling and heterogeneity), the funnel is lopsided (Harrer et al. 2022). Three small studies (Shapiro et al., Kang et al., DanitzOrsillo) sit in the bottom-right corner with very large effects, and nothing balances them in the bottom left: there are no small studies with small or negative effects. The most precise study, de Vibe et al., also sits well below the pooled effect.

A contour-enhanced funnel plot (Peters et al. 2008) adds shaded regions showing where a study would be significant at p < 0.1, 0.05 and 0.01 (Harrer et al. 2022). That links the asymmetry to significance, which is what the publication-bias story is about. In the therapy data, all three small, large-effect studies are significant despite their large standard errors. Imputed studies that would make the funnel symmetric would fall in the non-significant region, or show significant harm. Among the larger studies, several are not significant at 0.05, but almost all of them sit just inside the 0.05 < p < 0.1 band. Harrer et al. suggest two explanations: the original papers may have computed the effect a different way that was significant, or significance “at trend level” was enough to get published (Harrer et al. 2022).

Asymmetry is a warning sign, not proof. Harrer et al., after Page et al. (2020), list benign causes of the same pattern (Harrer et al. 2022):

  • Heterogeneity. The funnel assumes the scatter is sampling error around one effect. If small studies really do estimate different true effects, the funnel can be asymmetric without anything missing.
  • Different procedures in small studies. Treatment fidelity is easier to maintain in a small trial, so small studies may genuinely find larger effects.
  • Quality. Low-quality studies tend to show larger effects, and small studies are more often low-quality.
  • Chance.

Egger’s regression test

There is no rule for when a funnel is “too asymmetric”, so Egger’s regression test puts a number on it (Harrer et al. 2022). Each study’s z-score (effect divided by its standard error) is regressed on its precision (one over the standard error). The quantity of interest is the intercept: the predicted z-score for a study of zero precision. A study with an infinitely large standard error should almost never reach |z| \ge 1.96, so without bias the intercept should be near zero. If the published small studies are mostly the lucky significant ones, low-precision studies have too many large z-scores and the intercept is pushed above zero.

In the therapy data the intercept is 4.11 (95% CI 2.35 to 5.88; t = 4.68, p < 0.001), clear evidence of asymmetry (Harrer et al. 2022). We get the same values from the printed effects and CIs (our arithmetic). That tells us there are small-study effects. It does not tell us they come from publication bias.

Two refinements matter in practice (Harrer et al. 2022):

  • Standardised mean differences. The standard error of an SMD is computed partly from the SMD itself, so effect and standard error are correlated even without any bias, and Egger’s test gives too many false positives (Pustejovsky & Rodgers 2019). The fix is a modified standard error that uses only the two group sizes. Re-run this way, the therapy data still show asymmetry (intercept 11.19, p = 0.0005).
  • Binary outcomes. Log odds ratios, risk ratios and proportions have the same problem. Peters’ test regresses the log effect on 1/n (the inverse of the total sample size), weights the studies by their event counts, and tests the slope rather than the intercept. In Harrer et al.’s depression-and-mortality meta-analysis it finds nothing (t = -0.08, p = 0.94).

Like any test, these need enough data to have power. The standard recommendation is to test for funnel asymmetry only with K \ge 10 studies (Sterne et al. 2011), and {meta} refuses to run the test with fewer unless overridden (Harrer et al. 2022).

Correcting for small-study effects

Knowing that bias may exist is less useful than knowing how big it is: whether it shifts the pooled effect a little or changes the conclusion. Since publication bias can’t be measured directly, the corrections below adjust for small-study effects as a proxy (Harrer et al. 2022). If the asymmetry really was caused by publication bias, the corrected estimate is closer to what the full body of evidence would show. If it wasn’t, the correction may be wrong.

Trim and fill

The best-known correction, Duval and Tweedie’s trim and fill (2000), imputes the “missing” studies until the funnel is symmetric (Harrer et al. 2022). It works in two steps:

  1. Trim. Find the studies on the crowded side of the funnel that have no counterpart on the other side (here, the small studies with large effects). Remove them and recompute the pooled effect, usually with a fixed-effect model. Repeat until the number to remove settles.
  2. Fill. Take that recomputed effect as the centre. For each trimmed study, add a mirror-image study on the other side: if the centre is 0.5 and a trimmed study has 0.8, add one at 0.2. Then pool all of them, observed and imputed, usually with a random-effects model.

In the therapy data the procedure adds eight studies, mirrored around a centre of about 0.33, with effects from about 0.05 down to −1.14. The corrected estimate is g = 0.34 (CI 0.10 to 0.58), against 0.58 uncorrected (Harrer et al. 2022). Our hand implementation reproduces the eight imputed effects and the corrected estimate to three decimals (figure, left).

The important caveat is heterogeneity (Harrer et al. 2022). When studies don’t share one true effect, even large studies can sit far from the average, and the procedure trims and fills them too, although they are unlikely to be missing because of publication bias. With I^2 = 63\% that is a real risk here, so Harrer et al. repeat the analysis without the two outliers identified in heterogeneity. That run adds six studies and gives almost the same answer (g = 0.34, CI 0.19 to 0.49). Note also what imputation does to heterogeneity: with the imputed studies, I^2 rises to 76%, because the filled studies sit far from the observed ones.

PET-PEESE

PET-PEESE (Stanley & Doucouliagos 2014) has become popular in psychology and education, where standardised mean differences are common (Harrer et al. 2022). It is a weighted regression of the effect size on its standard error, the same regression as Egger’s test arranged differently. This time the intercept is the quantity of interest, and it means something specific: the predicted effect of a study with a standard error of zero, one with no sampling error at all. Harrer et al. call it the limit effect. If small studies are inflated by bias, including the standard error as a predictor should absorb the inflation and leave the intercept as the corrected effect.

There are two versions. PET uses the standard error as the predictor. PEESE uses the squared standard error (the variance), on the view that inflation is concentrated in the smallest, least powerful studies. PET does better when the true effect is zero, and PEESE when it isn’t, so the combined rule picks between them (Harrer et al. 2022). If PET’s intercept is significantly above zero (one-sided, \alpha = 0.05, which in two-sided output means p < 0.1 with a positive estimate), report PEESE’s intercept. Otherwise report PET’s.

The therapy data show the method’s best-known weakness (Harrer et al. 2022). With the Pustejovsky–Rodgers standard errors, PET’s intercept is g = -1.35 and PEESE’s is -0.44 (our recomputation agrees). PET’s intercept is significant but negative, so the rule says to keep PET. Taken literally, the therapy would then be seriously harmful, although every one of the eighteen observed effects is positive. That is overcorrection (Carter et al. 2019). Harrer et al.’s advice is not to read such an intercept as a point estimate. Interpret it as “no effect after correction”, which amounts to setting the estimate to zero.

PET-PEESE also performs badly with fewer than about 20 studies or I^2 above 80% (Stanley 2017), and it can overestimate the effect when there is no bias at all (Harrer et al. 2022). Those conditions are common, so Harrer et al. don’t recommend it as the only correction. A technical point: the usual implementation is ordinary weighted least squares, which assumes a multiplicative error. Meta-regression software assumes an additive one, the meta-analytic standard, so it’s worth re-fitting PET-PEESE as a meta-regression as a sensitivity check.

Limit meta-analysis

Rücker and colleagues’ limit meta-analysis (2011) follows the same idea as PET-PEESE, but builds the small-study bias into the random-effects model itself (Harrer et al. 2022). It adds a bias term that grows with each study’s error, then asks what the expected effect becomes as the sampling error goes to zero, keeping the between-study heterogeneity \tau in the model. Studies are also shrunk towards the adjusted mean, more strongly the less precise they are. It is fitted by maximum likelihood with limitmeta() in the {metasens} package.

On the therapy data it agrees with PET-PEESE: adjusted g = -0.03 (CI −0.36 to 0.29), essentially zero, and the three small, large-effect studies shrink from 1.27–1.79 to 0.04–0.34 (Harrer et al. 2022). On the depression-and-mortality data, where Peters’ test found no asymmetry, the adjusted and unadjusted risk ratios are close (2.26 and 2.02). The method handles heterogeneity explicitly, which PET-PEESE does not, but its performance has been studied less.

p-curve: looking at the p-values instead

Small-study methods assume that publication depends on the effect size. Harrer et al. point out that the real filter is probably the p-value (Harrer et al. 2022): a result is considered publishable when p < 0.05 (“significant p, or no PhD”). To a small-study method, p = 0.049 and p = 0.051 are almost the same. To a researcher they can be the difference between a publication and the file drawer. p-curve (Simonsohn, Nelson & Simmons 2014) was developed in the replication crisis to work directly with that filter. It uses only the significant results and asks how their p-values are distributed.

The idea is a fact about p-values (Harrer et al. 2022):

  • No true effect: p-values are uniformly distributed, so among significant results p = 0.04 is as common as p = 0.01. The p-curve is flat. (That uniformity is why 5% of p-values fall below 0.05 under the null; see p-values.)
  • A true effect: very small p-values are more common than barely significant ones, so the curve is right-skewed. The skew grows with the true effect and the sample size, and it appears even in badly underpowered studies (n = 20, true effect 0.2, in Harrer et al.’s simulations).
  • p-hacking: researchers tweak an analysis until it just crosses 0.05 and then stop, so values just under 0.05 are over-represented. Widespread p-hacking makes the curve left-skewed.

So the shape of the significant p-values shows whether the literature has evidential value, a true effect behind it, as opposed to a pattern produced by selective reporting (Harrer et al. 2022). That is a different question from “is there publication bias”, and arguably the one that matters most.

The tests (Harrer et al. 2022). Right-skewness is tested two ways. A binomial test counts how many significant p-values fall below 0.025 versus between 0.025 and 0.05: with 7 of 8 below, p = 0.035. A continuous test converts each significant p to a pp-value, its probability given that it is significant (for continuous outcomes, p \times 20, so 0.023 becomes 0.46). Under a flat curve these are uniform, and Fisher’s method combines them. Flatness turns the logic round to test whether a non-significant skew test means “no effect” or just “not enough studies”. The null becomes a very small effect, one the studies would detect with only 33% power, and the test asks whether the curve is significantly flatter than that. Each test is also run on the half curve (p < 0.025 only), as a guard against ambitious p-hacking that pushes results well below 0.05: very small p-values are hard to reach by hacking without a real effect.

Simonsohn, Simmons and Nelson’s decision rules (Harrer et al. 2022): evidential value is present if the half-curve skew test has p < 0.05, or both the half- and full-curve skew tests have p < 0.1; it is absent or inadequate if the full-curve flatness test has p < 0.05, or the half-curve flatness and binomial tests both have p < 0.1. If neither holds, the result is a “no-no”: a real effect can’t be confirmed, and a small one can’t be ruled out. That is common when few studies are significant.

On the therapy data, with the two outliers removed (for a reason given below), 9 of 16 studies are significant and 8 of those have p < 0.025 (Harrer et al. 2022). All three skew tests are significant (binomial p = 0.020, full curve p < 0.001, half curve p = 0.003), and none of the flatness tests is. Evidential value is present. This contradicts PET-PEESE and the limit meta-analysis, which both put the corrected effect near zero.

p-curve can also estimate the effect (Harrer et al. 2022). Given a study’s sample size, each candidate true effect implies a non-central t-distribution, and so a set of pp-values. At the right effect size those pp-values are uniform. The method tries many candidates and keeps the one whose pp-values are closest to uniform, measured by the Kolmogorov–Smirnov distance D. The search should produce a clean V-shape; an erratic curve means the minimum may not have been found. For the therapy data the estimate is d = 0.39, a little below the outlier-free pooled g = 0.45.

Why remove the outliers? p-curve assumes little heterogeneity, and its effect estimate is unreliable when heterogeneity is high. Van Aert and colleagues (2016) suggest using it only when I^2 \le 50\% (Harrer et al. 2022). Note also that p-curve estimates the true effect behind the significant studies, not behind every study in the meta-analysis.

Selection models: writing the filter down

Each method so far has a built-in theory of how studies go missing. Selection models generalise this by making the theory explicit (Harrer et al. 2022). They combine two pieces:

  • an effect-size model, the usual random-effects model, which says how likely each observed effect is without selection; and
  • a weight function w(p), which says how likely a study is to be published given its p-value.

Multiplying the two and renormalising gives the distribution of published effects. Fitting that by maximum likelihood estimates the corrected mean \mu and \tau^2, along with the selection weights (Harrer et al. 2022). The usual weight function is a step function: choose cut-points on the (one-sided) p-value scale, and give every study between two cut-points the same relative chance of publication. The first interval is the reference (weight 1), and a weight of 0.5 means “half as likely to be published”. A likelihood-ratio test checks whether the weights differ at all, though it is known to be anti-conservative. Every extra cut-point adds a weight to estimate, so models with many steps need about 100 studies or more.

The three-parameter selection model (3PSM; McShane, Böckenholt & Hansen 2016) has one cut-point, at a one-sided p of 0.025 (two-sided 0.05), so it estimates only \mu, \tau^2 and the relative chance that a non-significant result is published (Harrer et al. 2022). It works with as few as 15 to 20 studies. On the therapy data it gives g = 0.59 (CI 0.34 to 0.84), essentially the uncorrected 0.58, and the estimated weight for non-significant studies is 1.15, slightly above that for significant ones (LRT p = 0.85): no sign of selection. Moving the cut-point to 0.05 (treating two-sided p up to 0.10 as publishable) changes the picture. The estimate falls to 0.37 (CI 0.02 to 0.71), the weight for clearly non-significant results drops to 0.18, and the test is significant (p = 0.046). That is consistent with clearly null studies being missing (Harrer et al. 2022).

With too few studies to estimate the weights, you can fix them instead and ask what the effect would be if a given selection process were true. Using Vevea and Woods’s (2005) illustrative weights for moderate and severe selection over thirteen cut-points, the therapy estimate becomes 0.52 and 0.46 (Harrer et al. 2022). This is a sensitivity analysis, not an estimate: it is only right if the assumed selection process is right.

Which method should you use?

Several, and then compare them. Harrer et al. are blunt: many simulation studies have compared these methods and no method consistently beats the others (Carter et al. 2019) (Harrer et al. 2022). It is common for them to disagree widely, and the therapy data show it (figure, right). On one dataset the corrected effect ranges from practically zero (PET-PEESE, limit meta-analysis) to 0.59 (3PSM), with trim and fill, p-curve and the other selection models in between. One reading is “the effect disappears once small-study effects are removed”, another is “no selection detected”, and both come from the same eighteen studies.

So treat the methods as a range of plausible corrected effects (Harrer et al. 2022). If PET-PEESE, p-curve and a selection model all land near the uncorrected estimate, the finding is robust. If they scatter, as here, the honest conclusion is that the effect of publication bias is uncertain, and so is how far to trust the pooled effect. Harrer et al.’s summary of the trade-offs:

Method Strengths Weaknesses
Trim and fill Very widely used and widely understood Often under-corrects (misses a true zero); unreliable with high heterogeneity
PET-PEESE Simple model, easy to run and explain Can badly over- or under-correct; weak with few or small studies and high heterogeneity
Limit meta-analysis Like PET-PEESE but models heterogeneity Less studied; can fail with K < 10 and high heterogeneity
p-curve Beats trim and fill when its assumptions hold Assumes little heterogeneity; needs enough significant results; harder to explain
Selection models Can encode any selection story; 3PSM does well in simulations Only as good as the assumed selection process; need background knowledge

Two more points from Harrer et al. No method gives acceptable results when heterogeneity is high (I^2 around 75%; van Aert et al. 2016). Analysing without outliers, or within more homogeneous subgroups, helps in practice but doesn’t solve the problem (Harrer et al. 2022). And these analyses have caused real disputes, for instance over ego depletion (Friese et al. 2019). Harrer et al. call every statistical “proof” of publication bias “weak at best”. The best protection is a thorough search for unpublished evidence and changes to publication practice, such as preregistration and registered reports (Harrer et al. 2022).

Common pitfalls

WarningWhere this goes wrong
  • “The funnel is asymmetric, so there is publication bias.” Asymmetry shows small-study effects. Heterogeneity, better fidelity in small trials, lower quality and chance can all produce it. (Harrer et al. 2022)
  • “Egger’s test is not significant, so there is no bias.” With few studies it has little power. Don’t run it below K = 10, and don’t read silence as a clean result. (Harrer et al. 2022)
  • Running Egger’s test on SMDs or odds ratios with their usual standard errors. Effect and SE are correlated by construction, which inflates false positives. Use the Pustejovsky–Rodgers SE, or Peters’ test for binary outcomes. (Harrer et al. 2022)
  • Reporting the trim-and-fill estimate as “the true effect”. It corrects for one assumed mechanism, often too little, and it trims genuine studies when heterogeneity is high. (Harrer et al. 2022)
  • Taking a negative PET intercept literally. When every observed effect is positive, an intercept of −1.35 is overcorrection. Read it as “no effect after correction”. (Harrer et al. 2022)
  • Applying p-curve to heterogeneous data. Its estimate is unreliable above about I^2 = 50\%, and it describes the significant studies only. (Harrer et al. 2022)
  • Trusting one selection model. The corrected estimate is only right if the weight function is. Vary the cut-point (0.025 vs 0.05 changed 0.59 to 0.37 here). (Harrer et al. 2022)
  • Running one method and reporting it alone. No method consistently wins, so use several and report the range they give. (Harrer et al. 2022) Choosing among them after seeing the results would be the garden of forking paths again, so decide which to run in advance.
  • Using statistics instead of searching. Grey literature, registries and author contact do more than any correction. (Harrer et al. 2022)

Appendix · the mathematics

Egger’s test (Harrer et al. 2022). With observed effects \hat\theta_k and standard errors SE_k, fit by ordinary least squares

\frac{\hat\theta_k}{SE_k} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \frac{1}{SE_k},

and test H_0\colon \beta_0 = 0 with a t-test on K-2 degrees of freedom.

In plain words: the intercept is the z-score expected from a study with no precision at all, which should be about zero unless small studies are systematically “lucky”.

Multiply through by SE_k and the same model reads \hat\theta_k = \beta_1 + \beta_0 SE_k with weights 1/SE_k^2. That is PET with the roles swapped: Egger’s intercept is PET’s slope (11.19 in both regressions with the modified SE), and Egger’s slope is PET’s intercept (−1.35).

Pustejovsky–Rodgers standard error for a between-group SMD, which drops the term containing the SMD:

SE^*_{\text{SMD}} = \sqrt{\frac{n_1+n_2}{n_1 n_2}}.

Peters’ test for binary outcomes: weighted regression of \log\psi_k on 1/n_k with weights w_k = \big(\tfrac{1}{a_k+c_k} + \tfrac{1}{b_k+d_k}\big)^{-1} (a, c events and b, d non-events in the treatment and control groups); asymmetry is tested on the slope \beta_1.

PET-PEESE (Harrer et al. 2022). Weighted least squares with w_k = 1/s_k^2:

\text{PET: } \hat\theta_k = \beta_0 + \beta_1 SE_k, \qquad \text{PEESE: } \hat\theta_k = \beta_0 + \beta_1 SE_k^2,

\hat\theta_{\text{PET-PEESE}} = \begin{cases}\hat\beta_{0,\text{PEESE}} & \text{if } p(\beta_{0,\text{PET}} = 0) < 0.1 \text{ and } \hat\beta_{0,\text{PET}} > 0,\\ \hat\beta_{0,\text{PET}} & \text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

In plain words: extrapolate the effect–precision line to a perfectly precise study. Use the straight line unless it already shows an effect, then use the curved one.

Limit meta-analysis (Harrer et al. 2022). The random-effects model \hat\theta_k = \mu + \epsilon_k + \zeta_k is extended to \hat\theta_k = \mu_* + \theta_{\text{Bias}}(\epsilon_k + \zeta_k). Letting \epsilon_k \to 0 gives the adjusted effect \hat\theta_* = \mu_* + \theta_{\text{Bias}}\tau, with shrunken study estimates

\hat\theta_{*k} = \mu_* + \sqrt{\frac{\tau^2}{SE_k^2 + \tau^2}}\,(\hat\theta_k - \mu_*).

The shrinkage factor is the same “signal share” as in hierarchical Bayesian models, here as a square root.

p-curve (Harrer et al. 2022). For a significant result with two-sided p < 0.05, pp = 20p (continuous outcomes) is uniform on [0, 1] under no effect, and Fisher’s method gives

\chi^2_{2K} = -2\sum_{k=1}^{K}\log(pp_k).

Harrer et al.’s example, p = 0.001, 0.002, 0.003, 0.004, 0.03, gives pp = 0.02, \dots, 0.60 and \chi^2_{10} = 25.96. The printed code then evaluates the tail probability at 26.96, giving 0.0026; at 25.96 it is 0.0038 (our arithmetic; for even df, P(\chi^2_{2m} > x) = e^{-x/2}\sum_{j<m}(x/2)^j/j!). The conclusion is unchanged. The binomial check: P(X \ge 7 \mid n = 8, \tfrac12) = 9/256 = 0.035. For effect estimation, a candidate effect \theta gives the non-centrality \delta = \theta\big/\sqrt{(n_1+n_2)/(n_1 n_2)} and pp(t_k) = P(t > t_k \mid \delta, \text{df}, p < 0.05); the estimate minimises the Kolmogorov–Smirnov distance between these pp-values and the uniform.

Selection models (Harrer et al. 2022). With the random-effects density f(x_k) and weight function w(p_k), the density of published effects is

f^*(x_k) = \frac{w(p_k)\,f(x_k)}{\int w(p_k)\,f(x_k)\,dx_k},

with w a step function: w(p) = \omega_i for a_{i-1} < p \le a_i, \omega_1 = 1. The 3PSM has one cut-point a_1 = 0.025 and estimates (\mu, \tau^2, \omega_2) jointly by maximum likelihood.

In plain words: the published studies are the random-effects distribution reweighted by their chance of publication; fit the reweighted distribution, and \mu is what the unfiltered literature would average.

## Why it matters

By the 1990s it was taken as settled that antidepressants such as SSRIs beat placebo, and most of that
evidence came from meta-analyses of published trials. In *The Emperor's New Drugs* (2002), Irving Kirsch and
colleagues used the US Freedom of Information Act to get the trial data that drug companies had submitted to
the FDA, including trials that were never published. With the unpublished trials added, the advantage over
placebo was at best minimal [@harrer_doing_meta]. Kirsch argued that companies had published the favourable
trials and held back the disappointing ones. The claim is still disputed, and Harrer et al. use it without
taking sides, as an illustration of how much the missing studies can matter [@harrer_doing_meta].

Their analogy is an iceberg. If you can only see the tip, you can measure it perfectly and still get its
size wrong [@harrer_doing_meta]. Every model in [pooling studies](pooling-effect-sizes.qmd) estimates the
average effect of the studies it is given. If the studies it is given are a biased selection, the
estimate inherits the bias, however careful the statistics. The consequences Harrer et al. list are
overestimated treatment effects, missed side-effects, and continued belief in theories that are wrong.

The [gateway entry](meta-analysis.qmd) introduces the funnel plot and the file drawer. This entry covers
what to do about them: how to look for the bias, the main ways of correcting for it, and why the results of
those corrections need careful reading.

## What counts as publication bias

**Publication bias** exists when a study's chance of being published depends on its results
[@harrer_doing_meta]. There is good evidence that significant results, and results that confirm the
hypothesis, are more likely to be published. It belongs to a wider family of **non-reporting biases**, each
of which keeps some results out of a meta-analysis [@harrer_doing_meta]:

- **Citation bias**: negative or inconclusive studies are cited less, so reference-list searches miss them.
- **Time-lag bias**: positive results come out sooner, so a recent literature over-represents them.
- **Multiple-publication bias**: successful studies are reported in several articles ("salami slicing"),
  which makes them easier to find.
- **Language bias**: studies not published in English are harder to find, and may differ systematically
  from those that are.
- **Outcome-reporting bias**: a study measures several outcomes and reports the ones that worked. The paper
  is published, but the unfavourable result is still missing.

Even a complete set of studies can be biased by
[questionable research practices](questionable-research-practices.qmd) inside them
[@harrer_doing_meta]. The two Harrer et al. name are **$p$-hacking** (adjusting outlier rules, subgroups or
missing-data handling until $p < 0.05$) and **HARKing**, hypothesising after the results are known:
presenting an exploratory finding as if it had been predicted, or quietly dropping hypotheses the data did
not support. HARKing inflates the false-discovery rate and, in its second form, produces outcome-reporting
bias.

There are two lines of defence [@harrer_doing_meta]. The first is the **search**. Look for grey
literature (dissertations, preprints, government reports, conference proceedings), search trial registries
such as the ICTRP and OSF Registries for studies that were registered but never reported, and ask authors
for unpublished data. One large study found that including grey and unpublished literature helps prevent
overestimated effects (McAuley et al. 2000). The second line is **statistical**, and the rest of this entry
is about it. None of these methods detects publication bias directly. Each looks for a pattern in the data
that publication bias would produce.

## Small-study effects: the theory behind the funnel

The oldest family of methods looks for **small-study effects**, and it rests on a simple story about which
studies go missing (Harrer et al., following Borenstein et al.) [@harrer_doing_meta]:

- **Large studies** cost so much time and money that they get published whatever they find.
- **Medium-sized studies** risk non-publication, but they usually have enough power to reach significance,
  so only some of them disappear.
- **Small studies** are most likely to end up in the file drawer. They only reach significance if their
  effect is very large, so the small studies that *are* published are the ones with very large effects.

The mechanism is "only significant results get published". Because significance is easier to reach in large
samples, the damage falls mainly on small studies. In a meta-analysis this produces a **relationship
between a study's precision and its effect size**, which shouldn't otherwise exist.

### The funnel plot

A **funnel plot** shows each study's effect size (horizontal) against its standard error (vertical, with
the axis reversed so the most precise studies are at the top) [@harrer_doing_meta]. Without bias, the
points form a symmetric, upside-down funnel: precise studies cluster near the pooled effect, and imprecise
ones spread out evenly to both sides, because a large standard error means the observed effect can land far
from the true one in either direction.

In Harrer et al.'s eighteen trials of third-wave psychotherapies (the running example in
[pooling](pooling-effect-sizes.qmd) and [heterogeneity](between-study-heterogeneity.qmd)), the funnel is
lopsided [@harrer_doing_meta]. Three small studies (Shapiro et al., Kang et al., DanitzOrsillo) sit in
the bottom-right corner with very large effects, and nothing balances them in the bottom left: there are
no small studies with small or negative effects. The most precise study, de Vibe et al., also sits well
below the pooled effect.

A **contour-enhanced funnel plot** (Peters et al. 2008) adds shaded regions showing where a study would be
significant at $p < 0.1$, $0.05$ and $0.01$ [@harrer_doing_meta]. That links the asymmetry to
significance, which is what the publication-bias story is about. In the therapy data, all three small,
large-effect studies are significant despite their large standard errors. Imputed studies that would
make the funnel symmetric would fall in the non-significant region, or show significant *harm*. Among the
larger studies, several are not significant at 0.05, but almost all of them sit just inside the
$0.05 < p < 0.1$ band. Harrer et al. suggest two explanations: the original papers may have computed the
effect a different way that was significant, or significance "at trend level" was enough to get published
[@harrer_doing_meta].

Asymmetry is a warning sign, not proof. Harrer et al., after Page et al. (2020), list benign causes of the
same pattern [@harrer_doing_meta]:

- **Heterogeneity.** The funnel assumes the scatter is sampling error around one effect. If small studies
  really do estimate different true effects, the funnel can be asymmetric without anything missing.
- **Different procedures in small studies.** Treatment fidelity is easier to maintain in a small trial, so
  small studies may genuinely find larger effects.
- **Quality.** Low-quality studies tend to show larger effects, and small studies are more often
  low-quality.
- **Chance.**

### Egger's regression test

There is no rule for when a funnel is "too asymmetric", so **Egger's regression test** puts a number on it
[@harrer_doing_meta]. Each study's $z$-score (effect divided by its standard error) is regressed on its
precision (one over the standard error). The quantity of interest is the **intercept**: the predicted
$z$-score for a study of *zero* precision. A study with an infinitely large standard error should almost
never reach $|z| \ge 1.96$, so without bias the intercept should be near zero. If the published small
studies are mostly the lucky significant ones, low-precision studies have too many large $z$-scores and
the intercept is pushed above zero.

In the therapy data the intercept is **4.11** (95% CI 2.35 to 5.88; $t = 4.68$, $p < 0.001$), clear evidence
of asymmetry [@harrer_doing_meta]. We get the same values from the printed effects and CIs (our arithmetic).
That tells us there are small-study effects. It does not tell us they come from publication bias.

Two refinements matter in practice [@harrer_doing_meta]:

- **Standardised mean differences.** The standard error of an SMD is computed partly from the SMD itself,
  so effect and standard error are correlated even without any bias, and Egger's test gives too many false
  positives (Pustejovsky & Rodgers 2019). The fix is a modified standard error that uses only the two group
  sizes. Re-run this way, the therapy data still show asymmetry (intercept 11.19, $p = 0.0005$).
- **Binary outcomes.** Log odds ratios, risk ratios and proportions have the same problem. **Peters' test**
  regresses the log effect on $1/n$ (the inverse of the total sample size), weights the studies by their
  event counts, and tests the *slope* rather than the intercept. In Harrer et al.'s depression-and-mortality
  meta-analysis it finds nothing ($t = -0.08$, $p = 0.94$).

Like any test, these need enough data to have power. The standard recommendation is to test for
funnel asymmetry only with **$K \ge 10$ studies** (Sterne et al. 2011), and `{meta}` refuses to run the test
with fewer unless overridden [@harrer_doing_meta].

## Correcting for small-study effects

Knowing that bias may exist is less useful than knowing how big it is: whether it shifts the pooled effect
a little or changes the conclusion. Since publication bias can't be measured directly, the corrections below
adjust for **small-study effects** as a proxy [@harrer_doing_meta]. If the asymmetry really was caused by
publication bias, the corrected estimate is closer to what the full body of evidence would show. If it
wasn't, the correction may be wrong.

### Trim and fill

The best-known correction, Duval and Tweedie's **trim and fill** (2000), imputes the "missing" studies until
the funnel is symmetric [@harrer_doing_meta]. It works in two steps:

1. **Trim.** Find the studies on the crowded side of the funnel that have no counterpart on the other side
   (here, the small studies with large effects). Remove them and recompute the pooled effect, usually with a
   fixed-effect model. Repeat until the number to remove settles.
2. **Fill.** Take that recomputed effect as the centre. For each trimmed study, add a mirror-image study on
   the other side: if the centre is 0.5 and a trimmed study has 0.8, add one at 0.2. Then pool all of them,
   observed and imputed, usually with a random-effects model.

In the therapy data the procedure adds **eight** studies, mirrored around a centre of about 0.33, with
effects from about 0.05 down to −1.14. The corrected estimate is **$g = 0.34$** (CI 0.10 to 0.58), against
0.58 uncorrected [@harrer_doing_meta]. Our hand implementation reproduces the eight imputed effects and
the corrected estimate to three decimals (figure, left).

The important caveat is **heterogeneity** [@harrer_doing_meta]. When studies don't share one true effect,
even large studies can sit far from the average, and the procedure trims and fills them too, although
they are unlikely to be missing because of publication bias. With $I^2 = 63\%$ that is a real risk here,
so Harrer et al. repeat the analysis without the two outliers identified in
[heterogeneity](between-study-heterogeneity.qmd). That run adds six studies and gives almost the same answer
($g = 0.34$, CI 0.19 to 0.49). Note also what imputation does to heterogeneity: with the imputed studies,
$I^2$ rises to 76%, because the filled studies sit far from the observed ones.

### PET-PEESE

**PET-PEESE** (Stanley & Doucouliagos 2014) has become popular in psychology and education, where
standardised mean differences are common [@harrer_doing_meta]. It is a weighted regression of the effect
size on its standard error, the same regression as Egger's test arranged differently. This time the
**intercept** is the quantity of interest, and it means something specific: the predicted effect of a
study with a standard error of **zero**, one with no sampling error at all. Harrer et al. call it the
**limit effect**. If small studies are inflated by bias, including the standard error as a predictor should
absorb the inflation and leave the intercept as the corrected effect.

There are two versions. **PET** uses the standard error as the predictor. **PEESE** uses the squared standard
error (the variance), on the view that inflation is concentrated in the smallest, least powerful studies.
PET does better when the true effect is zero, and PEESE when it isn't, so the combined rule picks between
them [@harrer_doing_meta]. If PET's intercept is significantly *above* zero (one-sided, $\alpha = 0.05$, which
in two-sided output means $p < 0.1$ with a positive estimate), report PEESE's intercept. Otherwise report
PET's.

The therapy data show the method's best-known weakness [@harrer_doing_meta]. With the Pustejovsky–Rodgers
standard errors, PET's intercept is **$g = -1.35$** and PEESE's is $-0.44$ (our recomputation agrees). PET's
intercept is significant but *negative*, so the rule says to keep PET. Taken literally, the therapy
would then be seriously harmful, although every one of the eighteen observed effects is positive. That is
**overcorrection** (Carter et al. 2019). Harrer et al.'s advice is not to read such an intercept as a point
estimate. Interpret it as "no effect after correction", which amounts to setting the estimate to zero.

PET-PEESE also performs badly with **fewer than about 20 studies** or **$I^2$ above 80%** (Stanley 2017),
and it can *over*estimate the effect when there is no bias at all [@harrer_doing_meta]. Those conditions are
common, so Harrer et al. don't recommend it as the only correction. A technical point: the usual
implementation is ordinary weighted least squares, which assumes a *multiplicative* error. Meta-regression
software assumes an *additive* one, the meta-analytic standard, so it's worth re-fitting PET-PEESE as a
meta-regression as a sensitivity check.

### Limit meta-analysis

Rücker and colleagues' **limit meta-analysis** (2011) follows the same idea as PET-PEESE, but builds the
small-study bias into the random-effects model itself [@harrer_doing_meta]. It adds a bias term that grows
with each study's error, then asks what the expected effect becomes as the sampling error goes to zero,
keeping the between-study heterogeneity $\tau$ in the model. Studies are also **shrunk** towards the
adjusted mean, more strongly the less precise they are. It is fitted by maximum likelihood with
`limitmeta()` in the `{metasens}` package.

On the therapy data it agrees with PET-PEESE: adjusted **$g = -0.03$** (CI −0.36 to 0.29), essentially zero,
and the three small, large-effect studies shrink from 1.27–1.79 to 0.04–0.34 [@harrer_doing_meta]. On the
depression-and-mortality data, where Peters' test found no asymmetry, the adjusted and unadjusted risk ratios
are close (2.26 and 2.02). The method handles heterogeneity explicitly, which PET-PEESE does not, but its
performance has been studied less.

## p-curve: looking at the $p$-values instead

Small-study methods assume that publication depends on the **effect size**. Harrer et al. point out that the
real filter is probably the **$p$-value** [@harrer_doing_meta]: a result is considered publishable when
$p < 0.05$ ("significant $p$, or no PhD"). To a small-study method, $p = 0.049$ and $p = 0.051$ are almost
the same. To a researcher they can be the difference between a publication and the file drawer.
**p-curve** (Simonsohn, Nelson & Simmons 2014) was developed in the replication crisis to work directly with
that filter. It uses only the **significant** results and asks how their $p$-values are distributed.

The idea is a fact about $p$-values [@harrer_doing_meta]:

- **No true effect:** $p$-values are **uniformly** distributed, so among significant results $p = 0.04$ is
  as common as $p = 0.01$. The $p$-curve is **flat**. (That uniformity is why 5% of $p$-values fall below
  0.05 under the null; see [$p$-values](../02-inference/hypothesis-testing.qmd).)
- **A true effect:** very small $p$-values are more common than barely significant ones, so the curve is
  **right-skewed**. The skew grows with the true effect and the sample size, and it appears even in badly
  underpowered studies ($n = 20$, true effect 0.2, in Harrer et al.'s simulations).
- **$p$-hacking:** researchers tweak an analysis until it just crosses 0.05 and then stop, so values just
  under 0.05 are over-represented. Widespread $p$-hacking makes the curve **left-skewed**.

So the shape of the significant $p$-values shows whether the literature has **evidential value**, a true
effect behind it, as opposed to a pattern produced by selective reporting [@harrer_doing_meta]. That is a
different question from "is there publication bias", and arguably the one that matters most.

**The tests** [@harrer_doing_meta]. *Right-skewness* is tested two ways. A **binomial test** counts how many
significant $p$-values fall below 0.025 versus between 0.025 and 0.05: with 7 of 8 below, $p = 0.035$. A
continuous test converts each significant $p$ to a **$pp$-value**, its probability *given* that it is
significant (for continuous outcomes, $p \times 20$, so 0.023 becomes 0.46). Under a flat curve these are
uniform, and **Fisher's method** combines them. *Flatness* turns the logic round to test whether a
non-significant skew test means "no effect" or just "not enough studies". The null becomes a very small
effect, one the studies would detect with only **33% power**, and the test asks whether the curve is
significantly flatter than that. Each test is also run on the **half curve** ($p < 0.025$ only), as a guard
against ambitious $p$-hacking that pushes results well below 0.05: very small $p$-values are hard to
reach by hacking without a real effect.

Simonsohn, Simmons and Nelson's decision rules [@harrer_doing_meta]: **evidential value is present** if the
half-curve skew test has $p < 0.05$, or both the half- and full-curve skew tests have $p < 0.1$; it is
**absent or inadequate** if the full-curve flatness test has $p < 0.05$, or the half-curve flatness and
binomial tests both have $p < 0.1$. If neither holds, the result is a "no-no": a real effect can't be
confirmed, and a small one can't be ruled out. That is common when few studies are significant.

**On the therapy data**, with the two outliers removed (for a reason given below), 9 of 16 studies are
significant and 8 of those have $p < 0.025$ [@harrer_doing_meta]. All three skew tests are significant
(binomial $p = 0.020$, full curve $p < 0.001$, half curve $p = 0.003$), and none of the flatness tests is.
**Evidential value is present.** This contradicts PET-PEESE and the limit meta-analysis, which both put the
corrected effect near zero.

p-curve can also **estimate the effect** [@harrer_doing_meta]. Given a study's sample size, each candidate
true effect implies a non-central $t$-distribution, and so a set of $pp$-values. At the *right* effect
size those $pp$-values are uniform. The method tries many candidates and keeps the one whose $pp$-values are
closest to uniform, measured by the Kolmogorov–Smirnov distance $D$. The search should produce a clean
V-shape; an erratic curve means the minimum may not have been found. For the therapy data the estimate is
**$d = 0.39$**, a little below the outlier-free pooled $g = 0.45$.

Why remove the outliers? p-curve assumes **little heterogeneity**, and its effect estimate is unreliable when
heterogeneity is high. Van Aert and colleagues (2016) suggest using it only when $I^2 \le 50\%$
[@harrer_doing_meta]. Note also that p-curve estimates the true effect behind the *significant* studies, not
behind every study in the meta-analysis.

## Selection models: writing the filter down

Each method so far has a built-in theory of how studies go missing. **Selection models** generalise this by
making the theory explicit [@harrer_doing_meta]. They combine two pieces:

- an **effect-size model**, the usual random-effects model, which says how likely each observed effect is
  *without* selection; and
- a **weight function** $w(p)$, which says how likely a study is to be published given its $p$-value.

Multiplying the two and renormalising gives the distribution of *published* effects. Fitting that by maximum
likelihood estimates the corrected mean $\mu$ and $\tau^2$, along with the selection weights
[@harrer_doing_meta]. The usual weight function is a **step function**: choose cut-points on the
(one-sided) $p$-value scale, and give every study between two cut-points the same relative chance of
publication. The first interval is the reference (weight 1), and a weight of 0.5 means "half as likely to
be published". A likelihood-ratio test checks whether the weights differ at all, though it is known to be
anti-conservative. Every extra cut-point adds a weight to estimate, so models with many steps need **about
100 studies or more**.

The **three-parameter selection model** (3PSM; McShane, Böckenholt & Hansen 2016) has one cut-point, at a
one-sided $p$ of 0.025 (two-sided 0.05), so it estimates only $\mu$, $\tau^2$ and the relative chance that a
non-significant result is published [@harrer_doing_meta]. It works with as few as 15 to 20 studies. On the
therapy data it gives **$g = 0.59$** (CI 0.34 to 0.84), essentially the uncorrected 0.58, and the estimated
weight for non-significant studies is 1.15, slightly *above* that for significant ones (LRT $p = 0.85$): no
sign of selection. Moving the cut-point to 0.05 (treating two-sided $p$ up to 0.10 as publishable) changes
the picture. The estimate falls to **0.37** (CI 0.02 to 0.71), the weight for clearly non-significant
results drops to 0.18, and the test is significant ($p = 0.046$). That is consistent with clearly null
studies being missing [@harrer_doing_meta].

With too few studies to estimate the weights, you can **fix** them instead and ask what the effect would be
if a given selection process were true. Using Vevea and Woods's (2005) illustrative weights for **moderate**
and **severe** selection over thirteen cut-points, the therapy estimate becomes 0.52 and 0.46
[@harrer_doing_meta]. This is a sensitivity analysis, not an estimate: it is only right if the assumed
selection process is right.

## Which method should you use?

Several, and then compare them. Harrer et al. are blunt: many simulation studies have compared these
methods and **no method consistently beats the others** (Carter et al. 2019) [@harrer_doing_meta]. It is
common for them to disagree widely, and the therapy data show it (figure, right). On one dataset the
corrected effect ranges from practically zero (PET-PEESE, limit meta-analysis) to 0.59 (3PSM), with trim
and fill, p-curve and the other selection models in between. One reading is "the effect disappears once
small-study effects are removed", another is "no selection detected", and both come from the same eighteen
studies.

So treat the methods as a **range of plausible corrected effects** [@harrer_doing_meta]. If PET-PEESE,
p-curve and a selection model all land near the uncorrected estimate, the finding is robust. If they
scatter, as here, the honest conclusion is that the effect of publication bias is uncertain, and so is how
far to trust the pooled effect. Harrer et al.'s summary of the trade-offs:

| Method | Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|---|
| Trim and fill | Very widely used and widely understood | Often under-corrects (misses a true zero); unreliable with high heterogeneity |
| PET-PEESE | Simple model, easy to run and explain | Can badly over- or under-correct; weak with few or small studies and high heterogeneity |
| Limit meta-analysis | Like PET-PEESE but models heterogeneity | Less studied; can fail with $K < 10$ and high heterogeneity |
| p-curve | Beats trim and fill when its assumptions hold | Assumes little heterogeneity; needs enough significant results; harder to explain |
| Selection models | Can encode any selection story; 3PSM does well in simulations | Only as good as the assumed selection process; need background knowledge |

Two more points from Harrer et al. **No method gives acceptable results when heterogeneity is high**
($I^2$ around 75%; van Aert et al. 2016). Analysing without outliers, or within more homogeneous subgroups,
helps in practice but doesn't solve the problem [@harrer_doing_meta]. And these analyses have caused real
disputes, for instance over **ego depletion** (Friese et al. 2019). Harrer et al. call every statistical
"proof" of publication bias "weak at best". The best protection is a thorough search for unpublished
evidence and changes to publication practice, such as
[preregistration and registered reports](open-and-reproducible-science.qmd) [@harrer_doing_meta].

## Common pitfalls

::: {.callout-warning title="Where this goes wrong"}
- **"The funnel is asymmetric, so there is publication bias."** Asymmetry shows small-study effects.
  Heterogeneity, better fidelity in small trials, lower quality and chance can all produce it.
  [@harrer_doing_meta]
- **"Egger's test is not significant, so there is no bias."** With few studies it has little power. Don't
  run it below $K = 10$, and don't read silence as a clean result. [@harrer_doing_meta]
- **Running Egger's test on SMDs or odds ratios with their usual standard errors.** Effect and SE are
  correlated by construction, which inflates false positives. Use the Pustejovsky–Rodgers SE, or
  Peters' test for binary outcomes. [@harrer_doing_meta]
- **Reporting the trim-and-fill estimate as "the true effect".** It corrects for one assumed mechanism,
  often too little, and it trims genuine studies when heterogeneity is high. [@harrer_doing_meta]
- **Taking a negative PET intercept literally.** When every observed effect is positive, an intercept
  of −1.35 is overcorrection. Read it as "no effect after correction". [@harrer_doing_meta]
- **Applying p-curve to heterogeneous data.** Its estimate is unreliable above about $I^2 = 50\%$, and it
  describes the significant studies only. [@harrer_doing_meta]
- **Trusting one selection model.** The corrected estimate is only right if the weight function is. Vary
  the cut-point (0.025 vs 0.05 changed 0.59 to 0.37 here). [@harrer_doing_meta]
- **Running one method and reporting it alone.** No method consistently wins, so use several and report
  the range they give. [@harrer_doing_meta] Choosing among them after seeing the results would be the
  [garden of forking paths](multiple-testing.qmd) again, so decide which to run in advance.
- **Using statistics instead of searching.** Grey literature, registries and author contact do more than
  any correction. [@harrer_doing_meta]
:::

## Appendix · the mathematics {.unnumbered}

::: {.math-appendix}

**Egger's test** [@harrer_doing_meta]. With observed effects $\hat\theta_k$ and standard errors
$SE_k$, fit by ordinary least squares

$$\frac{\hat\theta_k}{SE_k} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \frac{1}{SE_k},$$

and test $H_0\colon \beta_0 = 0$ with a $t$-test on $K-2$ degrees of freedom.

> **In plain words:** the intercept is the $z$-score expected from a study with no precision at all, which
> should be about zero unless small studies are systematically "lucky".

Multiply through by $SE_k$ and the same model reads $\hat\theta_k = \beta_1 + \beta_0 SE_k$ with weights
$1/SE_k^2$. That is PET with the roles swapped: Egger's intercept is PET's slope (11.19 in both regressions
with the modified SE), and Egger's slope is PET's intercept (−1.35).

**Pustejovsky–Rodgers standard error** for a between-group SMD, which drops the term containing the SMD:

$$SE^*_{\text{SMD}} = \sqrt{\frac{n_1+n_2}{n_1 n_2}}.$$

**Peters' test** for binary outcomes: weighted regression of $\log\psi_k$ on $1/n_k$ with weights
$w_k = \big(\tfrac{1}{a_k+c_k} + \tfrac{1}{b_k+d_k}\big)^{-1}$ ($a, c$ events and $b, d$ non-events in the
treatment and control groups); asymmetry is tested on the **slope** $\beta_1$.

**PET-PEESE** [@harrer_doing_meta]. Weighted least squares with $w_k = 1/s_k^2$:

$$\text{PET: } \hat\theta_k = \beta_0 + \beta_1 SE_k, \qquad \text{PEESE: } \hat\theta_k = \beta_0 + \beta_1 SE_k^2,$$

$$\hat\theta_{\text{PET-PEESE}} = \begin{cases}\hat\beta_{0,\text{PEESE}} & \text{if } p(\beta_{0,\text{PET}} = 0) < 0.1 \text{ and } \hat\beta_{0,\text{PET}} > 0,\\ \hat\beta_{0,\text{PET}} & \text{otherwise.}\end{cases}$$

> **In plain words:** extrapolate the effect–precision line to a perfectly precise study. Use the straight
> line unless it already shows an effect, then use the curved one.

**Limit meta-analysis** [@harrer_doing_meta]. The random-effects model $\hat\theta_k = \mu + \epsilon_k + \zeta_k$
is extended to $\hat\theta_k = \mu_* + \theta_{\text{Bias}}(\epsilon_k + \zeta_k)$. Letting $\epsilon_k \to 0$
gives the adjusted effect $\hat\theta_* = \mu_* + \theta_{\text{Bias}}\tau$, with shrunken study estimates

$$\hat\theta_{*k} = \mu_* + \sqrt{\frac{\tau^2}{SE_k^2 + \tau^2}}\,(\hat\theta_k - \mu_*).$$

The shrinkage factor is the same "signal share" as in [hierarchical Bayesian
models](../02-inference/hierarchical-bayesian-models.qmd), here as a square root.

**p-curve** [@harrer_doing_meta]. For a significant result with two-sided $p < 0.05$, $pp = 20p$ (continuous
outcomes) is uniform on $[0, 1]$ under no effect, and Fisher's method gives

$$\chi^2_{2K} = -2\sum_{k=1}^{K}\log(pp_k).$$

Harrer et al.'s example, $p = 0.001, 0.002, 0.003, 0.004, 0.03$, gives $pp = 0.02, \dots, 0.60$ and
$\chi^2_{10} = 25.96$. The printed code then evaluates the tail probability at 26.96, giving 0.0026; at
25.96 it is **0.0038** (our arithmetic; for even df, $P(\chi^2_{2m} > x) = e^{-x/2}\sum_{j<m}(x/2)^j/j!$). The
conclusion is unchanged. The binomial check: $P(X \ge 7 \mid n = 8, \tfrac12) = 9/256 = 0.035$. For effect
estimation, a candidate effect $\theta$ gives the non-centrality $\delta = \theta\big/\sqrt{(n_1+n_2)/(n_1 n_2)}$
and $pp(t_k) = P(t > t_k \mid \delta, \text{df}, p < 0.05)$; the estimate minimises the
Kolmogorov–Smirnov distance between these $pp$-values and the uniform.

**Selection models** [@harrer_doing_meta]. With the random-effects density $f(x_k)$ and weight function
$w(p_k)$, the density of published effects is

$$f^*(x_k) = \frac{w(p_k)\,f(x_k)}{\int w(p_k)\,f(x_k)\,dx_k},$$

with $w$ a step function: $w(p) = \omega_i$ for $a_{i-1} < p \le a_i$, $\omega_1 = 1$. The 3PSM has one cut-point
$a_1 = 0.025$ and estimates $(\mu, \tau^2, \omega_2)$ jointly by maximum likelihood.

> **In plain words:** the published studies are the random-effects distribution reweighted by their chance
> of publication; fit the reweighted distribution, and $\mu$ is what the unfiltered literature would average.
