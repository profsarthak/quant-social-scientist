Effect Sizes for Synthesis: Computing & Converting Them

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source

Before studies can be averaged they have to speak one language. Every study hands over two numbers, an effect and its standard error, and most of the craft is getting those two numbers right.

A meta-analysis never pools raw data. It pools one effect size per study, together with that effect’s standard error. So the first job is translation: turn each paper’s means, counts, correlations or test statistics into the same metric, on a scale where its sampling error is roughly normal (logs for ratios, Fisher’s z for correlations, logits for proportions). Correct the metric’s known biases, and make sure no study is counted twice. Get this step wrong and the most careful pooling model will still average the wrong numbers.

Why it matters

Imagine pooling studies of eighth-graders’ maths skills. Even with strict inclusion criteria, the studies will not all have used the same test. Some will report points on a hard test, some on an easy one, and some only the share of students who passed. You can’t average those directly (Harrer et al. 2022). Before any pooling model can run, each result has to be re-expressed as a metric that means the same thing everywhere.

Harrer and colleagues list four properties that metric needs (Harrer et al. 2022):

  • Comparable: it means the same thing in every study. Raw point differences on different tests are not.
  • Computable: you can actually derive it from what each paper reports.
  • Reliable: you can compute its standard error, and its format suits the pooling method without introducing bias.
  • Interpretable: it answers the research question in a form people understand.

These pull against each other. The metric that is easiest to interpret (a risk ratio, a raw correlation) often has awkward statistical properties, so it is transformed before pooling and transformed back afterwards. Most of this entry is about that round trip. The inference-track entry on effect sizes explains why magnitude matters more than significance. This entry is the meta-analyst’s version: how to compute the magnitude, and its precision, for every study.

What each study must hand over: an estimate and its standard error

Write \theta_k for study k’s true effect and \hat\theta_k for the effect it observed. They differ by sampling error, because the study saw a sample and not the whole population (Harrer et al. 2022):

\hat\theta_k = \theta_k + \epsilon_k .

We never observe \epsilon_k, but statistical theory tells us how big it typically is. That typical size is the standard error: the standard deviation of the sampling distribution, the spread of estimates you would get by re-running the study many times with the same sample size. Harrer et al. show this with a simulation. They draw 1,000 samples of n = 50 from a population with mean 10 and SD 2. The standard deviation of the 1,000 sample means is 0.267, and the textbook formula s/\sqrt n applied to a single sample gives 0.262 (Harrer et al. 2022). One sample is enough to estimate the precision.

That is the key fact for everything that follows: an effect size plus its standard error is the minimum a study must provide to enter a meta-analysis (Harrer et al. 2022). Pooling weights each study by its precision (the inverse of its squared SE), so a wrong SE gives a wrong weight, even if the effect itself is right. Each metric below therefore comes with its own SE formula.

A field guide to the common metrics

Harrer et al. group the common metrics by the designs they usually come from (Harrer et al. 2022):

Mean one-group level (e.g. depression score) raw (or log if near zero) s/\sqrt n
Proportion prevalence logit \sqrt{1/(np) + 1/(n(1-p))}
Correlation r association of two continuous variables Fisher’s z 1/\sqrt{n-3}
Mean difference two groups, same instrument raw s_{\text{pooled}}\sqrt{1/n_1 + 1/n_2}
Standardised mean difference (SMD) two groups, different instruments raw, after the small-sample correction (Hedges’ g) \sqrt{\frac{n_1+n_2}{n_1n_2} + \frac{\text{SMD}^2}{2(n_1+n_2)}}

One-group measures: means and proportions

A mean pools without fuss, provided every study used the same scale. Harrer et al.’s example pools the average Beck Depression Inventory score across six trials of depressed patients: m = 31.12 (Harrer et al. 2022).

A proportion needs more care. It is trapped between 0 and 1, and near either end its sampling distribution becomes skewed, while the simple SE \sqrt{p(1-p)/n} shrinks artificially. That overstates the precision of exactly the rare-event prevalences that meta-analyses often target (Harrer et al. 2022). The fix is the logit: convert p to odds, p/(1-p), and take the log. On the logit scale p = 0.5 sits at 0 and there are no walls. With 25 cases in 125 people, p = 0.20 with SE 0.036 (Harrer et al. 2022); on the logit scale that is -1.39 with SE 0.22 (our arithmetic). The software logit-transforms automatically. Harrer et al.’s opioid-misuse example pools fifteen prevalence studies to a 12-month prevalence of 9.4% (8.4% to 10.7%) (Harrer et al. 2022). For proportions the {meta} default is a random-intercept logistic regression rather than inverse-variance weighting, as the pooling entry explains.

Correlations and Fisher’s z

A Pearson correlation standardises the covariance by both standard deviations, so it is comparable even when studies used different scales. Cohen’s rough benchmarks are about 0.1 small, 0.3 moderate and 0.5 large, and Harrer et al. stress that these are rules of thumb at best (Harrer et al. 2022).

Like a proportion, r has walls at -1 and 1, and its SE is biased in small samples. So correlations are pooled as Fisher’s z, z = \tfrac12 \ln\frac{1+r}{1-r}, whose SE depends only on the sample size: 1/\sqrt{n-3} (Harrer et al. 2022). A correlation of 0.28 becomes z = 0.29; the transform hardly changes small correlations and stretches large ones. The figure below shows why it is needed. In Harrer et al.’s health and well-being example, 29 studies pool (through z) to r = 0.36, a moderate association, reported back on the r scale (Harrer et al. 2022).

A point-biserial correlation links a continuous variable to a two-group variable. It measures much the same thing as a standardised mean difference, but it is range-restricted when the groups are unequal in size, so Harrer et al. advise converting it to an SMD for meta-analysis (formula in the appendix) (Harrer et al. 2022).

Mean differences, raw and standardised

When every study measured the outcome on the same scale (kilograms, HbA1c), the plain between-group mean difference is the most honest metric: it is already in meaningful units (Harrer et al. 2022).

When the instruments differ, divide the difference by the pooled standard deviation to get the standardised mean difference (SMD, often called Cohen’s d). An SMD of 1 means the group means are one standard deviation apart. Harrer et al.’s example has means 50 and 60, SDs of 10 and 100 people per group: SMD = -1.00 with SE 0.15 (Harrer et al. 2022).

The minus sign raises a practical point. If group 1 got a smoking intervention and smoked fewer cigarettes, a negative SMD is good news. In a meta-analysis every study must be coded in the same direction (say, positive = benefit), because some instruments score improvement as higher and some as lower (Harrer et al. 2022). Small studies also estimate the pooled SD poorly. One option is to standardise by an external SD taken from a large study that used the same instrument in a similar population (Harrer et al. 2022).

Within-group (pre–post) SMDs are a trap. They compare the same people at two times, so the two measurements are correlated, and the SE depends on that pre–post correlation, which papers almost never report. Harrer et al.’s example (means 20 → 30, pre-test SD 13, n = 80) gives SMD 0.77, with SE 0.127 only if you assume the correlation is 0.5 (Harrer et al. 2022). Even the standardiser has no consensus: there are at least five ways to compute a within-group SMD. Harrer et al.’s advice is to avoid within-group effects whenever a control group exists, and to compare groups at post-test instead (Harrer et al. 2022).

The limits of standardisation. Standardising is not a “get out of jail free” card (Harrer et al. 2022). An SMD depends on the spread of the sample. Run the same study, with the same raw effect, in a population whose standard deviation is twice as large, and its SMD halves. Jacob Cohen made the point that the effect of A on B can hardly depend on how much the group happens to vary. The same is true of correlations. Use raw mean differences when the scales allow it, and when they don’t, compute every SMD the same way (Harrer et al. 2022).

Risk ratios and odds ratios

A binary outcome in a two-arm trial gives a 2×2 table: a events and b non-events under treatment, c and d under control. The risk ratio divides the two event risks, \frac{a/(a+b)}{c/(c+d)}. RR = 1 means no difference, and RR < 1 means the treatment lowers the risk (Harrer et al. 2022). In Harrer et al.’s example 46 of 248 treated and 77 of 251 controls have the event, so RR = 0.60 (Harrer et al. 2022).

Ratios have a lopsided scale. Halving a risk is RR = 0.5, but the matching doubling is 2, not 1.5. Effects of the same size sit at different distances from 1, and the sampling distribution is skewed. Taking logs fixes this: \ln 0.5 = -0.69 and \ln 2 = +0.69, centred on 0. So ratios are pooled as log ratios and exponentiated at the end. Here \ln \text{RR} = -0.50 with SE 0.16 (Harrer et al. 2022).

Three warnings come with risk ratios (Harrer et al. 2022):

  • Zero cells. If no one in the control arm has the event, the ratio divides by zero. Adding 0.5 to empty cells is the traditional patch, but it can bias results. The Mantel–Haenszel method usually needs no patch.
  • Double-zero studies. Harrer et al.’s imaginary trial of Fulguridone, a pill against being struck by lightning, follows 100 people for three years. Nobody in either arm is struck. That isn’t evidence that RR = 1; it is a study too small to see a rare event, and such studies are usually dropped.
  • A ratio hides the baseline. RR = 0.5 could mean a fall from 40% to 20% or from 0.004% to 0.002%. These are very different findings for a population.

The odds ratio uses odds instead of risks: \frac{a/b}{c/d}. Odds count events per non-event. If two of three people have the event, the risk is 67% but the odds are 2 (Harrer et al. 2022). Harrer et al.’s example (45 events and 98 non-events against 67 and 76) gives OR = 0.52, log OR -0.65, SE 0.25 (Harrer et al. 2022). The OR has the same problems as the RR and one more: people tend to read it as a risk ratio. So it is often better to use RRs, or to convert ORs back for reporting using an assumed control risk (Harrer et al. 2022). The gap can be large. In this example the control risk is 47%, and the same data give RR = 0.67 (our arithmetic, via the Zhang–Yu conversion in the appendix). An OR of 0.52 sounds like “half”; the risk actually fell by a third.

Incidence rate ratios: when time matters

Risk and odds ratios ignore when events happen. Two groups can have the same number of deaths over ten years even though one group died sooner (Harrer et al. 2022). Incidence rate ratios bring time in through person-time: the total time participants were at risk.

Harrer et al.’s six-person example follows people for ten years with yearly check-ups. Two never have the event and contribute 10 years each. For the four who do, the event happened somewhere inside the year it was detected, so each is credited with the midpoint (1.5, 5.5, 4.5 and 8.5 years). That gives 40 person-years and an incidence rate of 4/40 = 0.1 per person-year (Harrer et al. 2022). Dividing one group’s rate by another’s gives the IRR. With 28 events in each arm but 3,025 against 2,380 person-years, IRR = 0.79: equal counts, but the treated group took longer to get there (Harrer et al. 2022).

The midpoint is a guess, so Harrer et al. recommend a sensitivity analysis: recompute the person-time using the midpoint, the last event-free check-up, and the check-up where the event was found, and see whether the conclusion holds (Harrer et al. 2022). IRRs also assume the risk within each group is constant over time. That is often unrealistic, and Bender and Beckmann’s simulations suggest IRRs are only adequate when follow-up times are similar and the event is fairly rare (baseline risk under 25%). When that fails, the better metric is the hazard ratio from a Cox model, pooled as a log ratio like any other (Harrer et al. 2022). (The survival-analysis entry explains hazards.) Harrer et al.’s eating-disorder-prevention example pools five trials to IRR = 0.62: about 38% fewer new cases a year (Harrer et al. 2022).

Correcting the metric’s own biases

\hat\theta_k = \theta_k + \epsilon_k assumes the only gap between estimate and truth is random noise. Some metrics also carry a systematic error, and Harrer et al. cover three standard corrections (Harrer et al. 2022). (Bias from how a study was run is a different matter, handled by risk-of-bias assessment.)

Small-sample bias: Hedges’ g. The SMD overestimates the true effect in small studies, especially below about 20 participants. Hedges’ correction multiplies it by 1 - \frac{3}{4n-9}, where n is the total sample size. An SMD of 0.5 with n = 30 becomes g = 0.486 (Harrer et al. 2022). The factor is 0.90 at n = 10, 0.96 at 20 and 0.99 at 100 (our arithmetic), so the correction only matters for small trials, which are common. Papers use “SMD” and “g” loosely, so check which one a study reports.

Unreliability: attenuation. A noisy measuring instrument dilutes a correlation toward zero. Spearman described this in 1904; the same problem appears for regression slopes as errors-in-variables bias. If you know the instrument’s reliability r_{xx} (say, its test–retest correlation), Hunter and Schmidt’s correction divides the effect and its SE by \sqrt{r_{xx}} (by \sqrt{r_{xx}r_{yy}} when both variables are measured with error) (Harrer et al. 2022). In Harrer et al.’s example, r = 0.34 with reliabilities 0.8 and 0.7 becomes 0.45, and its SE rises from 0.09 to 0.12. That is deliberate: the corrected estimate carries the measurement uncertainty too (Harrer et al. 2022).

Range restriction. A study of age and cognitive function among 65-to-69-year-olds will find almost no correlation, because age barely varies in its sample. Given the ratio U of the unrestricted SD (from a representative sample of the population the meta-analysis is about) to the study’s SD, the correlation can be corrected. With SDs 18 and 11, r = 0.34 becomes 0.51 (Harrer et al. 2022).

Both Hunter–Schmidt corrections are routine in some fields (organisational psychology) and rare in others (biomedicine). They also follow an all-or-none rule: you can’t correct some studies and not others, and an assumed reliability that is wrong can distort the results badly. In practice the missing reliabilities often make the correction impossible (Harrer et al. 2022).

When the paper doesn’t report what you need

Often a paper reports a t-test, an F, a \chi^2 or just a p-value instead of the group means and SDs. Such studies can still be included if the statistic can be converted into an effect and an SE, which are then pooled as pre-calculated values (metagen’s TE and seTE columns) (Harrer et al. 2022). Harrer et al.’s toolkit (Chapter 17, mostly via the {esc} package) covers:

  • Two-sample t → SMD. t = 3.3 with groups of 100 and 150 gives d \approx 0.43.
  • One-way ANOVA with two groups (F with 1 numerator df) → SMD. F = 5.04 with groups of 519 and 528 gives g = 0.14.
  • Point-biserial r → SMD. r = 0.25 (groups 99 and 120) gives d = 0.52.
  • Regression coefficients → SMD or r. This comes with a warning: coefficients from multiple regressions are only comparable if every study controlled for the same covariates, which they rarely do.
  • \chi^2 with 1 df → odds ratio. \chi^2 = 7.9 with N = 100 gives OR 2.63.
  • An effect and its p-value only → SE, by working back from the p-value (Altman and Bland’s formulas). d = 0.71 with p = 0.013 implies SE ≈ 0.29.

The numbers above are Harrer et al.’s (Harrer et al. 2022). The two-sample t and p-value cases are short enough to check by hand (appendix). If no conversion works, the remaining option is to email the authors, more than once. If that fails too, the study is excluded (Harrer et al. 2022).

Translating for decisions: the number needed to treat

A pooled g = 0.35 means little to a patient or a minister. Medicine’s usual translation is the number needed to treat (NNT): how many people must receive the treatment for one additional person to benefit or avoid a bad event (Harrer et al. 2022). With binary outcomes it is simply one over the absolute risk difference. In the risk-ratio example above, risks of 18.5% and 30.7% differ by 12.1 percentage points, so the NNT is about 8 (our arithmetic).

From an SMD the NNT depends on what you assume. The Kraemer–Kupfer method needs only the effect: d = 0.245 gives NNT 7.3. The Furukawa–Leucht method also uses the control group’s event rate (CER). With a CER of 0.35, the same d gives 10.6. Furukawa’s method estimates the true NNT better, so prefer it when a sensible CER is available (Harrer et al. 2022). Treat the NNT with care: lay readers often misunderstand it despite its reputation for being intuitive, researchers often compute it wrongly, and it has no reliable standard error, so it can’t be pooled. Compute it after pooling, from the pooled effect (Harrer et al. 2022).

The unit-of-analysis problem

Pooling assumes each effect size is independent. Two common situations break this (Harrer et al. 2022):

A study with more than two arms. Treatments A and B, each compared with the same control C, give two effects that share C’s data. That is double-counting: the two effects are correlated (at 0.5 when the arms are equal in size) and their precision is overstated. Harrer et al. list three remedies:

  • Split the shared group’s sample (and events) between the two comparisons. This fixes the inflated precision but leaves the correlation.
  • Drop one comparison. This solves the problem but throws data away.
  • Combine A and B into one arm. This is Cochrane’s recommendation. For continuous data the arms’ means and SDs are merged with a formula given in the appendix. Two arms of 50 with means 3.5 and 4 and SDs 3 and 3.8 become one arm of 100 with mean 3.75 and SD 3.42.

Combining also has a cost. If A is a state-of-the-art treatment and B an outdated one, a null result from the merged arm can’t tell you whether B diluted A, so splitting or dropping may be better when the arms really differ (Harrer et al. 2022).

Several instruments for one outcome. If a study measured depression on three scales, its three effects come from the same people. You can pick one per study, using a hierarchy of instruments fixed in the analysis plan beforehand; you can aggregate them, which requires assuming a within-study correlation (Harrer et al.’s example assumes 0.6); or you can keep them all and use a three-level model built for dependent effects (Harrer et al. 2022). The third option has its own entry, multilevel meta-analysis.

Common pitfalls

WarningWhere this goes wrong
  • Pooling on the wrong scale. Raw ratios, raw correlations and raw proportions near 0 or 1 have skewed sampling distributions and misleading SEs. Pool log ratios, Fisher’s z and logits, then back-transform. (Harrer et al. 2022)
  • Inconsistent signs. If “positive” means benefit in some studies and harm in others, the pooled effect is meaningless. Code every effect in one direction before pooling. (Harrer et al. 2022)
  • Mixing d and g, or different SMD recipes. Check whether each study applied Hedges’ correction and which SD it standardised by, and compute them all the same way. (Harrer et al. 2022)
  • Pre–post effects with a guessed correlation. The SE of a within-group SMD depends on a correlation papers rarely report. Use between-group post-test effects when a control group exists. (Harrer et al. 2022)
  • Treating standardised effects as fully comparable. An SMD or r shrinks or grows with the sample’s variability, not only with the true effect. (Harrer et al. 2022)
  • Reading an odds ratio as a risk ratio. When the event is common the two diverge (0.52 against 0.67 in the example above). Report RRs, or convert. (Harrer et al. 2022)
  • Forgetting the baseline. A relative effect says nothing about absolute risk. Report the control risk, or an NNT computed after pooling. (Harrer et al. 2022)
  • Correcting some studies but not others. Attenuation and range-restriction corrections are all or none, and an assumed reliability that is wrong distorts the result. (Harrer et al. 2022)
  • Double-counting a shared control arm. Two comparisons that use the same control group are not independent. Combine arms, split the control group, or model the dependence. (Harrer et al. 2022)
  • Converting multiple-regression coefficients across studies. Different covariate sets mean the coefficients answer different questions. (Harrer et al. 2022)

A meta-analysis never pools raw data. It pools one **effect size** per study, together with that effect's
**standard error**. So the first job is translation: turn each paper's means, counts, correlations or test
statistics into the same metric, on a scale where its sampling error is roughly normal (logs for ratios,

## Why it matters

Imagine pooling studies of eighth-graders' maths skills. Even with strict inclusion criteria, the studies
will not all have used the same test. Some will report points on a hard test, some on an easy one, and
some only the share of students who passed. You can't average those directly [@harrer_doing_meta]. Before
any [pooling model](pooling-effect-sizes.qmd) can run, each result has to be re-expressed as a metric that
means the same thing everywhere.

Harrer and colleagues list four properties that metric needs [@harrer_doing_meta]:

- **Comparable**: it means the same thing in every study. Raw point differences on different tests are not.
- **Computable**: you can actually derive it from what each paper reports.
- **Reliable**: you can compute its standard error, and its format suits the pooling method without
  introducing bias.
- **Interpretable**: it answers the research question in a form people understand.

These pull against each other. The metric that is easiest to interpret (a risk ratio, a raw correlation)
often has awkward statistical properties, so it is **transformed** before pooling and transformed back
afterwards. Most of this entry is about that round trip. The [inference-track entry on effect
sizes](../02-inference/effect-sizes.qmd) explains why magnitude matters more than significance. This entry is
the meta-analyst's version: how to *compute* the magnitude, and its precision, for every study.

## What each study must hand over: an estimate and its standard error

Write $\theta_k$ for study $k$'s **true** effect and $\hat\theta_k$ for the effect it **observed**. They
differ by sampling error, because the study saw a sample and not the whole population
[@harrer_doing_meta]:

$$\hat\theta_k = \theta_k + \epsilon_k .$$

We never observe $\epsilon_k$, but statistical theory tells us how big it typically is. That typical size is
the **standard error**: the standard deviation of the [sampling
distribution](../02-inference/sampling-distributions-clt.qmd), the spread of estimates you would get by
re-running the study many times with the same sample size. Harrer et al. show this with a simulation. They draw
1,000 samples of $n = 50$ from a population with mean 10 and SD 2. The standard deviation of the 1,000 sample
means is 0.267, and the textbook formula $s/\sqrt n$ applied to a *single* sample gives 0.262
[@harrer_doing_meta]. One sample is enough to estimate the precision.

That is the key fact for everything that follows: **an effect size plus its standard error is the minimum a
study must provide** to enter a meta-analysis [@harrer_doing_meta]. Pooling weights each study by its precision
(the inverse of its squared SE), so a wrong SE gives a wrong weight, even if the effect itself is right. Each
metric below therefore comes with its own SE formula.

## A field guide to the common metrics

Harrer et al. group the common metrics by the designs they usually come from [@harrer_doing_meta]:

| Metric | Typical use | Pooled on which scale | Standard error (of the pooled scale) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mean | one-group level (e.g. depression score) | raw (or log if near zero) | $s/\sqrt n$ |
| Proportion | prevalence | **logit** | $\sqrt{1/(np) + 1/(n(1-p))}$ |
| Correlation $r$ | association of two continuous variables | **Fisher's $z$** | $1/\sqrt{n-3}$ |
| Mean difference | two groups, same instrument | raw | $s_{\text{pooled}}\sqrt{1/n_1 + 1/n_2}$ |
| Standardised mean difference (SMD) | two groups, different instruments | raw, after the small-sample correction (**Hedges' $g$**) | $\sqrt{\frac{n_1+n_2}{n_1n_2} + \frac{\text{SMD}^2}{2(n_1+n_2)}}$ |
| Risk ratio / odds ratio | binary outcome, two groups | **log** | see below |
| Incidence rate ratio | events over person-time | **log** | $\sqrt{1/E_{\text{treat}} + 1/E_{\text{control}}}$ |

### One-group measures: means and proportions

A **mean** pools without fuss, provided every study used the same scale. Harrer et al.'s example pools
the average Beck Depression Inventory score across six trials of depressed patients: $m = 31.12$
[@harrer_doing_meta].

A **proportion** needs more care. It is trapped between 0 and 1, and near either end its sampling
distribution becomes skewed, while the simple SE $\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}$ shrinks artificially. That overstates
the precision of exactly the rare-event prevalences that meta-analyses often target [@harrer_doing_meta]. The
fix is the **logit**: convert $p$ to odds, $p/(1-p)$, and take the log. On the logit scale $p = 0.5$ sits at 0
and there are no walls. With 25 cases in 125 people, $p = 0.20$ with SE 0.036 [@harrer_doing_meta]; on
the logit scale that is $-1.39$ with SE 0.22 (our arithmetic). The software logit-transforms automatically.
Harrer et al.'s opioid-misuse example pools fifteen prevalence studies to a 12-month prevalence of
**9.4%** (8.4% to 10.7%) [@harrer_doing_meta]. For proportions the `{meta}` default is a random-intercept
logistic regression rather than inverse-variance weighting, as the [pooling
entry](pooling-effect-sizes.qmd) explains.

### Correlations and Fisher's $z$

A **Pearson correlation** standardises the covariance by both standard deviations, so it is comparable even
when studies used different scales. Cohen's rough benchmarks are about 0.1 small, 0.3 moderate and 0.5
large, and Harrer et al. stress that these are rules of thumb at best [@harrer_doing_meta].

Like a proportion, $r$ has walls at $-1$ and $1$, and its SE is biased in small samples. So correlations are
pooled as **Fisher's $z$**, $z = \tfrac12 \ln\frac{1+r}{1-r}$, whose SE depends only on the sample size:
$1/\sqrt{n-3}$ [@harrer_doing_meta]. A correlation of 0.28 becomes $z = 0.29$; the transform hardly changes
small correlations and stretches large ones. The figure below shows why it is needed. In Harrer et al.'s
health and well-being example, 29 studies pool (through $z$) to **$r = 0.36$**, a moderate association,
reported back on the $r$ scale [@harrer_doing_meta].

A **point-biserial correlation** links a continuous variable to a two-group variable. It measures much the
same thing as a standardised mean difference, but it is range-restricted when the groups are unequal in size,
so Harrer et al. advise converting it to an SMD for meta-analysis (formula in the appendix)
[@harrer_doing_meta].

### Mean differences, raw and standardised

When every study measured the outcome on the **same scale** (kilograms, HbA1c), the plain **between-group
mean difference** is the most honest metric: it is already in meaningful units [@harrer_doing_meta].

When the instruments differ, divide the difference by the pooled standard deviation to get the
**standardised mean difference** (SMD, often called Cohen's $d$). An SMD of 1 means the group means are one
standard deviation apart. Harrer et al.'s example has means 50 and 60, SDs of 10 and 100 people per group:
SMD $= -1.00$ with SE 0.15 [@harrer_doing_meta].

The minus sign raises a practical point. If group 1 got a smoking intervention and smoked *fewer* cigarettes,
a negative SMD is good news. In a meta-analysis **every study must be coded in the same direction** (say,
positive = benefit), because some instruments score improvement as higher and some as lower
[@harrer_doing_meta]. Small studies also estimate the pooled SD poorly. One option is to standardise by an
external SD taken from a large study that used the same instrument in a similar population
[@harrer_doing_meta].

**Within-group (pre–post) SMDs** are a trap. They compare the same people at two times, so the two
measurements are correlated, and the SE depends on that **pre–post correlation**, which papers almost never
report. Harrer et al.'s example (means 20 → 30, pre-test SD 13, $n = 80$) gives SMD 0.77, with SE 0.127 only
*if* you assume the correlation is 0.5 [@harrer_doing_meta]. Even the standardiser has no consensus: there
are at least five ways to compute a within-group SMD. Harrer et al.'s advice is to avoid within-group effects
whenever a control group exists, and to compare groups at post-test instead [@harrer_doing_meta].

**The limits of standardisation.** Standardising is not a "get out of jail free" card [@harrer_doing_meta].
An SMD depends on the spread of the sample. Run the same study, with the same raw effect, in a population whose
standard deviation is twice as large, and its SMD halves. Jacob Cohen made the point that the effect of A on B can hardly
depend on how much the group happens to vary. The same is true of correlations. Use raw mean differences when
the scales allow it, and when they don't, compute every SMD the same way [@harrer_doing_meta].

### Risk ratios and odds ratios

A binary outcome in a two-arm trial gives a 2×2 table: $a$ events and $b$ non-events under treatment, $c$ and
$d$ under control. The **risk ratio** divides the two event risks, $\frac{a/(a+b)}{c/(c+d)}$. RR = 1 means no
difference, and RR < 1 means the treatment lowers the risk [@harrer_doing_meta]. In Harrer et al.'s example
46 of 248 treated and 77 of 251 controls have the event, so RR = **0.60** [@harrer_doing_meta].

Ratios have a lopsided scale. Halving a risk is RR = 0.5, but the matching doubling is 2, not 1.5. Effects of
the same size sit at different distances from 1, and the sampling distribution is skewed. Taking logs fixes
this: $\ln 0.5 = -0.69$ and $\ln 2 = +0.69$, centred on 0. So ratios are pooled as **log ratios** and
exponentiated at the end. Here $\ln \text{RR} = -0.50$ with SE 0.16 [@harrer_doing_meta].

Three warnings come with risk ratios [@harrer_doing_meta]:

- **Zero cells.** If no one in the control arm has the event, the ratio divides by zero. Adding 0.5 to empty
  cells is the traditional patch, but it can bias results. The [Mantel–Haenszel
  method](pooling-effect-sizes.qmd) usually needs no patch.
- **Double-zero studies.** Harrer et al.'s imaginary trial of *Fulguridone*, a pill against being struck by
  lightning, follows 100 people for three years. Nobody in either arm is struck. That isn't evidence that
  RR = 1; it is a study too small to see a rare event, and such studies are usually dropped.
- **A ratio hides the baseline.** RR = 0.5 could mean a fall from 40% to 20% or from 0.004% to 0.002%. These
  are very different findings for a population.

The **odds ratio** uses odds instead of risks: $\frac{a/b}{c/d}$. Odds count events per non-event. If two of
three people have the event, the risk is 67% but the odds are 2 [@harrer_doing_meta]. Harrer et al.'s example
(45 events and 98 non-events against 67 and 76) gives OR = **0.52**, log OR $-0.65$, SE 0.25
[@harrer_doing_meta]. The OR has the same problems as the RR and one more: people tend to **read it as a risk
ratio**. So it is often better to use RRs, or to convert ORs back for reporting using an assumed control
risk [@harrer_doing_meta]. The gap can be large. In this example the control risk is 47%, and the same data
give RR = 0.67 (our arithmetic, via the Zhang–Yu conversion in the appendix). An OR of 0.52 sounds like
"half"; the risk actually fell by a third.

### Incidence rate ratios: when time matters

Risk and odds ratios ignore *when* events happen. Two groups can have the same number of deaths over ten
years even though one group died sooner [@harrer_doing_meta]. **Incidence rate ratios** bring time in through
**person-time**: the total time participants were at risk.

Harrer et al.'s six-person example follows people for ten years with yearly check-ups. Two never have the
event and contribute 10 years each. For the four who do, the event happened somewhere inside the year it was
detected, so each is credited with the midpoint (1.5, 5.5, 4.5 and 8.5 years). That gives 40 person-years
and an incidence rate of $4/40 = 0.1$ per person-year [@harrer_doing_meta]. Dividing one group's rate by
another's gives the IRR. With 28 events in each arm but 3,025 against 2,380 person-years, IRR = **0.79**:
equal counts, but the treated group took longer to get there [@harrer_doing_meta].

The midpoint is a guess, so Harrer et al. recommend a **sensitivity analysis**: recompute the person-time
using the midpoint, the last event-free check-up, and the check-up where the event was found, and see whether
the conclusion holds [@harrer_doing_meta]. IRRs also assume the risk within each group is constant over time.
That is often unrealistic, and Bender and Beckmann's simulations suggest IRRs are only adequate when follow-up
times are similar and the event is fairly rare (baseline risk under 25%). When that fails, the better metric
is the **hazard ratio** from a Cox model, pooled as a log ratio like any other [@harrer_doing_meta]. (The
[survival-analysis entry](../03-regression/survival-analysis.qmd) explains hazards.) Harrer et al.'s
eating-disorder-prevention example pools five trials to IRR = **0.62**: about 38% fewer new cases a year
[@harrer_doing_meta].

```{python}
#| label: fig-fisher-z
#| fig-cap: "Why correlations are pooled as Fisher's z. Left: 5,000 simulated studies, each computing r from n = 20 people when the true correlation is 0.8. The wall at 1 squeezes the distribution, which has a long left tail (skewness about −1.1) and a mean slightly below 0.8; the normal curve that a standard error assumes (dark line, SE = (1 − 0.8²)/√18) fits it badly. Right: the same 5,000 estimates after z = ½ ln[(1 + r)/(1 − r)]. The distribution is symmetric (skewness about 0) and matches the normal curve with SE = 1/√(n − 3) = 0.243 (orange), a standard error that needs nothing but the sample size. Logs do the same job for risk, odds and rate ratios, and the logit for proportions."
#| code-fold: true
#| code-summary: "Show the figure code"

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
rho, n, R = 0.8, 20, 5000
x = rng.standard_normal((R, n))
y = rho * x + np.sqrt(1 - rho**2) * rng.standard_normal((R, n))
xc = x - x.mean(axis=1, keepdims=True)
yc = y - y.mean(axis=1, keepdims=True)
r = (xc * yc).sum(axis=1) / np.sqrt((xc**2).sum(axis=1) * (yc**2).sum(axis=1))
z = np.arctanh(r)                                # 0.5 * ln((1 + r) / (1 - r))

def skew(a):
    return np.mean((a - a.mean())**3) / a.std()**3

def normal(t, m, s):
    return np.exp(-0.5 * ((t - m) / s)**2) / (s * np.sqrt(2 * np.pi))

fig, (ax, bx) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(9.6, 3.6))

ax.hist(r, bins=60, density=True, color="#b7b6a8", edgecolor="#f4f3ec", linewidth=0.3)
t = np.linspace(0.35, 1.05, 400)
se_r = (1 - rho**2) / np.sqrt(n - 2)
ax.plot(t, normal(t, rho, se_r), color="#161613", lw=1.6)
ax.axvline(1.0, color="#8a8a7e", ls=":", lw=1.0)
ax.set_xlim(0.35, 1.03)
ax.set_xlabel("sample correlation r")
ax.set_title(f"Raw r: walled in at 1, skew {skew(r):.2f}", fontsize=10)
ax.set_yticks([])

bx.hist(z, bins=60, density=True, color="#b7b6a8", edgecolor="#f4f3ec", linewidth=0.3)
u = np.linspace(z.min(), z.max(), 400)
bx.plot(u, normal(u, np.arctanh(rho), 1 / np.sqrt(n - 3)), color="#e8531d", lw=1.8)
bx.set_xlabel("Fisher's z")
bx.set_title(f"Fisher's z: symmetric, skew {skew(z):.2f}", fontsize=10)
bx.set_yticks([])

plt.show()
```

## Correcting the metric's own biases

$\hat\theta_k = \theta_k + \epsilon_k$ assumes the only gap between estimate and truth is random noise. Some
metrics also carry a **systematic** error, and Harrer et al. cover three standard corrections
[@harrer_doing_meta]. (Bias from *how a study was run* is a different matter, handled by risk-of-bias
assessment.)

**Small-sample bias: Hedges' $g$.** The SMD overestimates the true effect in small studies, especially below
about 20 participants. Hedges' correction multiplies it by $1 - \frac{3}{4n-9}$, where $n$ is the total sample
size. An SMD of 0.5 with $n = 30$ becomes $g = 0.486$ [@harrer_doing_meta]. The factor is 0.90 at $n = 10$,
0.96 at 20 and 0.99 at 100 (our arithmetic), so the correction only matters for small trials, which are
common. Papers use "SMD" and "$g$" loosely, so check which one a study reports.

**Unreliability: attenuation.** A noisy measuring instrument dilutes a correlation toward zero. Spearman
described this in 1904; the same problem appears for regression slopes as [errors-in-variables
bias](../03-regression/regression-and-causality.qmd). If you know the instrument's reliability $r_{xx}$ (say,
its test–retest correlation), Hunter and Schmidt's correction divides the effect *and* its SE by
$\sqrt{r_{xx}}$ (by $\sqrt{r_{xx}r_{yy}}$ when both variables are measured with error)
[@harrer_doing_meta]. In Harrer et al.'s example, $r = 0.34$ with reliabilities 0.8 and 0.7 becomes **0.45**,
and its SE rises from 0.09 to 0.12. That is deliberate: the corrected estimate carries the measurement
uncertainty too [@harrer_doing_meta].

**Range restriction.** A study of age and cognitive function among 65-to-69-year-olds will find almost no
correlation, because age barely varies in its sample. Given the ratio $U$ of the unrestricted SD (from a
representative sample of the population the meta-analysis is about) to the study's SD, the correlation can be
corrected. With SDs 18 and 11, $r = 0.34$ becomes **0.51** [@harrer_doing_meta].

Both Hunter–Schmidt corrections are routine in some fields (organisational psychology) and rare in others
(biomedicine). They also follow an **all-or-none** rule: you can't correct some studies and not others, and
an assumed reliability that is wrong can distort the results badly. In practice the missing reliabilities
often make the correction impossible [@harrer_doing_meta].

## When the paper doesn't report what you need

Often a paper reports a $t$-test, an $F$, a $\chi^2$ or just a $p$-value instead of the group means and SDs.
Such studies can still be included if the statistic can be converted into an effect and an SE, which are then
pooled as pre-calculated values (`metagen`'s `TE` and `seTE` columns) [@harrer_doing_meta]. Harrer et al.'s
toolkit (Chapter 17, mostly via the `{esc}` package) covers:

- **Two-sample $t$** → SMD. $t = 3.3$ with groups of 100 and 150 gives $d \approx 0.43$.
- **One-way ANOVA with two groups** ($F$ with 1 numerator df) → SMD. $F = 5.04$ with groups of 519 and 528
  gives $g = 0.14$.
- **Point-biserial $r$** → SMD. $r = 0.25$ (groups 99 and 120) gives $d = 0.52$.
- **Regression coefficients** → SMD or $r$. This comes with a warning: coefficients from *multiple* regressions
  are only comparable if every study controlled for the same covariates, which they rarely do.
- **$\chi^2$ with 1 df** → odds ratio. $\chi^2 = 7.9$ with $N = 100$ gives OR 2.63.
- **An effect and its $p$-value only** → SE, by working back from the $p$-value (Altman and Bland's
  formulas). $d = 0.71$ with $p = 0.013$ implies SE ≈ 0.29.

The numbers above are Harrer et al.'s [@harrer_doing_meta]. The two-sample $t$ and $p$-value cases are short
enough to check by hand (appendix). If no conversion works, the remaining option is to email the authors, more
than once. If that fails too, the study is excluded [@harrer_doing_meta].

The calls look like this in R (shown for reference; this site does not execute R):

```r

esc_f(f = 5.04, grp1n = 519, grp2n = 528, es.type = "g")     # F-value -> Hedges' g
```

## Translating for decisions: the number needed to treat

A pooled $g = 0.35$ means little to a patient or a minister. Medicine's usual translation is the **number
needed to treat** (NNT): how many people must receive the treatment for one additional person to benefit or
avoid a bad event [@harrer_doing_meta]. With binary outcomes it is simply one over the absolute risk
difference. In the risk-ratio example above, risks of 18.5% and 30.7% differ by 12.1 percentage points, so the
NNT is about 8 (our arithmetic).

From an SMD the NNT depends on what you assume. The Kraemer–Kupfer method needs only the effect: $d = 0.245$
gives NNT 7.3. The Furukawa–Leucht method also uses the control group's event rate (CER). With a CER of 0.35,
the same $d$ gives 10.6. Furukawa's method estimates the true NNT better, so prefer it when a sensible CER is
available [@harrer_doing_meta]. Treat the NNT with care: lay readers often misunderstand it despite its
reputation for being intuitive, researchers often compute it wrongly, and it has no reliable standard error,
so it can't be pooled. Compute it **after** pooling, from the pooled effect [@harrer_doing_meta].

## The unit-of-analysis problem

Pooling assumes each effect size is **independent**. Two common situations break this [@harrer_doing_meta]:

**A study with more than two arms.** Treatments A and B, each compared with the same control C, give two
effects that share C's data. That is **double-counting**: the two effects are correlated (at 0.5 when the
arms are equal in size) and their precision is overstated. Harrer et al. list three remedies:

- **Split** the shared group's sample (and events) between the two comparisons. This fixes the inflated
  precision but leaves the correlation.
- **Drop** one comparison. This solves the problem but throws data away.
- **Combine** A and B into one arm. This is Cochrane's recommendation. For continuous data the arms' means
  and SDs are merged with a formula given in the appendix. Two arms of 50 with means 3.5 and 4 and SDs 3 and
  3.8 become one arm of 100 with mean 3.75 and SD 3.42.

Combining also has a cost. If A is a state-of-the-art treatment and B an outdated one, a null result from the
merged arm can't tell you whether B diluted A, so splitting or dropping may be better when the arms really
differ [@harrer_doing_meta].

**Several instruments for one outcome.** If a study measured depression on three scales, its three effects
come from the same people. You can **pick one** per study, using a hierarchy of instruments fixed in the
analysis plan beforehand; you can **aggregate** them, which requires assuming a within-study correlation
(Harrer et al.'s example assumes 0.6); or you can keep them all and use a **three-level model** built for
dependent effects [@harrer_doing_meta]. The third option has its own entry, [multilevel
meta-analysis](multilevel-meta-analysis.qmd).

## Common pitfalls

::: {.callout-warning title="Where this goes wrong"}
- **Pooling on the wrong scale.** Raw ratios, raw correlations and raw proportions near 0 or 1 have skewed
  sampling distributions and misleading SEs. Pool log ratios, Fisher's $z$ and logits, then back-transform.
  [@harrer_doing_meta]
- **Inconsistent signs.** If "positive" means benefit in some studies and harm in others, the pooled
  effect is meaningless. Code every effect in one direction before pooling. [@harrer_doing_meta]
- **Mixing $d$ and $g$, or different SMD recipes.** Check whether each study applied Hedges' correction
  and which SD it standardised by, and compute them all the same way. [@harrer_doing_meta]
- **Pre–post effects with a guessed correlation.** The SE of a within-group SMD depends on a correlation
  papers rarely report. Use between-group post-test effects when a control group exists. [@harrer_doing_meta]
- **Treating standardised effects as fully comparable.** An SMD or $r$ shrinks or grows with the sample's
  variability, not only with the true effect. [@harrer_doing_meta]
- **Reading an odds ratio as a risk ratio.** When the event is common the two diverge (0.52 against 0.67
  in the example above). Report RRs, or convert. [@harrer_doing_meta]
- **Forgetting the baseline.** A relative effect says nothing about absolute risk. Report the control
  risk, or an NNT computed after pooling. [@harrer_doing_meta]
- **Correcting some studies but not others.** Attenuation and range-restriction corrections are all or
  none, and an assumed reliability that is wrong distorts the result. [@harrer_doing_meta]
- **Double-counting a shared control arm.** Two comparisons that use the same control group are not
  independent. Combine arms, split the control group, or model the dependence. [@harrer_doing_meta]
- **Converting multiple-regression coefficients across studies.** Different covariate sets mean the
  coefficients answer different questions. [@harrer_doing_meta]
:::

## Appendix · the mathematics {.unnumbered}

::: {.math-appendix}

**The formulas, collected** [@harrer_doing_meta]. With group sizes $n_1, n_2$, total $N$, and the pooled SD
$s_{\text{pooled}} = \sqrt{\frac{(n_1-1)s_1^2 + (n_2-1)s_2^2}{n_1+n_2-2}}$:

| Effect | Estimate | Standard error |
|---|---|---|
| Mean | $\bar x$ | $s/\sqrt n$ |
| Logit proportion | $\ln\frac{p}{1-p}$ | $\sqrt{\frac{1}{np} + \frac{1}{n(1-p)}}$ |
| Fisher's $z$ | $\tfrac12\ln\frac{1+r}{1-r}$ | $\frac{1}{\sqrt{n-3}}$ |
| Mean difference | $\bar x_1 - \bar x_2$ | $s_{\text{pooled}}\sqrt{\frac{1}{n_1} + \frac{1}{n_2}}$ |
| SMD | $\frac{\bar x_1 - \bar x_2}{s_{\text{pooled}}}$ | $\sqrt{\frac{n_1+n_2}{n_1n_2} + \frac{\text{SMD}^2}{2(n_1+n_2)}}$ |
| Within-group SMD | $\frac{\bar x_{t_2} - \bar x_{t_1}}{s_{t_1}}$ | $\sqrt{\frac{2(1-r_{t_1t_2})}{n} + \frac{\text{SMD}^2}{2n}}$ |
| Log risk ratio | $\ln\frac{a/(a+b)}{c/(c+d)}$ | $\sqrt{\frac1a + \frac1c - \frac{1}{a+b} - \frac{1}{c+d}}$ |
| Log odds ratio | $\ln\frac{a/b}{c/d}$ | $\sqrt{\frac1a + \frac1b + \frac1c + \frac1d}$ |
| Log incidence rate ratio | $\ln\frac{E_t/T_t}{E_c/T_c}$ | $\sqrt{\frac{1}{E_t} + \frac{1}{E_c}}$ |

> **In plain words:** every SE shrinks roughly like one over the square root of the amount of information.
> For ratios the "information" is the number of *events*, not people, which is why rare-event trials are
> imprecise however large they are.

**Why Fisher's $z$ works.** The variance of $r$ is approximately $(1-\rho^2)^2/n$, so it depends on the
unknown $\rho$: precise near $\pm1$, noisy near 0. The slope of $z(r)$ is $1/(1-r^2)$, so a small change in
$r$ moves $z$ by that factor, and the variance of $z$ is approximately
$\frac{(1-\rho^2)^2}{n}\cdot\frac{1}{(1-\rho^2)^2} = \frac1n$, no longer depending on $\rho$. The $n-3$ is a
small-sample refinement. This is the same reasoning used for the log ratios: a transformation chosen so that
the variance no longer depends on the parameter. In the figure's simulation, the SD of $z$ is 0.241 against the
formula's 0.243.

**Corrections** [@harrer_doing_meta].
Hedges: $g = \text{SMD}\left(1 - \frac{3}{4N-9}\right)$.
Attenuation: $r_c = \frac{r_{xy}}{\sqrt{r_{xx}r_{yy}}}$, $\text{SE}_c = \frac{\text{SE}}{\sqrt{r_{xx}r_{yy}}}$
(drop $r_{yy}$ to correct one variable; the SMD version divides by $\sqrt{r_{xx}}$).
Range restriction, with $U = s_{\text{unrestricted}}/s_{\text{restricted}}$:
$$r_c = \frac{U r}{\sqrt{(U^2-1)r^2 + 1}}, \qquad \text{SE}_c = \frac{r_c}{r}\,\text{SE},$$
and the same formulas for the SMD.

**Conversions** [@harrer_doing_meta].

$$
\text{SMD} = \frac{t(n_1+n_2)}{\sqrt{(n_1+n_2-2)\,n_1n_2}}, \qquad
\text{SMD} = \sqrt{F\,\frac{n_1+n_2}{n_1n_2}\,\frac{n_1+n_2}{n_1+n_2-2}},
$$

$$
\text{SMD} = \frac{r_{pb}}{\sqrt{(1-r_{pb}^2)\,\frac{n_1}{N}\left(1-\frac{n_1}{N}\right)}}
\quad\Big(= \frac{2r_{pb}}{\sqrt{1-r_{pb}^2}} \text{ when } n_1 = n_2\Big), \qquad
\text{SD} = \text{SE}\sqrt n .
$$

*A small discrepancy.* Harrer et al.'s printed $t$ formula gives 0.428 for their example, but their `esc_t`
output prints 0.426. The output matches the simpler $d = t\sqrt{1/n_1 + 1/n_2}$, which differs only by the
factor $\sqrt{(n_1+n_2)/(n_1+n_2-2)}$, close to 1 in large samples. The $F$ example shows the same fourth-decimal
gap. Neither changes any conclusion (our arithmetic).

**From a $p$-value to an SE** (Altman and Bland, as implemented in Harrer et al.'s `se.from.p`). For a
two-sided $p$, $z = -0.862 + \sqrt{0.743 - 2.404\ln p}$ and $\text{SE} = \text{effect}/z$; for ratios, use
the log effect. With $d = 0.71$ and $p = 0.013$: $z = 2.48$, SE = 0.286.

**Odds ratio to risk ratio** (Zhang and Yu), with control risk $p_c = c/n_{\text{control}}$:
$$\text{RR} = \frac{\text{OR}}{(1 - p_c) + p_c\,\text{OR}} .$$
With the study's own $p_c$ this recovers the table's RR exactly: OR 0.521, $p_c$ = 0.469, RR 0.672 (our
arithmetic). Applied to a *pooled* OR, $p_c$ has to be an assumed typical control risk.

**Number needed to treat** [@harrer_doing_meta]. Binary: $\text{NNT} = 1/|p_{\text{treat}} - p_{\text{control}}|$.
From an SMD (as implemented in `{dmetar}`), Kraemer–Kupfer: $\text{NNT} = 1/\big(2\Phi(d/\sqrt2) - 1\big)$;
Furukawa–Leucht: $\text{NNT} = 1/\big(\Phi(d + \Phi^{-1}(\text{CER})) - \text{CER}\big)$, which reproduce 7.27
and 10.62 for $d = 0.245$, CER 0.35.

**Combining two arms** [@harrer_doing_meta]:
$$
n = n_1 + n_2, \quad m = \frac{n_1m_1 + n_2m_2}{n_1+n_2}, \quad
SD = \sqrt{\frac{(n_1-1)SD_1^2 + (n_2-1)SD_2^2 + \frac{n_1n_2}{n_1+n_2}(m_1 - m_2)^2}{n_1+n_2-1}} .
$$

> **In plain words:** the merged SD is the within-arm spread plus the extra spread created by the two arms
> having different means.

:::
