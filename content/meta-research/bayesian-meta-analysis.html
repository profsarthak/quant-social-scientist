Bayesian Meta-Analysis: Priors, Posteriors and Borrowed Strength

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source

The random-effects model you already know, with one addition: you say in advance roughly how big the average effect and the disagreement between studies could plausibly be. In return you get a full probability distribution for both, so you can answer questions like ‘how likely is it that the real effect is too small to matter?’, which a confidence interval cannot answer.

A Bayesian meta-analysis is the ordinary random-effects model with a prior on the pooled effect \mu and on the between-study spread \tau. The model itself stays the same. What changes is the output: a whole posterior distribution for each parameter instead of a point estimate and an interval. That lets you carry the uncertainty about \tau into the answer, state the probability that the effect lies above or below any threshold you care about, and see each study’s estimate pulled toward the average by an amount set by how noisy it is. The price is having to choose the priors, and with only a few studies that choice can change the answer.

Why it matters

Every meta-analysis in the earlier entries is frequentist: it estimates \mu and \tau^2, then reports a confidence interval and a p-value. Harrer et al. point to three things a Bayesian version does better (Williams, Rast & Bürkner; McNeish; Chung et al.) (Harrer et al. 2022):

  • It models the uncertainty in \tau^2 directly. The frequentist random-effects model estimates \tau^2 once and then treats it as known when it pools, which is the problem Knapp–Hartung patches. The Bayesian model averages over every plausible \tau. It can also estimate pooled effects better when the number of studies is small, which in practice it very often is.
  • It gives full posterior distributions for \mu and \tau, so you can state the probability that either lies above or below a chosen value. A 95% confidence interval cannot do that; it only promises that the procedure would capture the true value in 95% of repeated samples.
  • It lets you build in prior knowledge, openly and in a form others can check.

The question a policy-maker actually asks is usually of the second kind: “how likely is it that this programme’s effect is big enough to be worth the money?” A Bayesian meta-analysis answers it directly.

The same model, plus priors

Harrer et al. start from the hierarchy every meta-analysis already has (see multilevel meta-analysis) (Harrer et al. 2022). Each study k reports an observed effect \hat\theta_k, which misses that study’s true effect \theta_k by sampling error. The true effects themselves vary around an overall mean \mu with spread \tau:

\hat\theta_k \sim \mathcal N(\theta_k,\ \sigma_k^2),\qquad \theta_k \sim \mathcal N(\mu,\ \tau^2).

In plain words: each study is a noisy reading of its own true effect, and the true effects are themselves scattered around one average.

Harrer et al. note that there is nothing particularly Bayesian about this yet: it is the random-effects model of earlier entries, and setting \tau^2 = 0 gives the fixed-effect model (Harrer et al. 2022). Integrating out the \theta_k gives the marginal form \hat\theta_k \sim \mathcal N(\mu,\ \sigma_k^2 + \tau^2). What makes it Bayesian is one more line:

(\mu,\ \tau^2) \sim p(\cdot),\qquad \tau^2 > 0,

a prior distribution for the two parameters, saying how plausible different values are before seeing the data (Williams, Rast & Bürkner) (Harrer et al. 2022). This is a Bayesian hierarchical model (Röver; Higgins, Thompson & Spiegelhalter), the same structure as the hierarchical Bayes entry with “schools” replaced by “studies”. It is also the model behind the Bayesian route in network meta-analysis, where the gemtc package chose the priors for you. Here you choose them yourself.

Choosing the priors

With many studies the data swamp almost any sensible prior. With few studies, Harrer et al. warn, the priors can have a considerable effect on the results, so they need choosing with care (Harrer et al. 2022). Their recommendation is weakly informative priors (Williams, Rast & Bürkner):

  • A non-informative prior, usually a uniform distribution, says every value is equally credible.
  • A weakly informative prior says some values are a little more credible than others, without making any specific claim about the value the data should find.

This matches the general advice in the Bayesian machinery: wide but not absurd, smooth rather than boxed, and a half-Cauchy rather than an inverse-gamma for a variance (Lambert 2018).

A prior for \mu. For standardised mean differences it is reasonable to expect the true effect to lie somewhere between −2 and 2, and very unlikely to be 50. A normal prior with mean 0 and variance 1, \mu \sim \mathcal N(0, 1), puts about 95% prior probability on −2 to 2 (Harrer et al. 2022). It is centred on “no effect”, so it leans slightly toward scepticism, but it is wide enough to let the data decide.

A prior for \tau. This one is harder: \tau can never be negative, but it can be close to zero. The usual choice is a Half-Cauchy distribution, a Cauchy cut in half so that it covers only positive values, written \mathcal{HC}(x_0, s) (Harrer et al. 2022). With location x_0 = 0 it peaks at zero, and the scale s sets how far its tail reaches toward large values. The heavy tail is the point: small values of \tau are favoured, but large ones stay possible.

How to pick s? In many meta-analyses \tau is somewhere around 0.3. Setting s = 0.3 puts exactly 50% of the prior below \tau = 0.3 (Williams, Rast & Bürkner) (Harrer et al. 2022). Harrer et al. consider that already a fairly specific assumption, so their worked example takes the more conservative s = 0.5, which spreads the prior further out (Harrer et al. 2022). (Our arithmetic: under \mathcal{HC}(0, 0.5) the prior probability of \tau < 0.3 falls to 34%, and \tau > 1 keeps about 30%.) The complete model is

\hat\theta_k \sim \mathcal N(\theta_k, \sigma_k^2),\quad \theta_k \sim \mathcal N(\mu, \tau^2),\quad \mu \sim \mathcal N(0, 1),\quad \tau \sim \mathcal{HC}(0, 0.5).

Fitting it

Harrer et al. fit the model with the brms package, which writes Bayesian regressions in R’s formula syntax and samples them with the No-U-Turn sampler (NUTS, Hoffman & Gelman), a form of Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (Harrer et al. 2022). A meta-analysis is an intercept-only regression of the effect sizes, weighted by their standard errors, with a random intercept per study:

priors <- c(prior(normal(0, 1),    class = Intercept),   # mu
            prior(cauchy(0, 0.5),  class = sd))          # tau (half-Cauchy, since sd > 0)

m.brm <- brm(TE | se(seTE) ~ 1 + (1 | Author),
             data = ThirdWave, prior = priors, iter = 4000)

The se(seTE) term supplies each study’s known sampling error, and (1 | Author) is the random effect that makes it a random-effects model; drop it and you have a fixed-effect model (Harrer et al. 2022). Expect this to take minutes rather than seconds: sampling is much more expensive than the closed-form estimators of meta and metafor.

Before reading any result, check the sampler. Non-convergence is common in Bayesian models and is often fixed by more iterations (Harrer et al. 2022). Harrer et al. ask for two checks:

  1. \hat R, the potential scale reduction factor from the network entry, should be below 1.01 for every parameter. It compares the variation within and between chains; near 1 means the chains agree.
  2. A posterior predictive check (pp_check): simulate new data sets from the fitted model and compare their distribution with the observed effects. If the model captures the data, the simulated densities should look roughly like the observed one.

A worked example: eighteen trials of third-wave therapy

The data are the ThirdWave set used throughout this track: 18 trials of “third wave” psychotherapies for college students, effects as Hedges’ g (Harrer et al. 2022). With the priors above, brms reports (four chains of 4,000 iterations, 2,000 of them warm-up, so 8,000 posterior draws):

Bayesian random-effects meta-analysis of ThirdWave (Harrer et al. 2022).
Posterior mean Est. error 95% credible interval \hat R
\mu (pooled SMD) 0.57 0.09 0.39 to 0.76 1.00
\tau (between-study SD) 0.29 0.10 0.11 to 0.51 1.00

Both \hat R values are 1, so the results can be read (Harrer et al. 2022). The pooled effect is a moderate SMD of 0.57 with a 95% credible interval of 0.39 to 0.76, and \tau = 0.29 sits close to the “best guess” built into the prior. Put the frequentist analysis from pooling beside it and the two agree closely: REML gives \hat\mu = 0.58 (Knapp–Hartung CI 0.38 to 0.78) and \hat\tau^2 = 0.08, so \hat\tau \approx 0.29. With 18 studies and weakly informative priors, that is what should happen.

There are no p-values in this output. Harrer et al. take that as a feature: you can draw sensible conclusions without significance tests (Harrer et al. 2022). Instead you ask the posterior.

Asking the posterior a question

Suppose earlier research says that effects below SMD = 0.30 are too small to matter. What is the probability that the true pooled effect falls below that line? Take the posterior draws of \mu, build their empirical cumulative distribution function (ECDF), and read it off at 0.30 (Harrer et al. 2022):

post.samples <- posterior_samples(m.brm, c("^b", "^sd"))
names(post.samples) <- c("smd", "tau")
ecdf(post.samples$smd)(0.3)
## [1] 0.002125

About 0.2%. If the 0.30 cut-off is valid, the effect is very likely to be large enough to matter (Harrer et al. 2022). Notice the form of the answer: a probability about the parameter, given the model and the data. A confidence interval cannot give that, and neither can a p-value; see reading the posterior.

The same draws can be pushed one step further (our extension, not in Harrer et al.). Drawing a new true effect \theta_{\text{new}} \sim \mathcal N(\mu, \tau^2) for each posterior draw gives the Bayesian version of the prediction interval: about −0.07 to 1.24, close to the frequentist −0.06 to 1.21. The pooled effect is almost certainly above 0.30, yet a new study’s true effect has roughly an 18% chance of falling below it. The two questions are different, and the posterior answers both.

Borrowing strength: why the forest plot moves

Harrer et al. build a Bayesian forest plot from the posterior draws, with a density for every study (via tidybayes) (Harrer et al. 2022). One thing in it needs explaining: the study estimates are not the observed effects. They are the model’s estimates of each study’s true effect \theta_k, and they sit closer to the pooled mean than the observed values do. The outliers DanitzOrsillo and Shapiro et al. move most visibly (Harrer et al. 2022).

This is shrinkage to the mean, typical of any hierarchical model with a shared overarching distribution. The model supplements what study k says on its own with what all the studies together say about the distribution of true effects. Harrer et al., citing Lunn et al., call this borrowing of strength, and it pulls extreme studies toward the mean more strongly when they carry less information (large standard errors) (Harrer et al. 2022). The figure shows both halves of the story.

y = np.array([r[1] for r in rows])
s2 = ((np.array([r[3] for r in rows]) - np.array([r[2] for r in rows])) / (2 * 1.959964)) ** 2

# --- exact posterior on a (mu, tau) grid: likelihood x N(0,1) prior on mu x Half-Cauchy(0,0.5) prior on tau
mu = np.linspace(-0.5, 1.8, 921)
tau = np.linspace(1e-6, 1.6, 801)
M, T = np.meshgrid(mu, tau, indexing="ij")
logp = -0.5 * M**2 - np.log1p((T / 0.5) ** 2)
for yk, vk in zip(y, s2):
    var = vk + T**2
    logp += -0.5 * np.log(var) - 0.5 * (yk - M) ** 2 / var

post /= post.sum()

theta = np.array([np.sum(((1 - vk / (vk + T**2)) * yk + vk / (vk + T**2) * M) * post)
                  for yk, vk in zip(y, s2)])

With 18 studies the conclusions barely move. Widening the prior on \mu to \mathcal N(0, 100) changes little too (0.57 [0.40, 0.77]).

Now keep only five studies (Call, Cavanagh, de Vibe, Frazier and Kuhlmann, an arbitrary subset chosen for illustration). The posterior for \mu still hovers around 0.31, but its credible interval and the posterior for \tau depend heavily on the prior: \tau’s upper limit is 0.32 under \mathcal{HC}(0, 0.1), 0.53 under \mathcal{HC}(0, 0.5) and 0.76 under the flat prior, and the interval for \mu widens from [0.13, 0.49] to [0.03, 0.61]. Five studies carry little information about a variance, so what you believed beforehand fills the gap. This is exactly the small-k case where Harrer et al. say the priors need choosing with care, and also where the frequentist \hat\tau^2 is least reliable. The Bayesian model does not make the uncertainty disappear. It makes it visible, and the sensitivity table makes it reportable.

Common pitfalls

WarningWhere this goes wrong
  • Reading results before checking convergence. If \hat R is above 1.01 or the posterior predictive check looks wrong, the parameters cannot be trusted. Rerun with more iterations before interpreting anything. (Harrer et al. 2022)
  • Reporting one prior and no sensitivity analysis. With few studies the prior on \tau can drive the result. Show what happens under reasonable alternatives. (Harrer et al. 2022)
  • Mistaking model-based estimates for observed effects. The study rows of a Bayesian forest plot are shrunken estimates of each study’s true effect, not the numbers the studies reported. Say so in the caption. (Harrer et al. 2022)
  • Treating shrinkage as a correction for bias. Shrinkage protects against noise: it pulls noisy studies toward the average. It does nothing about publication bias, and if the average itself is inflated by a filtered literature, every study is pulled toward a biased centre.
  • Calling a uniform prior “objective”. A flat prior on \tau up to some bound is still a choice (of the bound, and of the scale it is flat on), and it puts real weight on implausibly large values. Harrer et al. prefer weakly informative priors. (Harrer et al. 2022; Lambert 2018)
  • Expecting Bayes to rescue a bad meta-analysis. The hierarchical model is the same random-effects model. Its assumptions (exchangeable studies, normal true effects, which Higgins, Thompson & Spiegelhalter consider unnecessarily strict) and its inputs carry over unchanged. (Harrer et al. 2022)

Appendix · the mathematics

The posterior. With the marginal likelihood \hat\theta_k \sim \mathcal N(\mu, \sigma_k^2 + \tau^2) and independent priors,

p(\mu, \tau \mid \hat{\boldsymbol\theta}) \;\propto\; p(\mu)\,p(\tau)\prod_{k=1}^{K} \frac{1}{\sqrt{\sigma_k^2 + \tau^2}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{(\hat\theta_k - \mu)^2}{2(\sigma_k^2 + \tau^2)}\right).

In plain words: the posterior weighs each (\mu, \tau) pair by how plausible it was beforehand and how well it explains every study at once.

With only two parameters, this can be evaluated on a fine grid and normalised by summation, which is what the figure does. MCMC is needed when there are many parameters (meta-regression, multilevel or network models), not for this model.

The Half-Cauchy prior. For \tau \ge 0,

p(\tau) = \frac{2}{\pi s}\,\frac{1}{1 + (\tau/s)^2},\qquad P(\tau < t) = \frac{2}{\pi}\arctan(t/s).

So P(\tau < s) = \tfrac{2}{\pi}\arctan 1 = 0.5: the scale is the prior median, the property Harrer et al. use to pick s = 0.3. For s = 0.5, P(\tau < 0.3) = \tfrac{2}{\pi}\arctan 0.6 = 0.34 and P(\tau > 1) = 1 - \tfrac{2}{\pi}\arctan 2 = 0.30; the tail is heavy enough that the prior has no finite mean.

Shrinkage. Given \mu and \tau, the true effect of study k has a normal conditional posterior,

\theta_k \mid \mu, \tau, \hat\theta_k \;\sim\; \mathcal N\!\Big((1 - B_k)\,\hat\theta_k + B_k\,\mu,\ \ (1 - B_k)\,\sigma_k^2\Big), \qquad B_k = \frac{\sigma_k^2}{\sigma_k^2 + \tau^2}.

In plain words: the estimate of a study’s true effect is a weighted average of what it observed and the overall mean. The noisier the study relative to the spread between studies, the more weight goes to the mean.

Averaging over the posterior of (\mu, \tau) gives the dots in the figure. Posterior-average B_k ranges from 0.18 for de Vibe et al. (SE 0.12) to 0.60 for DanitzOrsillo (SE 0.35) and 0.61 for Ratanasiripong (SE 0.35): the precise study keeps most of its own estimate, and the imprecise ones are pulled more than halfway to \mu. This is the same shrinkage rule as in hierarchical Bayesian models, and the same pull appears in the predicted random effects of frequentist mixed-effects models.

The posterior predictive for a new study. For each posterior draw (\mu^{(s)}, \tau^{(s)}), draw \theta_{\text{new}}^{(s)} \sim \mathcal N(\mu^{(s)}, \tau^{(s)2}). The 2.5% and 97.5% quantiles of these draws give the Bayesian prediction interval, which carries the uncertainty in both \mu and \tau without the t_{K-2} approximation used in the frequentist formula.

ECDF. For draws x_1, \dots, x_S, \hat F(t) = \frac{1}{S}\sum_{s} \mathbf 1\{x_s \le t\}. With 8,000 draws, a probability near 0.002 rests on about 17 draws, so its Monte Carlo error is roughly \sqrt{0.002 \times 0.998/8000} \approx 0.0005. This explains the gap between the printed 0.0021 and the exact 0.0019.

A Bayesian meta-analysis is the ordinary random-effects model with a **prior** on the pooled effect $\mu$ and
on the between-study spread $\tau$. The model itself stays the same. What changes is the output: a whole
**posterior distribution** for each parameter instead of a point estimate and an interval. That lets you
carry the uncertainty about $\tau$ into the answer, state the probability that the effect lies above or below
any threshold you care about, and see each study's estimate **pulled toward the average** by an amount set
by how noisy it is. The price is having to choose the priors, and with only a few studies that choice can
change the answer.
:::

## Why it matters

Every meta-analysis in the earlier entries is **frequentist**: it estimates $\mu$ and $\tau^2$, then reports
a confidence interval and a $p$-value. Harrer et al. point to three things a Bayesian version does better
(Williams, Rast & Bürkner; McNeish; Chung et al.) [@harrer_doing_meta]:

- **It models the uncertainty in $\tau^2$ directly.** The frequentist random-effects model estimates
  $\tau^2$ once and then treats it as known when it pools, which is the problem
  [Knapp–Hartung](pooling-effect-sizes.qmd) patches. The Bayesian model averages over every plausible
  $\tau$. It can also estimate pooled effects better when the number of studies is **small**, which in
  practice it very often is.
- **It gives full posterior distributions** for $\mu$ and $\tau$, so you can state the probability that
  either lies above or below a chosen value. A 95% confidence interval cannot do that; it only promises
  that the procedure would capture the true value in 95% of repeated samples.
- **It lets you build in prior knowledge**, openly and in a form others can check.

The question a policy-maker actually asks is usually of the second kind: "how likely is it that this
programme's effect is big enough to be worth the money?" A Bayesian meta-analysis answers it directly.

## The same model, plus priors

Harrer et al. start from the hierarchy every meta-analysis already has (see
[multilevel meta-analysis](multilevel-meta-analysis.qmd)) [@harrer_doing_meta]. Each study $k$ reports an
observed effect $\hat\theta_k$, which misses that study's true effect $\theta_k$ by sampling error. The true
effects themselves vary around an overall mean $\mu$ with spread $\tau$:

$$\hat\theta_k \sim \mathcal N(\theta_k,\ \sigma_k^2),\qquad \theta_k \sim \mathcal N(\mu,\ \tau^2).$$

> **In plain words:** each study is a noisy reading of its own true effect, and the true effects are
> themselves scattered around one average.

Harrer et al. note that there is nothing particularly Bayesian about this yet: it is the
[random-effects model](pooling-effect-sizes.qmd) of earlier entries, and setting $\tau^2 = 0$ gives the
fixed-effect model [@harrer_doing_meta]. Integrating out the $\theta_k$ gives the **marginal form**
$\hat\theta_k \sim \mathcal N(\mu,\ \sigma_k^2 + \tau^2)$. What makes it Bayesian is one more line:

$$(\mu,\ \tau^2) \sim p(\cdot),\qquad \tau^2 > 0,$$

a **prior distribution** for the two parameters, saying how plausible different values are before seeing
the data (Williams, Rast & Bürkner) [@harrer_doing_meta]. This is a **Bayesian hierarchical model** (Röver;
Higgins, Thompson & Spiegelhalter), the same structure as the
[hierarchical Bayes entry](../02-inference/hierarchical-bayesian-models.qmd) with "schools" replaced by
"studies". It is also the model behind the Bayesian route in
[network meta-analysis](network-meta-analysis.qmd), where the `gemtc` package chose the priors for you. Here
you choose them yourself.

## Choosing the priors

With many studies the data swamp almost any sensible prior. With **few** studies, Harrer et al. warn, the
priors can have a considerable effect on the results, so they need choosing with care
[@harrer_doing_meta]. Their recommendation is **weakly informative priors** (Williams, Rast & Bürkner):

- A **non-informative** prior, usually a uniform distribution, says every value is equally credible.
- A **weakly informative** prior says some values are a little more credible than others, without making
  any specific claim about the value the data should find.

This matches the general advice in [the Bayesian machinery](../02-inference/bayesian-machinery.qmd): wide
but not absurd, smooth rather than boxed, and a half-Cauchy rather than an inverse-gamma for a variance
[@lambert_bayes].

**A prior for $\mu$.** For standardised mean differences it is reasonable to expect the true effect to lie
somewhere between −2 and 2, and very unlikely to be 50. A normal prior with mean 0 and variance 1,
$\mu \sim \mathcal N(0, 1)$, puts about 95% prior probability on −2 to 2 [@harrer_doing_meta]. It is centred
on "no effect", so it leans slightly toward scepticism, but it is wide enough to let the data decide.

**A prior for $\tau$.** This one is harder: $\tau$ can never be negative, but it can be close to zero. The
usual choice is a **Half-Cauchy** distribution, a Cauchy cut in half so that it covers only positive values,
written $\mathcal{HC}(x_0, s)$ [@harrer_doing_meta]. With location $x_0 = 0$ it peaks at zero, and the scale
$s$ sets how far its tail reaches toward large values. The heavy tail is the point: small values of $\tau$
are favoured, but large ones stay possible.

How to pick $s$? In many meta-analyses $\tau$ is somewhere around 0.3. Setting $s = 0.3$ puts exactly 50% of
the prior below $\tau = 0.3$ (Williams, Rast & Bürkner) [@harrer_doing_meta]. Harrer et al. consider that
already a fairly specific assumption, so their worked example takes the more conservative $s = 0.5$, which
spreads the prior further out [@harrer_doing_meta]. (Our arithmetic: under $\mathcal{HC}(0, 0.5)$ the prior
probability of $\tau < 0.3$ falls to 34%, and $\tau > 1$ keeps about 30%.) The complete model is

$$\hat\theta_k \sim \mathcal N(\theta_k, \sigma_k^2),\quad \theta_k \sim \mathcal N(\mu, \tau^2),\quad
\mu \sim \mathcal N(0, 1),\quad \tau \sim \mathcal{HC}(0, 0.5).$$

## Fitting it

Harrer et al. fit the model with the `brms` package, which writes Bayesian regressions in R's formula syntax
and samples them with the No-U-Turn sampler (NUTS, Hoffman & Gelman), a form of
[Hamiltonian Monte Carlo](../02-inference/mcmc-in-depth.qmd) [@harrer_doing_meta]. A meta-analysis is an
intercept-only regression of the effect sizes, weighted by their standard errors, with a random intercept per
study:

```r
priors <- c(prior(normal(0, 1),    class = Intercept),   # mu
            prior(cauchy(0, 0.5),  class = sd))          # tau (half-Cauchy, since sd > 0)

m.brm <- brm(TE | se(seTE) ~ 1 + (1 | Author),
             data = ThirdWave, prior = priors, iter = 4000)
```

The `se(seTE)` term supplies each study's known sampling error, and `(1 | Author)` is the random effect that
makes it a random-effects model; drop it and you have a fixed-effect model [@harrer_doing_meta]. Expect this
to take minutes rather than seconds: sampling is much more expensive than the closed-form estimators of
`meta` and `metafor`.

**Before reading any result, check the sampler.** Non-convergence is common in Bayesian models and is often
fixed by more iterations [@harrer_doing_meta]. Harrer et al. ask for two checks:

1. **$\hat R$**, the potential scale reduction factor from the network entry, should be **below 1.01** for
   every parameter. It compares the variation within and between chains; near 1 means the chains agree.
2. A **posterior predictive check** (`pp_check`): simulate new data sets from the fitted model and compare
   their distribution with the observed effects. If the model captures the data, the simulated densities
   should look roughly like the observed one.

## A worked example: eighteen trials of third-wave therapy

The data are the `ThirdWave` set used throughout this track: 18 trials of "third wave" psychotherapies for
college students, effects as Hedges' $g$ [@harrer_doing_meta]. With the priors above, `brms` reports (four
chains of 4,000 iterations, 2,000 of them warm-up, so 8,000 posterior draws):

| | Posterior mean | Est. error | 95% credible interval | $\hat R$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $\mu$ (pooled SMD) | 0.57 | 0.09 | 0.39 to 0.76 | 1.00 |
| $\tau$ (between-study SD) | 0.29 | 0.10 | 0.11 to 0.51 | 1.00 |

: Bayesian random-effects meta-analysis of `ThirdWave` [@harrer_doing_meta].

Both $\hat R$ values are 1, so the results can be read [@harrer_doing_meta]. The pooled effect is a moderate
SMD of 0.57 with a 95% **credible interval** of 0.39 to 0.76, and $\tau = 0.29$ sits close to the "best
guess" built into the prior. Put the frequentist analysis from [pooling](pooling-effect-sizes.qmd) beside it
and the two agree closely: REML gives $\hat\mu = 0.58$ (Knapp–Hartung CI 0.38 to 0.78) and
$\hat\tau^2 = 0.08$, so $\hat\tau \approx 0.29$. With 18 studies and weakly informative priors, that is what
should happen.

There are no $p$-values in this output. Harrer et al. take that as a feature: you can draw sensible
conclusions without significance tests [@harrer_doing_meta]. Instead you ask the posterior.

### Asking the posterior a question

Suppose earlier research says that effects below SMD = 0.30 are too small to matter. What is the probability
that the true pooled effect falls below that line? Take the posterior draws of $\mu$, build their
**empirical cumulative distribution function** (ECDF), and read it off at 0.30 [@harrer_doing_meta]:

```r
post.samples <- posterior_samples(m.brm, c("^b", "^sd"))
names(post.samples) <- c("smd", "tau")
ecdf(post.samples$smd)(0.3)
## [1] 0.002125
```

About 0.2%. If the 0.30 cut-off is valid, the effect is very likely to be large enough to matter
[@harrer_doing_meta]. Notice the form of the answer: a probability about the parameter, given the model and
the data. A confidence interval cannot give that, and neither can a $p$-value; see
[reading the posterior](../02-inference/bayesian-machinery.qmd).

The same draws can be pushed one step further (our extension, not in Harrer et al.). Drawing a new true
effect $\theta_{\text{new}} \sim \mathcal N(\mu, \tau^2)$ for each posterior draw gives the Bayesian
version of the [prediction interval](between-study-heterogeneity.qmd): about −0.07 to 1.24, close to the
frequentist −0.06 to 1.21. The pooled effect is almost certainly above 0.30, yet a new study's true effect
has roughly an 18% chance of falling below it. The two questions are different, and the posterior answers
both.

### Borrowing strength: why the forest plot moves

Harrer et al. build a Bayesian **forest plot** from the posterior draws, with a density for every study (via
`tidybayes`) [@harrer_doing_meta]. One thing in it needs explaining: the study estimates are **not** the
observed effects. They are the model's estimates of each study's *true* effect $\theta_k$, and they sit
closer to the pooled mean than the observed values do. The outliers DanitzOrsillo and Shapiro et al. move
most visibly [@harrer_doing_meta].

This is **shrinkage to the mean**, typical of any hierarchical model with a shared overarching distribution.
The model supplements what study $k$ says on its own with what all the studies together say about the
distribution of true effects. Harrer et al., citing Lunn et al., call this **borrowing of strength**, and it
pulls extreme studies toward the mean more strongly when they carry **less information** (large standard
errors) [@harrer_doing_meta]. The figure shows both halves of the story.

```{python}
    bx.plot(y[k], i, "o", mfc="none", mec="#5e5e54", ms=6)
    bx.plot(theta[k], i, "o", color="#161613", ms=5)
bx.axvline(mu_mean, color="#e8531d", lw=1.6)
bx.text(mu_mean + 0.03, len(y) - 0.4, f"μ = {mu_mean:.2f}", color="#e8531d", fontsize=8.5)
bx.set_yticks(range(len(y)))
bx.set_yticklabels([names[k] for k in order], fontsize=7.5)
bx.set_ylim(-0.8, len(y) - 0.2)
bx.set_xlabel("Hedges' g   (open = observed, dot = model's true-effect estimate)")
bx.set_title("Borrowing strength: every study pulled toward μ", fontsize=10)
fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

## How much does the prior matter?

Harrer et al.'s advice is to **always run a sensitivity analysis** with different priors and check whether
the results change materially [@harrer_doing_meta]. Here is what that looks like for these data (our
computation, using the same exact grid as the figure):

| Prior on $\tau$ | $\mu$: mean [95% CrI] | $\tau$: mean [95% CrI] |
|---|---|---|
| $\mathcal{HC}(0, 0.1)$, fairly sure heterogeneity is small | 0.56 [0.40, 0.74] | 0.24 [0.05, 0.46] |
| $\mathcal{HC}(0, 0.3)$ | 0.57 [0.40, 0.76] | 0.28 [0.10, 0.49] |
| $\mathcal{HC}(0, 0.5)$, Harrer et al.'s choice | 0.57 [0.39, 0.76] | 0.29 [0.11, 0.51] |
| $\mathcal{HC}(0, 1)$ | 0.57 [0.39, 0.77] | 0.30 [0.12, 0.54] |
| Flat on 0 to 2.5 | 0.57 [0.39, 0.77] | 0.31 [0.13, 0.55] |

: Prior sensitivity, all 18 studies, $\mu \sim \mathcal N(0, 1)$ throughout (our computation).

With 18 studies the conclusions barely move. Widening the prior on $\mu$ to $\mathcal N(0, 100)$ changes
little too (0.57 [0.40, 0.77]).

Now keep only **five** studies (Call, Cavanagh, de Vibe, Frazier and Kuhlmann, an arbitrary subset chosen
for illustration). The posterior for $\mu$ still hovers around 0.31, but its credible interval and the
posterior for $\tau$ depend heavily on the prior: $\tau$'s upper limit is 0.32 under $\mathcal{HC}(0, 0.1)$,
0.53 under $\mathcal{HC}(0, 0.5)$ and 0.76 under the flat prior, and the interval for $\mu$ widens from
[0.13, 0.49] to [0.03, 0.61]. Five studies carry little information about a variance, so what you believed
beforehand fills the gap. This is exactly the small-$k$ case where Harrer et al. say the priors need choosing
with care, and also where the frequentist $\hat\tau^2$ is least reliable. The Bayesian model does not make
the uncertainty disappear. It makes it **visible**, and the sensitivity table makes it reportable.

## Common pitfalls

::: {.callout-warning title="Where this goes wrong"}
- **Reading results before checking convergence.** If $\hat R$ is above 1.01 or the posterior predictive
  check looks wrong, the parameters cannot be trusted. Rerun with more iterations before interpreting
  anything. [@harrer_doing_meta]
- **Reporting one prior and no sensitivity analysis.** With few studies the prior on $\tau$ can drive the
  result. Show what happens under reasonable alternatives. [@harrer_doing_meta]
- **Mistaking model-based estimates for observed effects.** The study rows of a Bayesian forest plot are
  shrunken estimates of each study's true effect, not the numbers the studies reported. Say so in the
  caption. [@harrer_doing_meta]
- **Treating shrinkage as a correction for bias.** Shrinkage protects against noise: it pulls noisy
  studies toward the average. It does nothing about [publication bias](publication-bias-methods.qmd), and
  if the average itself is inflated by a filtered literature, every study is pulled toward a biased centre.
- **Calling a uniform prior "objective".** A flat prior on $\tau$ up to some bound is still a choice (of the
  bound, and of the scale it is flat on), and it puts real weight on implausibly large values. Harrer et al.
  prefer weakly informative priors. [@harrer_doing_meta; @lambert_bayes]
- **Expecting Bayes to rescue a bad meta-analysis.** The hierarchical model is the same random-effects
  model. Its assumptions (exchangeable studies, normal true effects, which Higgins, Thompson & Spiegelhalter
  consider unnecessarily strict) and its inputs carry over unchanged. [@harrer_doing_meta]
:::

## Appendix · the mathematics {.unnumbered}

::: {.math-appendix}

**The posterior.** With the marginal likelihood $\hat\theta_k \sim \mathcal N(\mu, \sigma_k^2 + \tau^2)$ and
independent priors,

$$p(\mu, \tau \mid \hat{\boldsymbol\theta}) \;\propto\; p(\mu)\,p(\tau)\prod_{k=1}^{K}
\frac{1}{\sqrt{\sigma_k^2 + \tau^2}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{(\hat\theta_k - \mu)^2}{2(\sigma_k^2 + \tau^2)}\right).$$

> **In plain words:** the posterior weighs each $(\mu, \tau)$ pair by how plausible it was beforehand and how
> well it explains every study at once.

With only two parameters, this can be evaluated on a fine grid and normalised by summation, which is what
the figure does. MCMC is needed when there are many parameters (meta-regression, multilevel or network
models), not for this model.

**The Half-Cauchy prior.** For $\tau \ge 0$,

$$p(\tau) = \frac{2}{\pi s}\,\frac{1}{1 + (\tau/s)^2},\qquad P(\tau < t) = \frac{2}{\pi}\arctan(t/s).$$

So $P(\tau < s) = \tfrac{2}{\pi}\arctan 1 = 0.5$: the scale is the prior median, the property Harrer et al.
use to pick $s = 0.3$. For $s = 0.5$, $P(\tau < 0.3) = \tfrac{2}{\pi}\arctan 0.6 = 0.34$ and
$P(\tau > 1) = 1 - \tfrac{2}{\pi}\arctan 2 = 0.30$; the tail is heavy enough that the prior has no finite mean.

**Shrinkage.** Given $\mu$ and $\tau$, the true effect of study $k$ has a normal conditional posterior,

$$\theta_k \mid \mu, \tau, \hat\theta_k \;\sim\; \mathcal N\!\Big((1 - B_k)\,\hat\theta_k + B_k\,\mu,\ \ (1 - B_k)\,\sigma_k^2\Big),
\qquad B_k = \frac{\sigma_k^2}{\sigma_k^2 + \tau^2}.$$

> **In plain words:** the estimate of a study's true effect is a weighted average of what it observed and the
> overall mean. The noisier the study relative to the spread between studies, the more weight goes to the
> mean.

Averaging over the posterior of $(\mu, \tau)$ gives the dots in the figure. Posterior-average $B_k$ ranges
from 0.18 for de Vibe et al. (SE 0.12) to 0.60 for DanitzOrsillo (SE 0.35) and 0.61 for Ratanasiripong
(SE 0.35): the precise study keeps most of its own estimate, and the imprecise ones are pulled more than
halfway to $\mu$. This is the same shrinkage rule as in [hierarchical Bayesian
models](../02-inference/hierarchical-bayesian-models.qmd), and the same pull appears in the predicted
random effects of frequentist [mixed-effects models](../03-regression/mixed-effects-models.qmd).

**The posterior predictive for a new study.** For each posterior draw $(\mu^{(s)}, \tau^{(s)})$, draw
$\theta_{\text{new}}^{(s)} \sim \mathcal N(\mu^{(s)}, \tau^{(s)2})$. The 2.5% and 97.5% quantiles of these
draws give the Bayesian prediction interval, which carries the uncertainty in both $\mu$ and $\tau$ without
the $t_{K-2}$ approximation used in the frequentist formula.

**ECDF.** For draws $x_1, \dots, x_S$, $\hat F(t) = \frac{1}{S}\sum_{s} \mathbf 1\{x_s \le t\}$. With 8,000
draws, a probability near 0.002 rests on about 17 draws, so its Monte Carlo error is roughly
$\sqrt{0.002 \times 0.998/8000} \approx 0.0005$. This explains the gap between the printed 0.0021 and the
exact 0.0019.

:::
