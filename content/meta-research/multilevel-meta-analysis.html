Multilevel Meta-Analysis: When Studies Give You More Than One Effect

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source

Real studies rarely report a single effect. They measure the outcome three ways, follow two groups, or compare two treatments with one control. Those effects are not independent, and pooling them as if they were makes the evidence look stronger than it is. Three-level models, robust standard errors and multivariate models are the tools for keeping the count honest.

Every meta-analysis is already a multilevel model: people are nested in studies, and the random-effects model has one level for each. When a study contributes several effect sizes, add a third level. The effects are nested in studies, and the model estimates two kinds of heterogeneity, within studies and between them. Treating dependent effects as independent understates the uncertainty, so confidence intervals come out too narrow and false positives become more likely. When even the three-level model may be wrong about how the effects are correlated, robust (sandwich) standard errors protect the inference. When the several effects are different outcomes, a multivariate meta-analysis pools them together and estimates how they move together.

Why it matters

The pooling machinery of the earlier entries rests on one assumption that real data break all the time: each effect size is independent of the others (Harrer et al. 2022). If two effects are correlated, the model counts them as two separate pieces of evidence when they are partly one. Heterogeneity looks smaller than it is, the pooled effect looks more precise than it is, and false positives follow. Harrer et al. call this the unit-of-analysis error, the problem the effect-size entry met with multi-arm trials and several instruments per study.

Dependence comes from two places, following Cheung (Harrer et al. 2022):

  • From the studies’ authors. A study collects data at several sites, compares several treatments with one control group, or measures the same outcome with several questionnaires. Each gives more than one effect size, and the effects from one study are related.
  • From the meta-analyst’s own design. A meta-analysis of a psychological mechanism includes studies from East Asian and Western European societies. If the mechanism depends on culture, studies from the same region will resemble each other more than studies from different regions.

The older remedies (pick one effect per study, average them, split the shared control group) are covered in effect sizes for synthesis. They work, but they throw information away or need a guess about the correlation. This entry covers the third option: keep every effect and model the dependence.

Every meta-analysis is already multilevel

Harrer et al. put “multilevel” in quotation marks in their chapter title, and their reason is the first idea to grasp. Calling a meta-analysis “multilevel” suggests something special, but every meta-analytic model assumes a multilevel structure (Pastor and Lazowski) (Harrer et al. 2022). You have been fitting one all along.

The random-effects model says an observed effect is the overall mean plus two errors, \hat\theta_k = \mu + \zeta_k + \epsilon_k. Split it into its two levels:

  • Level 1, participants. Study k’s observed effect misses its own true effect by sampling error: \hat\theta_k = \theta_k + \epsilon_k. The participants are pooled at this level, usually by the study’s authors, who report a mean and SD rather than raw data.
  • Level 2, studies. Each study’s true effect is drawn from a distribution of true effects around the overall mean: \theta_k = \mu + \zeta_k. This pooling is the meta-analysis.

Substitute the second line into the first and you are back at the random-effects model. Set \zeta_k to zero and you have the fixed-effect model (Harrer et al. 2022). Participants are nested in studies, just as pupils are nested in schools in a mixed-effects model, and the random-effects model is that model’s random intercept.

The three-level model

Once the two levels are visible, adding a third is natural. Insert a layer of clusters between the effect sizes and the overall mean (Harrer et al. 2022):

  • Level 1: each observed effect \hat\theta_{ij} (effect i in cluster j) misses its true effect by sampling error.
  • Level 2: the true effects within cluster j scatter around that cluster’s own average \kappa_j.
  • Level 3: the cluster averages scatter around the overall mean \mu.

A cluster can be a study that contributes several effect sizes, or a group of studies (a cultural region, a research lab) where each study gives one effect. Either way the model now has two heterogeneity variances to estimate instead of one: \tau^2_{\text{Level 2}}, the spread of true effects within clusters, and \tau^2_{\text{Level 3}}, the spread of cluster averages between them (Harrer et al. 2022). The overall mean \mu means much the same as in a random-effects model; what changes is that it now comes from a model that knows which effects belong together.

Why does this fix the double-counting? Effects in one cluster share their cluster’s deviation. If a study happened to sample unusually responsive people, all of its effects are high together. The model sees that, treats the study’s several effects as partly one piece of information, and weights them accordingly. In the same way, hierarchical Bayesian models let groups borrow strength without pretending to be more independent than they are.

A worked example: Chernobyl and mutation rates

Harrer et al.’s example is loosely based on a real meta-analysis by Møller and Mousseau of the correlation between ionising radiation from the 1986 Chernobyl disaster and mutation rates in humans (Harrer et al. 2022). It has 33 effect sizes from 14 studies. Most studies give more than one, because they measured mutations by several methods or studied several kinds of people (exposed parents and their children, for instance). The effects are correlations, pooled on the Fisher’s z scale.

The three-level model (REML, with a t-test) gives (Harrer et al. 2022):

The three-level Chernobyl model (Harrer et al. 2022).
Estimate
Pooled effect z = 0.52 (95% CI 0.25 to 0.80), so r \approx 0.48 (0.24 to 0.66)
\tau^2_{\text{Level 3}} (between studies, 14 clusters) 0.179
\tau^2_{\text{Level 2}} (within studies, 33 effects) 0.119
Share of total variance: sampling error / within / between 1% / 40% / 59%

A correlation of about 0.48 is large: a substantial association between radiation exposure and mutation rates. The heterogeneity test is significant, but Harrer et al. call it not very informative. The useful question is where the heterogeneity lives.

That is what the last row answers. In an ordinary meta-analysis, I^2 is the share of variation that sampling error doesn’t explain. In a three-level model it splits in two (Cheung): I^2_{\text{Level 2}} is the share due to true differences within clusters and I^2_{\text{Level 3}} the share due to differences between clusters (Harrer et al. 2022). Here sampling error is about 1% of the total, the within-study share about 40% and the between-study share about 59%. Most of the disagreement is between studies, but more than a third is between effects in the same study. A model that ignored that layer would be missing a large part of the picture.

Is the third level worth it?

A three-level model only makes sense if it describes the data better than a two-level one. If both fit equally well, Occam’s razor favours the simpler model (Harrer et al. 2022). To check, fit the model again with one variance forced to zero and compare. Forcing \tau^2_{\text{Level 3}} = 0 gives back the ordinary random-effects model that treats all 33 effects as independent, which we know they are not.

Removing the study level from the Chernobyl model (Harrer et al. 2022).
Model Pooled z (95% CI) SE AIC log-likelihood
Three-level 0.52 (0.25 to 0.80) 0.134 48.24 −21.12
Two-level, effects treated as independent 0.60 (0.38 to 0.81) 0.105 62.34 −29.17

The three-level model wins on AIC and BIC, and the likelihood-ratio test agrees (\chi^2_1 = 16.1, p < 0.001). The extra parameter is justified (Harrer et al. 2022). Notice also what the reduced model does to the uncertainty: its standard error is a fifth smaller and its interval narrower. Ignoring the nesting made the evidence look stronger than it is, which is the unit-of-analysis error in numbers.

The test is not the only consideration. Harrer et al. would keep the three-level model even if the test had favoured two levels whenever there is a solid theoretical reason for it (Harrer et al. 2022). Effects from the same study cannot be independent, so the nested model better represents how the data were generated, and a non-significant test would only mean the effects within studies happen to be fairly similar. The case is different when the cluster is a hunch. If level 3 were cultural region and the effect did not vary between regions, dropping that level would be fine.

Moderators in a three-level model

Subgroup analysis and meta-regression carry over directly: add predictors to the three-level model and it becomes a three-level mixed-effects model (Harrer et al. 2022). In the Chernobyl data, do correlations differ with the overall radiation dose of the sample (low, medium, high)? The test of moderators says no, F(2, 28) = 0.45, p = 0.64. As in any meta-regression, the coefficients are differences from a reference group. The intercept, z = 0.58, is the high-radiation group; the low group is 0.58 - 0.19 = 0.39 and the medium group 0.58 + 0.20 = 0.78.

To report a three-level model, give at least the pooled effect and both variance components. Harrer et al. suggest writing them as \tau^2_{\text{Level 3}} and \tau^2_{\text{Level 2}}, which says plainly that they are heterogeneity variances (the software calls them \sigma^2_1 and \sigma^2_2), plus the multilevel I^2 values, explained, and the likelihood-ratio test if you compared models (Harrer et al. 2022).

When the dependence is messier than the model

The three-level model is better than pretending effects are independent, but it is still a simplification. It assumes the sampling errors of effects within a study are uncorrelated, and often they are not (Harrer et al. 2022). The Chernobyl studies give multiple effects for different reasons. Some compared different target populations, which gives separate samples. Others measured the same sample by several methods, and effects computed from the same people have correlated sampling errors. The basic three-level model has no term for that.

The correlated and hierarchical effects (CHE) model

Pustejovsky and Tipton’s correlated and hierarchical effects model keeps the clusters of the three-level model and adds a correlation \rho between the sampling errors of effects in the same study (Harrer et al. 2022). The true correlations are almost never reported, so the model makes a simplifying assumption: one constant sampling correlation, the same within and across all studies. Harrer et al. call CHE a good starting point, especially when the dependence is complex or only partly known.

The cost is that you have to supply \rho. For Chernobyl, Harrer et al. assume a large correlation, \rho = 0.6, and say plainly that it is no more than a guess. Run sensitivity analyses over a range of values (Harrer et al. 2022).

Robust variance estimation: the sandwich

Because the CHE model’s assumptions are crude, its standard errors are computed with robust variance estimation (RVE). Harrer et al. point out that “robust variance estimation” is sometimes written about as if it were a special meta-analysis method for dependent effects. It is not. It is the sandwich estimator from ordinary regression, adapted to meta-analysis by Hedges, Tipton and Johnson (Harrer et al. 2022). It gives a consistent standard error even when the model’s assumptions are not met. It is the same tool as heteroskedasticity-robust and clustered standard errors in regression.

The name comes from its shape: two identical slices of “bread” around a “meat” in the middle. The bread is the model’s own idea of how precise the estimate is. The meat is what the residuals actually show, summed cluster by cluster, so whatever the true correlation within a study is, it gets counted (Harrer et al. 2022). If the model’s covariance assumptions are wrong, the meat corrects for it. With few studies (say 40 or fewer) the plain sandwich is too optimistic, and the recommended small-sample correction is Tipton and Pustejovsky’s CR2 adjustment.

The point, in Harrer et al.’s words, is to guard the inference against misspecification. Multilevel models are easy to get wrong, and RVE keeps the confidence intervals and p-values honest even if the assumed correlation structure is off (Harrer et al. 2022).

In the Chernobyl example the CHE model with CR2 standard errors gives point estimates close to the three-level moderator model’s (high radiation 0.58, low −0.19, medium +0.21) but much larger standard errors, with confidence intervals for the intercept running from −6.8 to 7.9 (Harrer et al. 2022). Nothing is significant. The reason is in the degrees of freedom column, about 1 to 2 per coefficient: robust inference is estimated cluster by cluster, and the high-radiation group contains very few studies. With so little information per coefficient, RVE becomes very conservative.

Cluster wild bootstrapping

For small numbers of studies, where RVE can be overly conservative, as in this example, Joshi, Pustejovsky and Beretvas suggest cluster wild bootstrapping (Harrer et al. 2022). It is also the natural way to test a multiple-contrast hypothesis, such as whether a three-level categorical moderator matters at all. The algorithm:

  1. Fit the full model to the real data and compute the test statistic.
  2. Fit a null model (without the covariates being tested) and take its residuals.
  3. For each study, draw one random value and multiply all of that study’s residuals by it. The whole cluster moves together, so the within-study dependence is preserved.
  4. Add the transformed residuals to the null model’s predictions to make a new set of effect sizes.
  5. Refit the full model and recompute the test statistic.

Repeat steps 3 to 5 many times (2,000 in Harrer et al.’s example). The p-value is the share of bootstrap statistics more extreme than the real one (Harrer et al. 2022). Testing whether radiation dose moderates the Chernobyl correlations, holding publication year fixed, gives p = 0.36, in line with the earlier moderator test. The logic is that of the bootstrap, resampling whole clusters’ residuals rather than single data points.

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# --- Left: Harrer et al. §10.2, printed variance components and pooled effects (Fisher's z)
models = ["three-level", "two-level\n(independent)"]
between = [0.1788, 0.0]
within = [0.1194, 0.3550]
est = [(0.5231, 0.2500, 0.7963), (0.5985, 0.3844, 0.8126)]

# --- Right: simulation. 14 studies give 33 effects; correlated sampling errors within a study
rng = np.random.default_rng(2026)
k_j = np.array([4, 1, 3, 2, 4, 1, 2, 3, 2, 3, 1, 2, 3, 2])
J = len(k_j)
cl = np.repeat(np.arange(J), k_j)
n = cl.size
Z = (cl[:, None] == np.arange(J)).astype(float)
ZZ = Z @ Z.T
mu, t3, t2, rho = 0.40, 0.06, 0.03, 0.6
v = rng.uniform(0.02, 0.08, J)[cl]                       # each study's sampling variance
S = np.diag(v) + rho * np.sqrt(np.outer(v, v)) * (ZZ - np.eye(n))
L = np.linalg.cholesky(S + t3 * ZZ + t2 * np.eye(n))   # true covariance of the 33 effects
X = np.ones((n, 1))

def reml(y, comps):
    """REML by Fisher scoring for variance components; returns mean, model SE, V^-1, (X'V^-1 X)^-1."""
    th = np.full(len(comps), 0.03)
    for _ in range(200):
        Vi = np.linalg.inv(np.diag(v) + sum(t * D for t, D in zip(th, comps)))
        P = Vi - Vi @ X @ np.linalg.inv(X.T @ Vi @ X) @ X.T @ Vi
        Py = P @ y
        score = np.array([0.5 * (Py @ D @ Py - np.trace(P @ D)) for D in comps])
        info = np.array([[0.5 * np.trace(P @ Di @ P @ Dj) for Dj in comps] for Di in comps])
        new = np.maximum(th + np.linalg.solve(info, score), 0)
        done = np.max(np.abs(new - th)) < 1e-8
        th = new
        if done:
            break
    Vi = np.linalg.inv(np.diag(v) + sum(t * D for t, D in zip(th, comps)))
    A = np.linalg.inv(X.T @ Vi @ X)
    return (A @ X.T @ Vi @ y)[0], np.sqrt(A[0, 0]), Vi, A[0, 0]

hits = np.zeros(3)

Several outcomes at once: multivariate meta-analysis

Sometimes a study’s several effects are not repeated measures of one outcome but different outcomes, each worth estimating in its own right. A treatment may be judged on two outcomes that most trials measure. A multivariate meta-analysis estimates both pooled effects in one model and uses the correlation between them (Harrer et al. 2022). It also answers a new question: do studies with a large effect on one outcome tend to have a large effect on the other?

Harrer et al. fit it from a structural equation modelling angle, building on the idea above: if every meta-analysis is a multilevel model, and multilevel models are a special case of SEM (Mehta and Neale; Bauer), then a meta-analysis can be written as a SEM in which the true effect is a latent variable (Cheung) (Harrer et al. 2022). Multivariate meta-analysis can also be done outside SEM. For the example they extend the third-wave psychotherapy trials (stress outcomes, see pooling) with each trial’s effect on anxiety, its standard error, and the covariance between the two outcomes. One trial did not measure anxiety; the maximum-likelihood fit still uses its stress effect.

Both effects are significant and both are heterogeneous. The new number is the correlation between the studies’ true effects, 0.45: treatments that reduce stress more also tend to reduce anxiety more (Harrer et al. 2022). It comes from the between-study covariance matrix, which has a heterogeneity variance for each outcome on the diagonal and their covariance off it.

The usual practical obstacle is that primary studies rarely report the covariance between their outcomes. Then it has to be imputed from an assumed correlation, ideally from previous literature in a similar context: the covariance is approximately SE_1 \times SE_2 \times \rho (Harrer et al. 2022). Harrer et al. take \rho \approx 0.6 for stress and anxiety in clinical trials. The assumed correlation can change the results a lot, so always report it and rerun the analysis with other plausible values.

Meta-analytic SEM: pooling correlation matrices

The SEM view goes further than two outcomes. Two-stage structural equation modelling (TSSEM; Cheung, Tang and Cheung) first pools each study’s correlation matrix in a multivariate random-effects model, and then fits a structural model to the pooled matrix by weighted least squares (Harrer et al. 2022). Studies with some correlations missing can still contribute to stage 1.

Harrer et al.’s example is a meta-analytic confirmatory factor analysis of a fictitious sleep questionnaire, with simulated correlation matrices from 11 studies (N = 3{,}272). The hypothesis, after Koffel and Watson, is that five symptoms reflect two latent factors: sleep quality, latency and efficiency load on insomnia, and daytime dysfunction and hypersomnia on lassitude (Harrer et al. 2022). Stage 1 pools the ten correlations (I^2 between 65% and 93%). Stage 2 fits the two-factor model: loadings from 0.61 to 0.79, a factor correlation of 0.33, and a non-significant misfit test (\chi^2_4 = 5.26, p = 0.26) with RMSEA 0.0098, well under the 0.05 rule of thumb for good fit.

One feature of SEM is that the logic of testing is reversed. A t-test aims to reject its null; a SEM proposes a model and hopes the fit test does not reject it (Harrer et al. 2022). That invites a trap Harrer et al. spell out: a well-fitting model does not prove the theory, because other models may fit the same data just as well. Test plausible alternatives too. The factor analysis entry covers the measurement side in detail.

Common pitfalls

WarningWhere this goes wrong
  • Pooling several effects per study as if they were independent. The unit-of-analysis error: too little heterogeneity, too-narrow intervals and false positives. Model the nesting or reduce to one effect per study. (Harrer et al. 2022)
  • Dropping the third level because a likelihood-ratio test says so. When effects come from the same study, the dependence is known, so keep the three-level model even if the test is not significant. Drop a level only when the clustering variable was a guess (such as cultural region) and shows no variation. (Harrer et al. 2022)
  • Reporting one \tau^2. A three-level model has two variance components. Report both, as \tau^2_{\text{Level 2}} and \tau^2_{\text{Level 3}}, with the multilevel I^2 values. (Harrer et al. 2022)
  • Forgetting that the three-level model assumes uncorrelated sampling errors. Effects measured on the same participants have correlated errors. Use a CHE model or robust standard errors. (Harrer et al. 2022)
  • Treating an assumed correlation as data. The \rho in a CHE model, or the correlation used to impute covariances in a multivariate model, is usually a guess. Report it and run sensitivity analyses over plausible values. (Harrer et al. 2022)
  • Using the plain sandwich with few studies. It is too optimistic with about 40 studies or fewer. Use the CR2 small-sample correction, and with very few clusters per coefficient consider the cluster wild bootstrap. (Harrer et al. 2022)
  • Taking a good SEM fit as proof. Other models may fit just as well. Fit the alternatives. (Harrer et al. 2022)

Appendix · the mathematics

The three-level model. For effect i in cluster j with known sampling variance v_{ij},

\hat\theta_{ij} = \mu + \zeta_{(3)j} + \zeta_{(2)ij} + \epsilon_{ij},\qquad \zeta_{(3)j}\sim\mathcal N(0,\tau^2_{(3)}),\ \ \zeta_{(2)ij}\sim\mathcal N(0,\tau^2_{(2)}),\ \ \epsilon_{ij}\sim\mathcal N(0,v_{ij}).

Stack cluster j’s n_j effects into \hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_j. Their covariance matrix is

\mathbf V_j = \operatorname{diag}(v_{1j},\dots,v_{n_jj}) + \tau^2_{(2)}\,\mathbf I_{n_j} + \tau^2_{(3)}\,\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top .

In plain words: two effects from the same cluster share the cluster’s deviation, so their covariance is \tau^2_{(3)}; effects from different clusters are uncorrelated.

So the correlation between two true effects in one cluster is \tau^2_{(3)}/(\tau^2_{(3)}+\tau^2_{(2)}), the intraclass correlation of the mixed-effects entry. For Chernobyl, 0.1788/0.2982 = 0.60. Given the variances, \hat\mu = \sum_j\mathbf 1^\top\mathbf V_j^{-1}\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_j \big/ \sum_j \mathbf 1^\top\mathbf V_j^{-1}\mathbf 1 with model-based variance 1/\sum_j \mathbf 1^\top\mathbf V_j^{-1}\mathbf 1. The variances are estimated by REML (the figure uses Fisher scoring). Setting \tau^2_{(3)} = 0 gives the ordinary random-effects model on all effects.

Model comparison. \text{LRT} = 2(\ell_{\text{full}} - \ell_{\text{reduced}}) = 2(-21.12 + 29.17) = 16.10 on 1 df, and \text{AIC} = -2\ell + 2p: 42.24 + 6 = 48.24 and 58.34 + 4 = 62.34.

Multilevel I^2 (our algebra, following Cheung’s definition as a share of total variation). With a typical sampling variance \tilde v,

I^2_{\text{Level 3}} = \frac{\tau^2_{(3)}}{\tau^2_{(3)} + \tau^2_{(2)} + \tilde v},\qquad I^2_{\text{Level 2}} = \frac{\tau^2_{(2)}}{\tau^2_{(3)} + \tau^2_{(2)} + \tilde v}.

The printed 59.22% for level 3 implies a total of 0.1788/0.5922 = 0.3019, so \tilde v \approx 0.0037 (1.2% of the total) and I^2_{\text{Level 2}} = 0.1194/0.3019 \approx 39.5\%, consistent with the printed shares. Fisher’s z has variance 1/(n-3), so \tilde v \approx 0.0037 corresponds to studies of a few hundred people.

The CHE working model. Add a constant correlation \rho between sampling errors in one study: \operatorname{Cov}(\epsilon_{ij}, \epsilon_{i'j}) = \rho\sqrt{v_{ij}v_{i'j}} for i\ne i'. This is the covariance the figure’s simulation draws from, with \rho = 0.6.

The sandwich estimator. For meta-regression \mathbf T_j = \mathbf X_j\boldsymbol\beta + \mathbf u_j + \mathbf e_j with working weights \mathbf W_j,

\mathbf V^{\text{R}}_{\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}} = \Big(\sum_j \mathbf X_j^\top\mathbf W_j\mathbf X_j\Big)^{-1} \Big(\sum_j \mathbf X_j^\top\mathbf W_j\mathbf A_j\boldsymbol\Phi_j\mathbf A_j\mathbf W_j\mathbf X_j\Big) \Big(\sum_j \mathbf X_j^\top\mathbf W_j\mathbf X_j\Big)^{-1},

where \boldsymbol\Phi_j = \operatorname{Var}(\mathbf u_j + \mathbf e_j) is the true within-cluster covariance, estimated in practice by the cluster’s residual cross-product \mathbf e_j\mathbf e_j^\top, \mathbf W_j the model’s inverse covariance and \mathbf A_j the small-sample adjustment (CR2) (Harrer et al. 2022).

In plain words: the bread is the model’s own variance; the meat replaces the model’s guess about how the effects co-vary with what each cluster’s residuals actually do.

If the model is right, the meat averages to the bread’s inverse and the sandwich collapses to the model-based variance. The figure uses the simpler CR1 correction, multiplying by J/(J-1) with J clusters and using t_{J-1}. See regression diagnostics for the same estimator with clusters of people.

Multivariate random effects. Study k’s vector of p outcome effects is \hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_k \sim \mathcal N(\boldsymbol\mu,\ \mathbf S_k + \mathbf T), with \mathbf S_k the known within-study covariance (variances on the diagonal, the reported or imputed covariance \widehat{\text{Cov}} = SE_1 SE_2\,\hat\rho off it) and \mathbf T the between-study covariance. A study missing an outcome contributes the matching sub-vector and sub-matrix. For ThirdWave the ML estimate (our refit) is

\hat{\boldsymbol\mu} = \begin{pmatrix}0.570\\0.407\end{pmatrix},\qquad \hat{\mathbf T} = \begin{pmatrix}0.0733 & 0.0289\\ 0.0289 & 0.0575\end{pmatrix},\qquad \hat\rho_{\text{between}} = \frac{0.0289}{\sqrt{0.0733\times0.0575}} = 0.446,

## Why it matters

The pooling machinery of the [earlier entries](pooling-effect-sizes.qmd) rests on one assumption that real
data break all the time: **each effect size is independent** of the others [@harrer_doing_meta]. If two
effects are correlated, the model counts them as two separate pieces of evidence when they are partly one.
Heterogeneity looks smaller than it is, the pooled effect looks more precise than it is, and false positives
follow. Harrer et al. call this the **unit-of-analysis error**, the problem the [effect-size
entry](effect-sizes-for-synthesis.qmd) met with multi-arm trials and several instruments per study.

Dependence comes from two places, following Cheung [@harrer_doing_meta]:

- **From the studies' authors.** A study collects data at several sites, compares several treatments with
  one control group, or measures the same outcome with several questionnaires. Each gives more than one effect
  size, and the effects from one study are related.
- **From the meta-analyst's own design.** A meta-analysis of a psychological mechanism includes studies from
  East Asian and Western European societies. If the mechanism depends on culture, studies from the same region
  will resemble each other more than studies from different regions.

The older remedies (pick one effect per study, average them, split the shared control group) are covered in
[effect sizes for synthesis](effect-sizes-for-synthesis.qmd). They work, but they throw information away or
need a guess about the correlation. This entry covers the third option: keep every effect and **model** the
dependence.

## Every meta-analysis is already multilevel

Harrer et al. put "multilevel" in quotation marks in their chapter title, and their reason is the first idea
to grasp. Calling a meta-analysis "multilevel" suggests something special, but **every** meta-analytic model
assumes a multilevel structure (Pastor and Lazowski) [@harrer_doing_meta]. You have been fitting one all along.

The random-effects model says an observed effect is the overall mean plus two errors,
$\hat\theta_k = \mu + \zeta_k + \epsilon_k$. Split it into its two levels:

- **Level 1, participants.** Study $k$'s observed effect misses its own true effect by sampling error:
  $\hat\theta_k = \theta_k + \epsilon_k$. The participants are pooled at this level, usually by the study's
  authors, who report a mean and SD rather than raw data.
- **Level 2, studies.** Each study's true effect is drawn from a distribution of true effects around the
  overall mean: $\theta_k = \mu + \zeta_k$. This pooling is the meta-analysis.

Substitute the second line into the first and you are back at the random-effects model. Set $\zeta_k$ to zero
and you have the fixed-effect model [@harrer_doing_meta]. Participants are **nested** in studies, just as
pupils are nested in schools in a [mixed-effects model](../03-regression/mixed-effects-models.qmd), and the
random-effects model is that model's random intercept.

## The three-level model

Once the two levels are visible, adding a third is natural. Insert a layer of **clusters** between the effect
sizes and the overall mean [@harrer_doing_meta]:

- **Level 1:** each observed effect $\hat\theta_{ij}$ (effect $i$ in cluster $j$) misses its true effect by
  sampling error.
- **Level 2:** the true effects within cluster $j$ scatter around that cluster's own average $\kappa_j$.
- **Level 3:** the cluster averages scatter around the overall mean $\mu$.

A cluster can be a **study** that contributes several effect sizes, or a **group of studies** (a cultural
region, a research lab) where each study gives one effect. Either way the model now has **two** heterogeneity
variances to estimate instead of one: $\tau^2_{\text{Level 2}}$, the spread of true effects *within* clusters,
and $\tau^2_{\text{Level 3}}$, the spread of cluster averages *between* them [@harrer_doing_meta]. The overall
mean $\mu$ means much the same as in a random-effects model; what changes is that it now comes from a model
that knows which effects belong together.

Why does this fix the double-counting? Effects in one cluster share their cluster's deviation. If a study
happened to sample unusually responsive people, all of its effects are high together. The model sees that,
treats the study's several effects as partly one piece of information, and weights them accordingly. In the
same way, [hierarchical Bayesian models](../02-inference/hierarchical-bayesian-models.qmd) let groups borrow
strength without pretending to be more independent than they are.

### A worked example: Chernobyl and mutation rates

Harrer et al.'s example is loosely based on a real meta-analysis by Møller and Mousseau of the correlation
between ionising radiation from the 1986 Chernobyl disaster and mutation rates in humans
[@harrer_doing_meta]. It has **33 effect sizes from 14 studies**. Most studies give more than one, because they
measured mutations by several methods or studied several kinds of people (exposed parents and their children,
for instance). The effects are correlations, pooled on the [Fisher's $z$ scale](effect-sizes-for-synthesis.qmd).

The three-level model (REML, with a $t$-test) gives [@harrer_doing_meta]:

| | Estimate |
|---|---|
| Pooled effect | $z = 0.52$ (95% CI 0.25 to 0.80), so $r \approx 0.48$ (0.24 to 0.66) |
| $\tau^2_{\text{Level 3}}$ (between studies, 14 clusters) | 0.179 |
| $\tau^2_{\text{Level 2}}$ (within studies, 33 effects) | 0.119 |
| Share of total variance: sampling error / within / between | 1% / 40% / 59% |

: The three-level Chernobyl model [@harrer_doing_meta].

A correlation of about 0.48 is large: a substantial association between radiation exposure and mutation
rates. The heterogeneity test is significant, but Harrer et al. call it not very informative. The useful
question is **where** the heterogeneity lives.

That is what the last row answers. In an ordinary meta-analysis, [$I^2$](between-study-heterogeneity.qmd) is
the share of variation that sampling error doesn't explain. In a three-level model it splits in two (Cheung):
$I^2_{\text{Level 2}}$ is the share due to true differences within clusters and $I^2_{\text{Level 3}}$ the
share due to differences between clusters [@harrer_doing_meta]. Here sampling error is about 1% of the total,
the within-study share about 40% and the between-study share about 59%. Most of the disagreement is between
studies, but more than a third is between effects in the **same** study. A model that ignored that layer would
be missing a large part of the picture.

### Is the third level worth it?

A three-level model only makes sense if it describes the data better than a two-level one. If both fit
equally well, Occam's razor favours the simpler model [@harrer_doing_meta]. To check, fit the model again with
one variance forced to zero and compare. Forcing $\tau^2_{\text{Level 3}} = 0$ gives back the ordinary
random-effects model that treats all 33 effects as independent, which we know they are not.

| Model | Pooled $z$ (95% CI) | SE | AIC | log-likelihood |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Three-level | 0.52 (0.25 to 0.80) | 0.134 | 48.24 | −21.12 |
| Two-level, effects treated as independent | 0.60 (0.38 to 0.81) | 0.105 | 62.34 | −29.17 |

: Removing the study level from the Chernobyl model [@harrer_doing_meta].

The three-level model wins on AIC and BIC, and the likelihood-ratio test agrees ($\chi^2_1 = 16.1$,
$p < 0.001$). The extra parameter is justified [@harrer_doing_meta]. Notice also what the reduced model does
to the uncertainty: its standard error is a fifth smaller and its interval narrower. Ignoring the nesting made
the evidence look stronger than it is, which is the unit-of-analysis error in numbers.

The test is not the only consideration. Harrer et al. would keep the three-level model **even if the test
had favoured two levels** whenever there is a solid theoretical reason for it [@harrer_doing_meta]. Effects
from the same study cannot be independent, so the nested model better represents how the data were
generated, and a non-significant test would only mean the effects within studies happen to be fairly
similar. The case is different when the cluster is a hunch. If level 3 were cultural region and the effect
did not vary between regions, dropping that level would be fine.

### Moderators in a three-level model

[Subgroup analysis and meta-regression](subgroups-and-meta-regression.qmd) carry over directly: add
predictors to the three-level model and it becomes a **three-level mixed-effects model**
[@harrer_doing_meta]. In the Chernobyl data, do correlations differ with the overall radiation dose of the
sample (low, medium, high)? The test of moderators says no, $F(2, 28) = 0.45$, $p = 0.64$. As in any
meta-regression, the coefficients are differences from a reference group. The intercept, $z = 0.58$, is the
high-radiation group; the low group is $0.58 - 0.19 = 0.39$ and the medium group $0.58 + 0.20 = 0.78$.

To report a three-level model, give at least the pooled effect and **both** variance components. Harrer et
al. suggest writing them as $\tau^2_{\text{Level 3}}$ and $\tau^2_{\text{Level 2}}$, which says plainly that
they are heterogeneity variances (the software calls them $\sigma^2_1$ and $\sigma^2_2$), plus the multilevel
$I^2$ values, explained, and the likelihood-ratio test if you compared models [@harrer_doing_meta].

## When the dependence is messier than the model

The three-level model is better than pretending effects are independent, but it is still a simplification.
It assumes the **sampling errors** of effects within a study are uncorrelated, and often they are not
[@harrer_doing_meta]. The Chernobyl studies give multiple effects for different reasons. Some compared
different target populations, which gives separate samples. Others measured the **same sample** by several
methods, and effects computed from the same people have correlated sampling errors. The basic three-level
model has no term for that.

### The correlated and hierarchical effects (CHE) model

Pustejovsky and Tipton's **correlated and hierarchical effects** model keeps the clusters of the three-level
model and adds a correlation $\rho$ between the sampling errors of effects in the same study
[@harrer_doing_meta]. The true correlations are almost never reported, so the model makes a simplifying
assumption: one **constant sampling correlation**, the same within and across all studies. Harrer et al. call
CHE a good starting point, especially when the dependence is complex or only partly known.

The cost is that you have to supply $\rho$. For Chernobyl, Harrer et al. assume a large correlation,
$\rho = 0.6$, and say plainly that it is no more than a guess. Run sensitivity analyses over a range of values
[@harrer_doing_meta].

### Robust variance estimation: the sandwich

Because the CHE model's assumptions are crude, its standard errors are computed with **robust variance
estimation** (RVE). Harrer et al. point out that "robust variance estimation" is sometimes written about as
if it were a special meta-analysis method for dependent effects. It is not. It is the **sandwich estimator**
from ordinary regression, adapted to meta-analysis by Hedges, Tipton and Johnson [@harrer_doing_meta]. It
gives a consistent standard error even when the model's assumptions are not met. It is the same tool as
[heteroskedasticity-robust](../03-regression/heteroskedasticity-and-gls.qmd) and [clustered standard
errors](../03-regression/regression-diagnostics.qmd) in regression.

The name comes from its shape: two identical slices of "bread" around a "meat" in the middle. The bread is
the model's own idea of how precise the estimate is. The meat is what the residuals actually show, summed
cluster by cluster, so whatever the true correlation within a study is, it gets counted
[@harrer_doing_meta]. If the model's covariance assumptions are wrong, the meat corrects for it. With few
studies (say 40 or fewer) the plain sandwich is too optimistic, and the recommended small-sample correction
is Tipton and Pustejovsky's **CR2** adjustment.

The point, in Harrer et al.'s words, is to guard the inference against **misspecification**. Multilevel
models are easy to get wrong, and RVE keeps the confidence intervals and $p$-values honest even if the
assumed correlation structure is off [@harrer_doing_meta].

In the Chernobyl example the CHE model with CR2 standard errors gives point estimates close to the
three-level moderator model's (high radiation 0.58, low −0.19, medium +0.21) but **much** larger standard
errors, with confidence intervals for the intercept running from −6.8 to 7.9 [@harrer_doing_meta]. Nothing is
significant. The reason is in the degrees of freedom column, about 1 to 2 per coefficient: robust inference
is estimated cluster by cluster, and the high-radiation group contains very few studies. With so little
information per coefficient, RVE becomes very conservative.

### Cluster wild bootstrapping

For small numbers of studies, where RVE can be overly conservative, as in this example, Joshi, Pustejovsky and
Beretvas suggest **cluster wild bootstrapping** [@harrer_doing_meta]. It is also the natural way to test a
**multiple-contrast hypothesis**, such as whether a three-level categorical moderator matters at all. The
algorithm:

1. Fit the full model to the real data and compute the test statistic.
2. Fit a **null** model (without the covariates being tested) and take its residuals.
3. For each study, draw one random value and multiply all of that study's residuals by it. The whole cluster
   moves together, so the within-study dependence is preserved.
4. Add the transformed residuals to the null model's predictions to make a new set of effect sizes.
5. Refit the full model and recompute the test statistic.

Repeat steps 3 to 5 many times (2,000 in Harrer et al.'s example). The $p$-value is the share of bootstrap
statistics more extreme than the real one [@harrer_doing_meta]. Testing whether radiation dose moderates the
Chernobyl correlations, holding publication year fixed, gives $p = 0.36$, in line with the earlier moderator
test. The logic is that of the [bootstrap](../02-inference/the-bootstrap.qmd), resampling whole clusters'
residuals rather than single data points.

def reml(y, comps):

    th = np.full(len(comps), 0.03)
    for _ in range(200):

        P = Vi - Vi @ X @ np.linalg.inv(X.T @ Vi @ X) @ X.T @ Vi

## Several outcomes at once: multivariate meta-analysis

Sometimes a study's several effects are not repeated measures of one outcome but **different outcomes**, each
worth estimating in its own right. A treatment may be judged on two outcomes that most trials measure. A
**multivariate meta-analysis** estimates both pooled effects in one model and uses the correlation between
them [@harrer_doing_meta]. It also answers a new question: do studies with a large effect on one outcome tend
to have a large effect on the other?

Harrer et al. fit it from a [structural equation modelling](../03-regression/structural-equation-models.qmd)
angle, building on the idea above: if every meta-analysis is a multilevel model, and multilevel models are a
special case of SEM (Mehta and Neale; Bauer), then a meta-analysis can be written as a SEM in which the true
effect is a latent variable (Cheung) [@harrer_doing_meta]. Multivariate meta-analysis can also be done
outside SEM. For the example they extend the third-wave psychotherapy trials (stress outcomes, see
[pooling](pooling-effect-sizes.qmd)) with each trial's effect on **anxiety**, its standard error, and the
covariance between the two outcomes. One trial did not measure anxiety; the maximum-likelihood fit still uses
its stress effect.

| | Stress | Anxiety |
|---|---|---|
| Pooled Hedges' $g$ | 0.57 (0.40 to 0.74) | 0.41 (0.24 to 0.57) |
| $\tau^2$ | 0.073 | 0.058 |
| $I^2$ | 62% | 53% |
| Correlation of the true effects | 0.45 | |

: Multivariate meta-analysis of 18 third-wave therapy trials [@harrer_doing_meta].

Both effects are significant and both are heterogeneous. The new number is the correlation between the
studies' true effects, 0.45: treatments that reduce stress more also tend to reduce anxiety more
[@harrer_doing_meta]. It comes from the between-study covariance matrix, which has a heterogeneity variance
for each outcome on the diagonal and their covariance off it.

The usual practical obstacle is that primary studies rarely report the covariance between their outcomes.
Then it has to be **imputed** from an assumed correlation, ideally from previous literature in a similar
context: the covariance is approximately $SE_1 \times SE_2 \times \rho$ [@harrer_doing_meta]. Harrer et al.
take $\rho \approx 0.6$ for stress and anxiety in clinical trials. The assumed correlation can change the
results a lot, so always report it and rerun the analysis with other plausible values.

### Meta-analytic SEM: pooling correlation matrices

The SEM view goes further than two outcomes. **Two-stage structural equation modelling** (TSSEM; Cheung, Tang
and Cheung) first pools each study's **correlation matrix** in a multivariate random-effects model, and then
fits a structural model to the pooled matrix by weighted least squares [@harrer_doing_meta]. Studies with some
correlations missing can still contribute to stage 1.

Harrer et al.'s example is a meta-analytic **confirmatory factor analysis** of a fictitious sleep
questionnaire, with simulated correlation matrices from 11 studies ($N = 3{,}272$). The hypothesis, after Koffel
and Watson, is that five symptoms reflect two latent factors: sleep quality, latency and efficiency load on
**insomnia**, and daytime dysfunction and hypersomnia on **lassitude** [@harrer_doing_meta]. Stage 1 pools the
ten correlations ($I^2$ between 65% and 93%). Stage 2 fits the two-factor model: loadings from 0.61 to 0.79, a
factor correlation of 0.33, and a non-significant misfit test ($\chi^2_4 = 5.26$, $p = 0.26$) with RMSEA 0.0098,
well under the 0.05 rule of thumb for good fit.

One feature of SEM is that the logic of testing is reversed. A $t$-test aims to *reject* its null; a SEM
proposes a model and hopes the fit test does **not** reject it [@harrer_doing_meta]. That invites a trap
Harrer et al. spell out: a well-fitting model does not prove the theory, because other models may fit the
same data just as well. Test plausible alternatives too. The [factor analysis
entry](../03-regression/factor-analysis.qmd) covers the measurement side in detail.

## Common pitfalls

::: {.callout-warning title="Where this goes wrong"}
- **Pooling several effects per study as if they were independent.** The unit-of-analysis error: too little
  heterogeneity, too-narrow intervals and false positives. Model the nesting or reduce to one effect per
  study. [@harrer_doing_meta]
- **Dropping the third level because a likelihood-ratio test says so.** When effects come from the same
  study, the dependence is known, so keep the three-level model even if the test is not significant. Drop a
  level only when the clustering variable was a guess (such as cultural region) and shows no variation.
  [@harrer_doing_meta]
- **Reporting one $\tau^2$.** A three-level model has two variance components. Report both, as
  $\tau^2_{\text{Level 2}}$ and $\tau^2_{\text{Level 3}}$, with the multilevel $I^2$ values. [@harrer_doing_meta]
- **Forgetting that the three-level model assumes uncorrelated sampling errors.** Effects measured on the
  same participants have correlated errors. Use a CHE model or robust standard errors. [@harrer_doing_meta]
- **Treating an assumed correlation as data.** The $\rho$ in a CHE model, or the correlation used to impute
  covariances in a multivariate model, is usually a guess. Report it and run sensitivity analyses over
  plausible values. [@harrer_doing_meta]
- **Using the plain sandwich with few studies.** It is too optimistic with about 40 studies or fewer. Use the
  CR2 small-sample correction, and with very few clusters per coefficient consider the cluster wild
  bootstrap. [@harrer_doing_meta]
- **Taking a good SEM fit as proof.** Other models may fit just as well. Fit the alternatives.
  [@harrer_doing_meta]
:::

## Appendix · the mathematics {.unnumbered}

::: {.math-appendix}

**The three-level model.** For effect $i$ in cluster $j$ with known sampling variance $v_{ij}$,

$$\hat\theta_{ij} = \mu + \zeta_{(3)j} + \zeta_{(2)ij} + \epsilon_{ij},\qquad
\zeta_{(3)j}\sim\mathcal N(0,\tau^2_{(3)}),\ \ \zeta_{(2)ij}\sim\mathcal N(0,\tau^2_{(2)}),\ \ \epsilon_{ij}\sim\mathcal N(0,v_{ij}).$$

Stack cluster $j$'s $n_j$ effects into $\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_j$. Their covariance matrix is

$$\mathbf V_j = \operatorname{diag}(v_{1j},\dots,v_{n_jj}) + \tau^2_{(2)}\,\mathbf I_{n_j} + \tau^2_{(3)}\,\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top .$$

> **In plain words:** two effects from the same cluster share the cluster's deviation, so their covariance is
> $\tau^2_{(3)}$; effects from different clusters are uncorrelated.

So the correlation between two true effects in one cluster is $\tau^2_{(3)}/(\tau^2_{(3)}+\tau^2_{(2)})$, the
intraclass correlation of the [mixed-effects entry](../03-regression/mixed-effects-models.qmd). For Chernobyl,
$0.1788/0.2982 = 0.60$. Given the variances, $\hat\mu = \sum_j\mathbf 1^\top\mathbf V_j^{-1}\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_j \big/ \sum_j \mathbf 1^\top\mathbf V_j^{-1}\mathbf 1$
with model-based variance $1/\sum_j \mathbf 1^\top\mathbf V_j^{-1}\mathbf 1$. The variances are estimated by
REML (the figure uses Fisher scoring). Setting $\tau^2_{(3)} = 0$ gives the ordinary random-effects model on
all effects.

**Model comparison.** $\text{LRT} = 2(\ell_{\text{full}} - \ell_{\text{reduced}}) = 2(-21.12 + 29.17) = 16.10$ on
1 df, and $\text{AIC} = -2\ell + 2p$: $42.24 + 6 = 48.24$ and $58.34 + 4 = 62.34$.

**Multilevel $I^2$** (our algebra, following Cheung's definition as a share of total variation). With a
typical sampling variance $\tilde v$,

$$I^2_{\text{Level 3}} = \frac{\tau^2_{(3)}}{\tau^2_{(3)} + \tau^2_{(2)} + \tilde v},\qquad
I^2_{\text{Level 2}} = \frac{\tau^2_{(2)}}{\tau^2_{(3)} + \tau^2_{(2)} + \tilde v}.$$

The printed 59.22% for level 3 implies a total of $0.1788/0.5922 = 0.3019$, so $\tilde v \approx 0.0037$ (1.2%
of the total) and $I^2_{\text{Level 2}} = 0.1194/0.3019 \approx 39.5\%$, consistent with the printed shares.
Fisher's $z$ has variance $1/(n-3)$, so $\tilde v \approx 0.0037$ corresponds to studies of a few hundred
people.

**The CHE working model.** Add a constant correlation $\rho$ between sampling errors in one study:
$\operatorname{Cov}(\epsilon_{ij}, \epsilon_{i'j}) = \rho\sqrt{v_{ij}v_{i'j}}$ for $i\ne i'$. This is the
covariance the figure's simulation draws from, with $\rho = 0.6$.

**The sandwich estimator.** For meta-regression $\mathbf T_j = \mathbf X_j\boldsymbol\beta + \mathbf u_j + \mathbf e_j$
with working weights $\mathbf W_j$,

$$\mathbf V^{\text{R}}_{\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}} =
\Big(\sum_j \mathbf X_j^\top\mathbf W_j\mathbf X_j\Big)^{-1}
\Big(\sum_j \mathbf X_j^\top\mathbf W_j\mathbf A_j\boldsymbol\Phi_j\mathbf A_j\mathbf W_j\mathbf X_j\Big)
\Big(\sum_j \mathbf X_j^\top\mathbf W_j\mathbf X_j\Big)^{-1},$$

where $\boldsymbol\Phi_j = \operatorname{Var}(\mathbf u_j + \mathbf e_j)$ is the true within-cluster covariance,
estimated in practice by the cluster's residual cross-product $\mathbf e_j\mathbf e_j^\top$, $\mathbf W_j$ the
model's inverse covariance and $\mathbf A_j$ the small-sample adjustment (CR2) [@harrer_doing_meta].

> **In plain words:** the bread is the model's own variance; the meat replaces the model's guess about how
> the effects co-vary with what each cluster's residuals actually do.

If the model is right, the meat averages to the bread's inverse and the sandwich collapses to the model-based
variance. The figure uses the simpler CR1 correction, multiplying by $J/(J-1)$ with $J$ clusters and using
$t_{J-1}$. See [regression diagnostics](../03-regression/regression-diagnostics.qmd) for the same estimator with
clusters of people.

**Multivariate random effects.** Study $k$'s vector of $p$ outcome effects is
$\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_k \sim \mathcal N(\boldsymbol\mu,\ \mathbf S_k + \mathbf T)$, with $\mathbf S_k$ the known
within-study covariance (variances on the diagonal, the reported or imputed covariance
$\widehat{\text{Cov}} = SE_1 SE_2\,\hat\rho$ off it) and $\mathbf T$ the between-study covariance. A study missing an
outcome contributes the matching sub-vector and sub-matrix. For ThirdWave the ML estimate (our refit) is

$$\hat{\boldsymbol\mu} = \begin{pmatrix}0.570\\0.407\end{pmatrix},\qquad
\hat{\mathbf T} = \begin{pmatrix}0.0733 & 0.0289\\ 0.0289 & 0.0575\end{pmatrix},\qquad
\hat\rho_{\text{between}} = \frac{0.0289}{\sqrt{0.0733\times0.0575}} = 0.446,$$

matching Harrer et al.'s printed output.

**TSSEM stage 2.** With pooled correlation vector $\mathbf r$ and its covariance $\mathbf V$ from stage 1, the
structural parameters $\boldsymbol\vartheta$ minimise

$$F_{\text{WLS}}(\boldsymbol\vartheta) = \big(\mathbf r - \boldsymbol\rho(\boldsymbol\vartheta)\big)^\top \mathbf V^{-1}\big(\mathbf r - \boldsymbol\rho(\boldsymbol\vartheta)\big),$$

the same fitting function whether stage 1 was fixed- or random-effects, since stage 1 has already dealt with
heterogeneity [@harrer_doing_meta].
