Pooling Studies: Fixed- & Random-Effects Models

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source

A pooled effect is a weighted average. The whole craft is deciding what the studies are averages of, and how to weight them.

Pooling looks like arithmetic: weight each study by its precision and average. But the weights depend on a model of where the studies came from. Either they are all noisy measurements of one true effect (fixed-effect), or each measures its own true effect, drawn from a spread of possible effects (random-effects). The second model gives small studies a bigger say, adds a variance \tau^2 that has to be estimated, and should come with a more honest confidence interval. In social science the second is the default, and the choices it brings (which \tau^2 estimator, whether to adjust the interval) change the answer more than most readers realise.

Why it matters

The gateway entry showed the idea: four towel-reuse studies, weighted by precision, give one estimate that is sharper than any of them. Real syntheses are messier. Eighteen trials of a psychotherapy differ in who they enrolled, how long the treatment ran, whether it was delivered in a group or online, and what the control group got. Pool them as if they were one experiment repeated eighteen times and you get a number. Pool them as samples from a family of related experiments and you get a different number, with a wider interval. Harrer and colleagues’ worked example below moves from g = 0.48 to g = 0.58 on exactly this choice (Harrer et al. 2022).

That gap isn’t a rounding issue. It comes from a modelling assumption, and an assumption can be argued about. Harrer et al. stress that meta-analysis comes with its own researcher degrees of freedom, a large set of statistical choices where the better option often depends on context (Harrer et al. 2022). This entry covers the main ones: the model, the \tau^2 estimator, the confidence-interval method, and, for binary outcomes, the pooling method. Each is defensible, and together they can move the result. Knowing what each one does is how you report them honestly. It is also how you notice when someone else hasn’t (questionable research practices apply to syntheses too).

A model is a story about the black box

Harrer et al. open the chapter by asking what a statistical model is. The observed effect sizes come out of a black box, and a model is a small theory about what goes on inside it, written as a formula (Harrer et al. 2022). Every meta-analysis has to tell that story before it can average anything, because the story decides the weights.

Fixed-effect: one truth, many noisy readings

The fixed-effect model says every study estimates the same true effect \theta. Study k’s observed effect differs from it only by sampling error:

\hat\theta_k = \theta + \epsilon_k .

If that is the whole story, the best estimate of \theta trusts precise studies more. A study with a small standard error has small sampling error and is likely to land near the truth. A study with a large one could land almost anywhere. Harrer et al. show this with a simulation: when the true effect is zero, estimates with small standard errors pack tightly around zero and the scatter fans out as the standard error grows (Harrer et al. 2022). So each study gets weight w_k = 1/s_k^2, the inverse of its sampling variance, and the pooled estimate is the weighted mean. This is inverse-variance pooling, the most common way to average effects in meta-analysis, and the meta-analytic cousin of weighted least squares.

Newer software calls this the common-effect model, which is a clearer name: “fixed” means one shared effect, not fixed in the sense used for panel-data fixed effects.

Random-effects: a family of truths

Now a reality check. The studies in a real meta-analysis differ in their populations, treatment formats and intensities, outcome measures and control groups, usually several of these at once (Harrer et al. 2022). It would be strange if the true effect were identical across all of them. It is more natural to expect each study to have its own true effect \theta_k, and those true effects to differ for real reasons.

The random-effects model writes this as a two-stage process (Harrer et al. 2022):

\hat\theta_k = \theta_k + \epsilon_k, \qquad \theta_k = \mu + \zeta_k .

The observed effect misses the study’s true effect by sampling error \epsilon_k. The study’s true effect is itself a draw from a distribution of true effects with mean \mu and variance \tau^2, and \zeta_k is how far this study’s truth sits from the average. Each study is one draw from a “universe” of populations, and the target of the meta-analysis is now \mu, the mean of the true effects, not one shared effect.

The model needs one more assumption, exchangeability: before seeing the data there is nothing to say that one study’s \zeta_k will be bigger than another’s. The deviations are treated as chance (Harrer et al. 2022). If you do know something (say, that online delivery works worse) the deviations aren’t exchangeable, and that knowledge belongs in a subgroup analysis or a meta-regression, not in \tau^2.

If this looks familiar, it should. A study’s true effect plus a study-level deviation is the structure of a mixed-effects model. Harrer et al. make the link explicit: a random-effects meta-analysis is a mixed-effects regression with only an intercept (\mu) and a random effect on that intercept (Harrer et al. 2022). The same structure is a hierarchical Bayesian model without the priors.

What the extra variance does to the weights

Under random-effects each study’s weight becomes

w_k^* = \frac{1}{s_k^2 + \tau^2}.

Every study’s variance gets the same \tau^2 added. For a huge study, s_k^2 is tiny, so \tau^2 dominates its variance and its weight drops a lot. For a small study, s_k^2 was already large, so adding \tau^2 changes little. The weights are evened out. That follows from the model: if true effects genuinely vary, even a perfectly measured study tells you about only one draw from the family, so no single study should dominate the estimate of the family’s mean.

Which model? The convention and its critics

Harrer et al. report the convention in medicine and the social sciences: use random-effects by default, because some between-study heterogeneity is almost always expected, even with a tight research question (Harrer et al. 2022). Fixed-effect is reserved for cases with good reason to believe the effect really is common, such as exact replications or subsets of one large study. Such cases are rare.

The convention has critics, and their point is worth understanding rather than memorising. Random-effects gives small studies more weight, and small studies are the ones most prone to bias, including the publication bias that leaves small null studies in the file drawer. For that reason some authors argue fixed-effect is sometimes preferable, and Stanley, Doucouliagos and Ioannidis argue for an “unrestricted weighted least squares” model instead in some disciplines (Harrer et al. 2022). The disagreement is about which error you fear more. Random-effects protects against false precision when effects vary, and fixed-effect protects against over-weighting small, possibly biased studies.

A related bad habit: choosing the model after running a heterogeneity test (fixed-effect if Cochran’s Q is non-significant, random-effects if not). Harrer et al. discourage this firmly: the test’s power depends on how many studies you have and how big they are, so its verdict says as much about the size of the literature as about heterogeneity (Harrer et al. 2022). The choice should rest on what you believe about the studies before you see the numbers. See between-study heterogeneity for why Q makes a poor referee.

Estimating \tau^2: a choice that changes the answer

The random-effects weights need \tau^2, and \tau^2 is unknown. It has to be estimated from how much the studies disagree beyond what their standard errors explain. There are several estimators, and Harrer et al. list the common ones with their software codes (Harrer et al. 2022):

Estimator How it works Harrer et al.’s guidance
DerSimonian–Laird (DL) Closed-form formula built on Cochran’s Q The historical default (RevMan, older {meta}), so “random-effects” in old papers often means DL. Can be biased when studies are few and heterogeneity high, which is common. Use it if outsiders must replicate your numbers outside R.
Restricted maximum likelihood (REML) Iterative; maximises a likelihood that accounts for estimating \mu A good first choice for continuous outcomes; the {metafor} default.
Paule–Mandel (PM) Iterative; picks the \tau^2 at which a generalised Q equals its expected value Recommended for binary and continuous data, and suits small meta-analyses; may be off when study sizes vary drastically. Empirical Bayes is practically identical.
Sidik–Jonkman (SJ) Closed-form Only when \tau^2 is believed very large and avoiding false positives is the priority.

Which estimator performs best is still an open research question. It depends on the number of studies, their sizes, how unequal those sizes are, and how large \tau^2 is (Harrer et al. 2022). Harrer et al.’s practical advice is reassuring: the differences are often minor, and when in doubt you can re-run with a different estimator and see whether the interpretation changes. That re-run is a sensitivity analysis, and it belongs in the report.

Knapp–Hartung: an interval that knows \tau^2 was guessed

The usual confidence interval for \hat\mu treats \hat\tau^2 as if it were the true value, and builds a normal-theory (Wald) interval. But \tau^2 is estimated from a handful of studies, often badly. The Knapp–Hartung adjustment allows for that uncertainty. It rescales the standard error by how much the studies actually scatter around \hat\mu, and it uses a t-distribution with K-1 degrees of freedom instead of the normal (Harrer et al. 2022). Usually the interval gets somewhat wider.

Harrer et al. call the adjustment usually sensible, because simulation studies show it cuts false positives, especially when there are few studies (Harrer et al. 2022). It isn’t uncontroversial: in the rare case of very homogeneous effects it can produce intervals that are too narrow. It only applies to random-effects models. Their suggested methods-section sentence names all three choices: the model (random-effects, because heterogeneity was expected), the \tau^2 estimator (with a citation), and the interval method (Knapp–Hartung). A reader can’t evaluate a pooled estimate without all three.

Worked example: eighteen trials of third-wave therapy

Harrer et al.’s main example pools 18 randomised trials of “third-wave” psychotherapies (mindfulness, ACT and related approaches) for perceived stress in college students (Harrer et al. 2022). Each study contributes Hedges’ g, a standardised mean difference between treatment and control with the small-sample correction already applied. The studies plainly differ: in risk of bias, type of control group (wait-list, no intervention, information only), intervention length, intervention type and mode of delivery. Harrer et al. therefore choose random-effects, REML and Knapp–Hartung before looking at a single estimate.

Their results (Harrer et al. 2022):

  • Random-effects pooled effect g = 0.58, 95% CI 0.38 to 0.78, t = 6.12, p < 0.001.
  • \hat\tau^2 = 0.08 (CI 0.03 to 0.35), so the true effects do appear to vary.
  • The fixed-effect estimate, from the same data, is g = 0.48.
  • Switching the estimator to Paule–Mandel gives \hat\tau^2 = 0.11 but barely moves the pooled effect, to 0.59.
  • The largest random-effects weight, 7.9%, goes to de Vibe et al. The smallest goes to Ratanasiripong, whose wide interval marks an imprecise estimate.

Why the two models disagree. We rebuilt the analysis from the effects and confidence intervals in Harrer et al.’s output (our own arithmetic, which reproduces their printed numbers to the third decimal). De Vibe et al. is by far the most precise study, and it found a small effect, g = 0.18. Under fixed-effect it carries 17.5% of the total weight. Under random-effects, adding \tau^2 = 0.08 to every variance cuts it to 7.9%. Meanwhile the three small studies with the largest effects (DanitzOrsillo g = 1.79, Shapiro et al. 1.48, Kang et al. 1.28) roughly double their weight, from about 2% each to about 4%. The ratio of largest to smallest weight falls from about 9:1 to about 2:1. The random-effects mean rises because the model moves weight from the big, small-effect study to the small, large-effect ones, exactly as described above. For DerSimonian–Laird (also our arithmetic) \hat\tau^2 = 0.075 and \hat\mu = 0.574, very close to REML here.

So which is “right”? If you believe these eighteen trials measure one common effect, 0.48. If you believe they sample a family of effects, 0.58 is your estimate of the family’s average. The critics’ worry also applies here: the weight moved toward small studies with big effects. Whether those studies are signal or small-study bias is a question for heterogeneity diagnostics and publication-bias methods, not for the pooling formula.

A homogeneous contrast. In Harrer et al.’s second example, nine trials of suicide-prevention interventions, the studies hardly disagree: \hat\tau^2 = 0.004, I^2 = 7\%, and Q is not significant (Harrer et al. 2022). The pooled g = -0.23 (lower suicidal ideation under treatment; Harrer et al. suggest flipping signs so positive always means benefit) is the same to two decimals under either model, because a \tau^2 near zero leaves the weights almost unchanged. The model choice only matters when the studies really vary.

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# Harrer et al., Doing Meta-Analysis with R, §4.2.1 (ThirdWave): g and 95% CI
names = ["Call", "Cavanagh", "DanitzOrsillo", "de Vibe", "Frazier", "Frogeli",
         "Gallego", "Hazlett-Stevens", "Hintz", "Kang", "Kuhlmann", "Lever Taylor",
         "Phang", "Rasanen", "Ratanasiripong", "Shapiro", "Song & Lindquist", "Warnecke"]
g = np.array([0.7091, 0.3549, 1.7912, 0.1825, 0.4219, 0.6300, 0.7249, 0.5287, 0.2840,
              1.2751, 0.1036, 0.3884, 0.5407, 0.4262, 0.5154, 1.4797, 0.6126, 0.6000])
lo = np.array([0.1979, -0.0300, 1.1139, -0.0484, 0.1380, 0.2458, 0.2846, 0.1162, -0.0453,
               0.6142, -0.2781, -0.0639, 0.0619, -0.0794, -0.1731, 0.8618, 0.1683, 0.1120])
hi = np.array([1.2203, 0.7397, 2.4685, 0.4133, 0.7057, 1.0142, 1.1652, 0.9412, 0.6133,
               1.9360, 0.4853, 0.8407, 1.0196, 0.9317, 1.2039, 2.0977, 1.0569, 1.0880])
v = ((hi - lo) / (2 * 1.959964))**2            # sampling variances from the CIs
K = len(g)

# fixed (common) effect: inverse-variance weights
w = 1 / v
mu_fe = np.sum(w * g) / w.sum()                # 0.4805

# REML for tau^2 by fixed-point iteration
t2 = 0.0
for _ in range(500):
    ws = 1 / (v + t2)
    m = np.sum(ws * g) / ws.sum()
    t2_new = max(0.0, np.sum(ws**2 * ((g - m)**2 - v)) / np.sum(ws**2) + 1 / ws.sum())
    if abs(t2_new - t2) < 1e-12:
        break
    t2 = t2_new                                # 0.0820

ws = 1 / (v + t2)
mu_re = np.sum(ws * g) / ws.sum()              # 0.5771
# Knapp-Hartung standard error and t(K-1) interval
se_hk = np.sqrt(np.sum(ws * (g - mu_re)**2) / ((K - 1) * ws.sum()))
t975 = 2.109816                                # 97.5th percentile of t with 17 df
ci_re = (mu_re - t975 * se_hk, mu_re + t975 * se_hk)     # 0.378 to 0.776
se_fe = np.sqrt(1 / w.sum())
ci_fe = (mu_fe - 1.959964 * se_fe, mu_fe + 1.959964 * se_fe)

share_fe, share_re = 100 * w / w.sum(), 100 * ws / ws.sum()
order = np.argsort(g)                          # sort by effect, smallest at top
y = np.arange(K)[::-1] + 3

fig, (ax, bx) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(9.6, 6.4), sharey=True,
                             gridspec_kw={"width_ratios": [1.7, 1]})
for yi, i in zip(y, order):
    ax.plot([lo[i], hi[i]], [yi, yi], color="#161613", lw=1.0, zorder=1)
    ax.scatter(g[i], yi, marker="s", s=12 + 22 * share_re[i], color="#161613", zorder=2)

Pooling binary outcomes: when inverse-variance isn’t enough

Ratios such as risk and odds ratios are pooled on the log scale, because a ratio is symmetric only in logs (the opposite of RR = 0.5 is 2, not 1.5), and the result is exponentiated back at the end. The same idea applies to other effect types: correlations are pooled as Fisher’s z and proportions as logits (Harrer et al. 2022). Effect sizes for synthesis covers these transformations, and their standard errors, in detail.

Inverse-variance pooling of log ratios works, but Harrer et al. call it suboptimal for sparse binary data. When events are few or studies small, the estimated standard error is a poor measure of a study’s real precision (Harrer et al. 2022). Three alternatives exist for the fixed-effect weights:

  • Mantel–Haenszel weights come from the 2×2 table’s counts rather than an estimated variance. This is the default in metabin, and Cochrane’s general recommendation.
  • Peto’s method uses a special odds ratio built from observed minus expected events. It suits only odds ratios, and simulations show it behaves well only when the arms are similar in size, the event is rare (under 1%), and the effect is not huge.
  • Sample-size weights (Bakbergenuly and colleagues) are newer and less studied.

Zero cells. A study with no events in one arm breaks the log odds ratio. The traditional fix is to add 0.5 to the empty cells, but continuity corrections are now discouraged because they can bias results. Mantel–Haenszel needs one only when the same cell is zero in every study, so Harrer et al. recommend the exact Mantel–Haenszel method without a correction (Harrer et al. 2022).

None of this changes the random-effects model, which still pools by inverse variance even when MH is chosen for the fixed-effect part (Harrer et al. 2022). For proportions, metaprop instead fits a generalised linear mixed model (a random-intercept logistic regression) directly to the event counts, so there are no study weights to display and \tau^2 is estimated by maximum likelihood without a confidence interval.

Reading and drawing a forest plot

The forest plot is how nearly every meta-analysis reports its result, and Harrer et al. list its parts (Harrer et al. 2022). Study labels run down the left. Each study gets a square at its point estimate, sized by its weight, and a line for its confidence interval. A diamond at the bottom shows the pooled effect, with its width marking the pooled interval. A vertical reference line marks no effect (0 for differences, 1 for ratios). Ratios go on a log axis so that 0.5 and 2 sit equally far from 1. A good forest plot also prints the numbers behind each row, so anyone can re-run the analysis, and can add columns such as each study’s risk-of-bias rating.

A forest plot fixes one significance level, conventionally a 95% interval. Harrer et al. describe drapery plots as a complement: each study gets a p-value function, a curve showing its confidence interval at every level, so readers aren’t anchored on p < 0.05 (Harrer et al. 2022). They recommend drapery plots alongside forest plots, not instead of them, because the forest plot carries the data needed for replication.

Common pitfalls

WarningWhere this goes wrong
  • Reporting “a random-effects model” and nothing else. The \tau^2 estimator and the interval method are part of the model. “Random-effects” in older papers usually means DerSimonian–Laird with a Wald interval, which can be biased with few, heterogeneous studies. Name all three choices. (Harrer et al. 2022)
  • Picking the model from the Q-test. Whether Q is significant depends on the number and size of the studies. Decide from what you know about the studies beforehand. (Harrer et al. 2022)
  • Forgetting what \mu means. Under random-effects, \hat\mu is the average of a distribution of true effects. When \tau is large, many settings will have effects far from it. Report the spread too (prediction intervals).
  • Ignoring where the weight went. Random-effects up-weights small studies. If the small studies are also the ones with the biggest effects, as in the worked example, the pooled mean inherits any small-study bias. Look at the weights, not just the diamond.
  • Wald intervals with a handful of studies. With few studies \hat\tau^2 is very noisy. Knapp–Hartung usually guards against false positives here (though not when the effects are almost identical). (Harrer et al. 2022)
  • Pooling raw ratios or raw correlations. Pool on the log scale (ratios), Fisher’s z (correlations) or the logit (proportions), and back-transform at the end. (Harrer et al. 2022)
  • Automatic continuity corrections. Adding 0.5 to zero cells can bias the result. Use exact Mantel–Haenszel when you can. (Harrer et al. 2022)
  • Treating the pooled number as validated. Pooling raises precision, not validity: garbage in, precisely-estimated garbage out. (Harrer et al. 2022)

Appendix · the mathematics

Fixed (common) effect. With \hat\theta_k = \theta + \epsilon_k, \epsilon_k \sim \mathcal N(0, s_k^2) and s_k^2 treated as known,

\hat\theta_{\text{FE}} = \frac{\sum_k w_k \hat\theta_k}{\sum_k w_k}, \quad w_k = \frac{1}{s_k^2}, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(\hat\theta_{\text{FE}}) = \frac{1}{\sum_k w_k}.

This is the minimum-variance unbiased linear combination, and also the maximum-likelihood estimate of \theta.

Random effects. With \hat\theta_k = \mu + \zeta_k + \epsilon_k, \zeta_k \sim \mathcal N(0, \tau^2) independent of \epsilon_k, each \hat\theta_k has marginal variance s_k^2 + \tau^2. Given \tau^2,

\hat\mu = \frac{\sum_k w_k^* \hat\theta_k}{\sum_k w_k^*}, \quad w_k^* = \frac{1}{s_k^2 + \tau^2}, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(\hat\mu) = \frac{1}{\sum_k w_k^*}.

In plain words: the same weighted average, except that each study’s noise now includes the spread of true effects. As \tau^2 \to \infty the weights become equal and \hat\mu becomes the plain average of the studies; at \tau^2 = 0 it is the fixed-effect estimate.

Equivalently, this is an intercept-only mixed model, \hat\theta_k = \beta_0 + u_k + \epsilon_k, with \beta_0 = \mu and u_k = \zeta_k (Harrer et al. 2022).

DerSimonian–Laird. From Q = \sum_k w_k(\hat\theta_k - \hat\theta_{\text{FE}})^2, which has expectation (K-1) + \tau^2 C with C = \sum w_k - \sum w_k^2 / \sum w_k, the method-of-moments estimate is

\hat\tau^2_{\text{DL}} = \max\!\left(0,\ \frac{Q - (K-1)}{C}\right).

Paule–Mandel picks the \tau^2 at which the generalised statistic Q_{\text{gen}}(\tau^2) = \sum_k w_k^*(\hat\theta_k - \hat\mu)^2 equals its expectation K-1, found by search, since Q_{\text{gen}} decreases in \tau^2.

REML maximises the restricted likelihood. One standard way to solve it, used in the figure code, is the fixed-point update

\tau^2 \leftarrow \frac{\sum_k w_k^{*2}\big[(\hat\theta_k - \hat\mu)^2 - s_k^2\big]}{\sum_k w_k^{*2}} + \frac{1}{\sum_k w_k^*},

iterated (truncating at zero) until it stops moving. The final term 1/\sum w_k^* is the “restricted” part: it allows for the degree of freedom spent on estimating \mu.

Knapp–Hartung. Replace \operatorname{Var}(\hat\mu) = 1/\sum w_k^* with

\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}_{\text{HK}}(\hat\mu) = \frac{\sum_k w_k^*(\hat\theta_k - \hat\mu)^2}{(K-1)\sum_k w_k^*},

and use t_{K-1} quantiles. The ratio \sum w_k^*(\hat\theta_k-\hat\mu)^2/(K-1) is Q_{\text{gen}}/(K-1). When the studies scatter more than the model expects, it exceeds 1 and the interval widens. When they scatter less (very homogeneous effects) it falls below 1 and the interval can shrink, which is the source of the anti-conservative cases Wiksten et al. flag (Harrer et al. 2022). In the worked example \text{SE}_{\text{HK}} = 0.094 against a Wald SE of 0.087, and with t_{17} the interval is 0.58 \pm 2.11 \times 0.094.

Binary pooling weights (Harrer et al. 2022). With cells a_k, b_k (treatment events, non-events), c_k, d_k (control) and total n_k, the Mantel–Haenszel weights are w_k = (a_k + b_k)c_k / n_k for the risk ratio and w_k = b_k c_k / n_k for the odds ratio. Peto’s log odds ratio is (O_k - E_k)/V_k, with O_k = a_k, E_k = (a_k+b_k)(a_k+c_k)/n_k and the hypergeometric variance V_k = (a_k+b_k)(c_k+d_k)(a_k+c_k)(b_k+d_k)/[n_k^2(n_k-1)].

## Why it matters

The [gateway entry](meta-analysis.qmd) showed the idea: four towel-reuse studies, weighted by precision,
give one estimate that is sharper than any of them. Real syntheses are messier. Eighteen trials of a
psychotherapy differ in who they enrolled, how long the treatment ran, whether it was delivered in a
group or online, and what the control group got. Pool them as if they were one experiment repeated
eighteen times and you get a number. Pool them as samples from a *family* of related experiments and you
get a different number, with a wider interval. Harrer and colleagues' worked example below moves from
$g = 0.48$ to $g = 0.58$ on exactly this choice [@harrer_doing_meta].

That gap isn't a rounding issue. It comes from a modelling assumption, and an assumption can be argued
about. Harrer et al. stress that meta-analysis comes with its own **researcher degrees of freedom**, a
large set of statistical choices where the better option often depends on context [@harrer_doing_meta].
This entry covers the main ones: the model, the $\tau^2$ estimator, the confidence-interval method, and,
for binary outcomes, the pooling method. Each is defensible, and together they can move the result. Knowing what each one
does is how you report them honestly. It is also how you notice when someone else hasn't
([questionable research practices](questionable-research-practices.qmd) apply to syntheses too).

## A model is a story about the black box

Harrer et al. open the chapter by asking what a statistical model *is*. The observed effect sizes come out
of a black box, and a model is a small theory about what goes on inside it, written as a formula
[@harrer_doing_meta]. Every meta-analysis has to tell that story before it can average anything, because
the story decides the weights.

### Fixed-effect: one truth, many noisy readings

The **fixed-effect model** says every study estimates the *same* true effect $\theta$. Study $k$'s observed
effect differs from it only by sampling error:

$$\hat\theta_k = \theta + \epsilon_k .$$

If that is the whole story, the best estimate of $\theta$ trusts precise studies more. A study with a small
standard error has small sampling error and is likely to land near the truth. A study with a large one
could land almost anywhere. Harrer et al. show this with a simulation: when the true effect is zero,
estimates with small standard errors pack tightly around zero and the scatter fans out as the standard
error grows [@harrer_doing_meta]. So each study gets weight $w_k = 1/s_k^2$, the inverse of its sampling
variance, and the pooled estimate is the weighted mean. This is **inverse-variance pooling**, the most
common way to average effects in meta-analysis, and the meta-analytic cousin of
[weighted least squares](../03-regression/heteroskedasticity-and-gls.qmd).

Newer software calls this the **common-effect** model, which is a clearer name: "fixed" means one shared
effect, not fixed in the sense used for panel-data fixed effects.

### Random-effects: a family of truths

Now a reality check. The studies in a real meta-analysis differ in their populations, treatment formats and
intensities, outcome measures and control groups, usually several of these at once
[@harrer_doing_meta]. It would be strange if the *true* effect were identical across all of them. It is
more natural to expect each study to have its own true effect $\theta_k$, and those true effects to
differ for real reasons.

The **random-effects model** writes this as a two-stage process [@harrer_doing_meta]:

$$\hat\theta_k = \theta_k + \epsilon_k, \qquad \theta_k = \mu + \zeta_k .$$

The observed effect misses the study's true effect by sampling error $\epsilon_k$. The study's true effect
is itself a draw from a distribution of true effects with mean $\mu$ and variance $\tau^2$, and
$\zeta_k$ is how far this study's truth sits from the average. Each study is one draw from a "universe" of
populations, and the target of the meta-analysis is now $\mu$, **the mean of the true effects**, not one
shared effect.

The model needs one more assumption, **exchangeability**: before seeing the data there is nothing to
say that one study's $\zeta_k$ will be bigger than another's. The deviations are treated as chance
[@harrer_doing_meta]. If you *do* know something (say, that online delivery works worse) the deviations
aren't exchangeable, and that knowledge belongs in a [subgroup analysis or a
meta-regression](subgroups-and-meta-regression.qmd), not in
$\tau^2$.

If this looks familiar, it should. A study's true effect plus a study-level deviation is the structure of
a [mixed-effects model](../03-regression/mixed-effects-models.qmd). Harrer et al. make the link explicit:
a random-effects meta-analysis is a mixed-effects regression with **only an intercept** ($\mu$) and a random
effect on that intercept [@harrer_doing_meta]. The same structure is a [hierarchical Bayesian
model](../02-inference/hierarchical-bayesian-models.qmd) without the priors.

### What the extra variance does to the weights

Under random-effects each study's weight becomes

$$w_k^* = \frac{1}{s_k^2 + \tau^2}.$$

Every study's variance gets the *same* $\tau^2$ added. For a huge study, $s_k^2$ is tiny, so $\tau^2$
dominates its variance and its weight drops a lot. For a small study, $s_k^2$ was already large, so adding
$\tau^2$ changes little. The weights are **evened out**. That follows from the model: if true effects
genuinely vary, even a perfectly measured study tells you about only *one* draw from the family, so no single
study should dominate the estimate of the family's mean.

## Which model? The convention and its critics

Harrer et al. report the convention in medicine and the social sciences: use random-effects by default,
because some between-study heterogeneity is almost always expected, even with a tight research question
[@harrer_doing_meta]. Fixed-effect is reserved for cases with good reason to believe the effect really is
common, such as exact replications or subsets of one large study. Such cases are rare.

The convention has critics, and their point is worth understanding rather than memorising. Random-effects
gives **small studies more weight**, and small studies are the ones most prone to bias, including the
[publication bias](publication-bias-methods.qmd) that leaves small null studies in the file drawer. For that reason
some authors argue fixed-effect is sometimes preferable, and Stanley, Doucouliagos and Ioannidis argue for an
"unrestricted weighted least squares" model instead in some disciplines [@harrer_doing_meta]. The
disagreement is about which error you fear more. Random-effects protects against **false precision** when
effects vary, and fixed-effect protects against **over-weighting small, possibly biased studies**.

A related bad habit: choosing the model *after* running a heterogeneity test (fixed-effect if Cochran's
$Q$ is non-significant, random-effects if not). Harrer et al. discourage this firmly: the test's power
depends on how many studies you have and how big they are, so its verdict says as much about the size of
the literature as about heterogeneity [@harrer_doing_meta]. The choice should rest on what you believe
about the studies before you see the numbers. See [between-study
heterogeneity](between-study-heterogeneity.qmd) for why $Q$ makes a poor referee.

## Estimating $\tau^2$: a choice that changes the answer

The random-effects weights need $\tau^2$, and $\tau^2$ is unknown. It has to be estimated from how much the
studies disagree *beyond* what their standard errors explain. There are several estimators, and Harrer et
al. list the common ones with their software codes [@harrer_doing_meta]:

| Estimator | How it works | Harrer et al.'s guidance |
|---|---|---|
| **DerSimonian–Laird** (DL) | Closed-form formula built on Cochran's $Q$ | The historical default (RevMan, older `{meta}`), so "random-effects" in old papers often *means* DL. Can be biased when studies are few and heterogeneity high, which is common. Use it if outsiders must replicate your numbers outside R. |
| **Restricted maximum likelihood** (REML) | Iterative; maximises a likelihood that accounts for estimating $\mu$ | A good first choice for continuous outcomes; the `{metafor}` default. |
| **Paule–Mandel** (PM) | Iterative; picks the $\tau^2$ at which a generalised $Q$ equals its expected value | Recommended for binary and continuous data, and suits small meta-analyses; may be off when study sizes vary drastically. Empirical Bayes is practically identical. |
| **Sidik–Jonkman** (SJ) | Closed-form | Only when $\tau^2$ is believed very large and avoiding false positives is the priority. |

Which estimator performs best is still an open research question. It depends on the number of studies, their
sizes, how unequal those sizes are, and how large $\tau^2$ is [@harrer_doing_meta]. Harrer et al.'s practical
advice is reassuring: the differences are often minor, and when in doubt you can **re-run with a different
estimator** and see whether the interpretation changes. That re-run is a sensitivity analysis, and it belongs
in the report.

## Knapp–Hartung: an interval that knows $\tau^2$ was guessed

The usual confidence interval for $\hat\mu$ treats $\hat\tau^2$ as if it were the true value, and builds a
normal-theory (Wald) interval. But $\tau^2$ is estimated from a handful of studies, often badly. The
**Knapp–Hartung adjustment** allows for that uncertainty. It rescales the standard error by how much the
studies actually scatter around $\hat\mu$, and it uses a $t$-distribution with $K-1$ degrees of freedom
instead of the normal [@harrer_doing_meta]. Usually the interval gets somewhat wider.

Harrer et al. call the adjustment usually sensible, because simulation studies show it cuts false
positives, especially when there are few studies [@harrer_doing_meta]. It isn't uncontroversial: in the
rare case of very homogeneous effects it can produce intervals that are *too narrow*. It only applies to
random-effects models. Their suggested methods-section sentence names all three choices: the model
(random-effects, because heterogeneity was expected), the $\tau^2$ estimator (with a citation), and the
interval method (Knapp–Hartung). A reader can't evaluate a pooled estimate without all three.

## Worked example: eighteen trials of third-wave therapy

Harrer et al.'s main example pools **18 randomised trials** of "third-wave" psychotherapies (mindfulness,
ACT and related approaches) for **perceived stress in college students** [@harrer_doing_meta]. Each study
contributes Hedges' $g$, a [standardised mean difference](../02-inference/effect-sizes.qmd) between
treatment and control with the small-sample correction already applied. The studies plainly differ: in risk
of bias, type of control group (wait-list, no intervention, information only), intervention length,
intervention type and mode of delivery. Harrer et al. therefore choose random-effects, REML and
Knapp–Hartung before looking at a single estimate.

Their results [@harrer_doing_meta]:

- **Random-effects pooled effect $g = 0.58$**, 95% CI 0.38 to 0.78, $t = 6.12$, $p < 0.001$.
- **$\hat\tau^2 = 0.08$** (CI 0.03 to 0.35), so the true effects do appear to vary.
- **The fixed-effect estimate, from the same data, is $g = 0.48$.**
- Switching the estimator to **Paule–Mandel** gives $\hat\tau^2 = 0.11$ but barely moves the pooled
  effect, to 0.59.
- The largest random-effects weight, **7.9%**, goes to de Vibe et al. The smallest goes to
  Ratanasiripong, whose wide interval marks an imprecise estimate.

**Why the two models disagree.** We rebuilt the analysis from the effects and confidence intervals in
Harrer et al.'s output (our own arithmetic, which reproduces their printed numbers to the third decimal).
De Vibe et al. is by far the most precise study, and it found a *small* effect, $g = 0.18$. Under
fixed-effect it carries **17.5%** of the total weight. Under random-effects, adding $\tau^2 = 0.08$ to every
variance cuts it to **7.9%**. Meanwhile the three small studies with the largest effects (DanitzOrsillo
$g = 1.79$, Shapiro et al. 1.48, Kang et al. 1.28) roughly **double** their weight, from about 2% each to about
4%. The ratio of largest to smallest weight falls from about 9:1 to about 2:1. The random-effects mean
rises because the model moves weight from the big, small-effect study to the small, large-effect ones,
exactly as described above. For DerSimonian–Laird (also our arithmetic) $\hat\tau^2 = 0.075$ and
$\hat\mu = 0.574$, very close to REML here.

So which is "right"? If you believe these eighteen trials measure one common effect, 0.48. If you believe
they sample a family of effects, 0.58 is your estimate of the family's average. The critics' worry also
applies here: the weight moved toward small studies with big effects. Whether those studies are signal or
small-study bias is a question for [heterogeneity diagnostics](between-study-heterogeneity.qmd) and
[publication-bias methods](publication-bias-methods.qmd), not for the pooling formula.

**A homogeneous contrast.** In Harrer et al.'s second example, nine trials of suicide-prevention
interventions, the studies hardly disagree: $\hat\tau^2 = 0.004$, $I^2 = 7\%$, and $Q$ is not significant
[@harrer_doing_meta]. The pooled $g = -0.23$ (lower suicidal ideation under treatment; Harrer et al.
suggest flipping signs so positive always means benefit) is the same to two decimals under either model,
because a $\tau^2$ near zero leaves the weights almost unchanged. The model choice only matters when the
studies really vary.

**A binary example, on the ratio scale.** Harrer et al. also pool 18 cohort studies of whether depression
predicts all-cause mortality, from Cuijpers and Smit's meta-analysis [@harrer_doing_meta]. The pooled risk
ratio is **2.02** under random-effects and **2.06** under fixed-effect: depression roughly doubles the risk
of death over the follow-up. Heterogeneity is large ($\hat\tau^2 = 0.19$ with Paule–Mandel). Re-running with
REML gives $\hat\tau^2 = 0.16$ and an essentially identical RR of 2.02, the kind of estimator sensitivity
check described above. As odds ratios the pooled effect is 2.29.

## Pooling binary outcomes: when inverse-variance isn't enough

Ratios such as risk and odds ratios are pooled on the **log scale**, because a ratio is symmetric only in
logs (the opposite of RR = 0.5 is 2, not 1.5), and the result is exponentiated back at the end. The same
idea applies to other effect types: correlations are pooled as Fisher's $z$ and proportions as logits
[@harrer_doing_meta]. [Effect sizes for synthesis](effect-sizes-for-synthesis.qmd) covers these
transformations, and their standard errors, in detail.

Inverse-variance pooling of log ratios works, but Harrer et al. call it **suboptimal for sparse binary
data**. When events are few or studies small, the estimated standard error is a poor measure of a
study's real precision [@harrer_doing_meta]. Three alternatives exist for the fixed-effect weights:

- **Mantel–Haenszel** weights come from the 2×2 table's counts rather than an estimated variance. This is
  the default in `metabin`, and Cochrane's general recommendation.
- **Peto's method** uses a special odds ratio built from observed minus expected events. It suits only odds
  ratios, and simulations show it behaves well only when the arms are similar in size, the event is
  **rare (under 1%)**, and the effect is not huge.
- **Sample-size weights** (Bakbergenuly and colleagues) are newer and less studied.

**Zero cells.** A study with no events in one arm breaks the log odds ratio. The traditional fix is to add
0.5 to the empty cells, but continuity corrections are now discouraged because they can bias results.
Mantel–Haenszel needs one only when the same cell is zero in *every* study, so Harrer et al. recommend the
exact Mantel–Haenszel method without a correction [@harrer_doing_meta].

None of this changes the random-effects model, which still pools by inverse variance even when MH is chosen
for the fixed-effect part [@harrer_doing_meta]. For proportions, `metaprop` instead fits a **generalised
linear mixed model** (a random-intercept logistic regression) directly to the event counts, so there are no
study weights to display and $\tau^2$ is estimated by maximum likelihood without a confidence interval.

## Reading and drawing a forest plot

The forest plot is how nearly every meta-analysis reports its result, and Harrer et al. list its parts
[@harrer_doing_meta]. Study labels run down the left. Each study gets a **square at its point estimate**,
sized by its weight, and a **line for its confidence interval**. A **diamond** at the bottom shows the
pooled effect, with its width marking the pooled interval. A vertical **reference line** marks no effect
(0 for differences, 1 for ratios). Ratios go on a **log axis** so that 0.5 and 2 sit equally far from 1.
A good forest plot also prints the numbers behind each row, so anyone can re-run the analysis, and can add
columns such as each study's risk-of-bias rating.

A forest plot fixes one significance level, conventionally a 95% interval. Harrer et al. describe
**drapery plots** as a complement: each study gets a *p-value function*, a curve showing its confidence
interval at every level, so readers aren't anchored on $p < 0.05$ [@harrer_doing_meta]. They recommend
drapery plots alongside forest plots, not instead of them, because the forest plot carries the data needed
for replication.

## Common pitfalls

::: {.callout-warning title="Where this goes wrong"}
- **Reporting "a random-effects model" and nothing else.** The $\tau^2$ estimator and the interval method
  are part of the model. "Random-effects" in older papers usually means DerSimonian–Laird with a Wald
  interval, which can be biased with few, heterogeneous studies. Name all three choices.
  [@harrer_doing_meta]
- **Picking the model from the $Q$-test.** Whether $Q$ is significant depends on the number and size of
  the studies. Decide from what you know about the studies beforehand. [@harrer_doing_meta]
- **Forgetting what $\mu$ means.** Under random-effects, $\hat\mu$ is the *average* of a distribution of
  true effects. When $\tau$ is large, many settings will have effects far from it. Report the spread too
  ([prediction intervals](between-study-heterogeneity.qmd)).
- **Ignoring where the weight went.** Random-effects up-weights small studies. If the small studies are
  also the ones with the biggest effects, as in the worked example, the pooled mean inherits any
  small-study bias. Look at the weights, not just the diamond.
- **Wald intervals with a handful of studies.** With few studies $\hat\tau^2$ is very noisy. Knapp–Hartung
  usually guards against false positives here (though not when the effects are almost identical).
  [@harrer_doing_meta]
- **Pooling raw ratios or raw correlations.** Pool on the log scale (ratios), Fisher's $z$
  (correlations) or the logit (proportions), and back-transform at the end. [@harrer_doing_meta]
- **Automatic continuity corrections.** Adding 0.5 to zero cells can bias the result. Use exact
  Mantel–Haenszel when you can. [@harrer_doing_meta]
- **Treating the pooled number as validated.** Pooling raises precision, not validity: garbage in,
  [precisely-estimated garbage out](meta-analysis.qmd). [@harrer_doing_meta]
:::

## Appendix · the mathematics {.unnumbered}

::: {.math-appendix}

**Fixed (common) effect.** With $\hat\theta_k = \theta + \epsilon_k$, $\epsilon_k \sim \mathcal N(0, s_k^2)$
and $s_k^2$ treated as known,

$$
\hat\theta_{\text{FE}} = \frac{\sum_k w_k \hat\theta_k}{\sum_k w_k}, \quad w_k = \frac{1}{s_k^2},
\qquad \operatorname{Var}(\hat\theta_{\text{FE}}) = \frac{1}{\sum_k w_k}.
$$

This is the minimum-variance unbiased linear combination, and also the maximum-likelihood estimate of $\theta$.

**Random effects.** With $\hat\theta_k = \mu + \zeta_k + \epsilon_k$, $\zeta_k \sim \mathcal N(0, \tau^2)$
independent of $\epsilon_k$, each $\hat\theta_k$ has marginal variance $s_k^2 + \tau^2$. Given $\tau^2$,

$$
\hat\mu = \frac{\sum_k w_k^* \hat\theta_k}{\sum_k w_k^*}, \quad w_k^* = \frac{1}{s_k^2 + \tau^2},
\qquad \operatorname{Var}(\hat\mu) = \frac{1}{\sum_k w_k^*}.
$$

> **In plain words:** the same weighted average, except that each study's noise now includes the spread of
> true effects. As $\tau^2 \to \infty$ the weights become equal and $\hat\mu$ becomes the plain average of
> the studies; at $\tau^2 = 0$ it is the fixed-effect estimate.

Equivalently, this is an intercept-only mixed model, $\hat\theta_k = \beta_0 + u_k + \epsilon_k$, with
$\beta_0 = \mu$ and $u_k = \zeta_k$ [@harrer_doing_meta].

**DerSimonian–Laird.** From $Q = \sum_k w_k(\hat\theta_k - \hat\theta_{\text{FE}})^2$, which has expectation
$(K-1) + \tau^2 C$ with $C = \sum w_k - \sum w_k^2 / \sum w_k$, the method-of-moments estimate is

$$\hat\tau^2_{\text{DL}} = \max\!\left(0,\ \frac{Q - (K-1)}{C}\right).$$

**Paule–Mandel** picks the $\tau^2$ at which the generalised statistic
$Q_{\text{gen}}(\tau^2) = \sum_k w_k^*(\hat\theta_k - \hat\mu)^2$ equals its expectation $K-1$, found by
search, since $Q_{\text{gen}}$ decreases in $\tau^2$.

**REML** maximises the restricted likelihood. One standard way to solve it, used in the figure code, is the
fixed-point update

$$
\tau^2 \leftarrow \frac{\sum_k w_k^{*2}\big[(\hat\theta_k - \hat\mu)^2 - s_k^2\big]}{\sum_k w_k^{*2}}
+ \frac{1}{\sum_k w_k^*},
$$

iterated (truncating at zero) until it stops moving. The final term $1/\sum w_k^*$ is the "restricted" part:
it allows for the degree of freedom spent on estimating $\mu$.

**Knapp–Hartung.** Replace $\operatorname{Var}(\hat\mu) = 1/\sum w_k^*$ with

$$
\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}_{\text{HK}}(\hat\mu) = \frac{\sum_k w_k^*(\hat\theta_k - \hat\mu)^2}{(K-1)\sum_k w_k^*},
$$

and use $t_{K-1}$ quantiles. The ratio $\sum w_k^*(\hat\theta_k-\hat\mu)^2/(K-1)$ is $Q_{\text{gen}}/(K-1)$.
When the studies scatter more than the model expects, it exceeds 1 and the interval widens. When they
scatter less (very homogeneous effects) it falls below 1 and the interval can shrink, which is the source of
the anti-conservative cases Wiksten et al. flag [@harrer_doing_meta]. In the worked example
$\text{SE}_{\text{HK}} = 0.094$ against a Wald SE of $0.087$, and with $t_{17}$ the interval is $0.58 \pm 2.11 \times 0.094$.

**Binary pooling weights** [@harrer_doing_meta]. With cells $a_k, b_k$ (treatment events, non-events), $c_k,
d_k$ (control) and total $n_k$, the Mantel–Haenszel weights are $w_k = (a_k + b_k)c_k / n_k$ for the risk
ratio and $w_k = b_k c_k / n_k$ for the odds ratio. Peto's log odds ratio is $(O_k - E_k)/V_k$, with
$O_k = a_k$, $E_k = (a_k+b_k)(a_k+c_k)/n_k$ and the hypergeometric variance
$V_k = (a_k+b_k)(c_k+d_k)(a_k+c_k)(b_k+d_k)/[n_k^2(n_k-1)]$.
