Doing a Systematic Review: Question, Search, Risk of Bias, Power and Reporting

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source

Most of a meta-analysis happens before any statistics: deciding exactly what question you are answering, finding every study that answers it, judging how far each can be trusted, and writing all of it down so someone else could do it again and get the same set of studies.

A meta-analysis can only be as good as the set of studies fed into it, and that set is built by hand. A systematic review builds it by rules fixed in advance: a precise question, written eligibility criteria, a search wide enough to reach unpublished work, two people screening independently, a risk-of-bias judgement for every study, an analysis plan registered before the data come in, and a report (PRISMA) that lets anyone retrace each step. Each rule exists to block one specific way a literature review goes wrong.

Why it matters

The entries before this one take a table of effect sizes as given and ask how to pool it. In practice that table is the hard part. Harrer et al. estimate from their own experience that the statistical analysis is at most about 15% of the time spent on a meta-analysis. The rest goes on specifying the question, searching, selecting and coding studies (Harrer et al. 2022).

Those steps decide the answer as much as the model does. A review that searches only one database, keeps only journal articles, or lets one enthusiastic expert decide which studies “count” can produce a precise, well-modelled pooled estimate of the wrong thing. Harrer et al. list four classic problems of meta-analysis (Harrer et al. 2022), and each step of a systematic review is a defence against at least one of them:

Problem What goes wrong The step that defends against it
Apples and oranges Studies too different to share one meaningful average A precise question and eligibility criteria (PICO)
Garbage in, garbage out Biased studies give a biased pool Risk-of-bias assessment, sensitivity analyses
File drawer Null results never published, so never found Searching grey literature and trial registries
Researcher agenda Choices bent, knowingly or not, toward the hoped-for answer Analysis plan, preregistration, double screening

Bryman’s textbook makes the same contrast from the social-science side (Clark et al. 2021). A narrative review, the ordinary “literature review” that opens a dissertation, summarises the work its author chooses. The author decides what goes in. A systematic review follows a formal protocol that aims to gather all the relevant research, and the inclusion decisions come from criteria written down beforehand. Its defining feature, in the definition Bryman quotes from Tranfield et al. (2003), is an audit trail: a record of every decision and procedure that lets someone else repeat the review.

Three ways to combine studies

Harrer et al., following Cuijpers, distinguish three kinds of evidence synthesis (Harrer et al. 2022):

  • Narrative review. Written by an expert, with no fixed rules for which studies to include or how to reach conclusions. Useful as an overview when written in a balanced way, but open to the author’s biases.
  • Systematic review. Question set in advance, studies selected by an explicit, reproducible method, the validity of each study assessed against predefined standards. It aims to cover all the evidence.
  • Meta-analysis. A systematic review that goes one step further and combines the quantitative results into one numerical estimate. This usually requires the studies to be more alike (same design, same kind of measurement or intervention) than a systematic review needs.

So most meta-analyses are a kind of systematic review, which is why papers are so often titled “a systematic review and meta-analysis”. Bryman draws the same line: a systematic review of quantitative studies that is summarised quantitatively is a meta-analysis (Clark et al. 2021). When the studies are qualitative, or a mix, the synthesis is instead a narrative synthesis that “tells the story” of the findings, often with simple counts (the share of studies that examined a given issue), or, for qualitative studies, meta-ethnography (Clark et al. 2021).

A fourth form is individual participant data (IPD) meta-analysis, which pools the raw data from each study rather than its published summary. It lets you apply the same analysis to every study and examine participant-level moderators such as age, which aggregate data cannot. It is rare because the data are hard to obtain: one study found IPD could be obtained for only about 64% of eligible studies, and a later review found a median of eleven included studies but IPD from a median of only seven (Harrer et al. 2022).

Step 1: a question precise enough to decide on

Is the question worth asking? Harrer et al. suggest starting from a problem in a field you know: an open dispute, a gap, a group (clinicians, schools, agencies) that needs an answer. Then check the literature: are there primary studies to pool, and has a recent good review already answered it? Cummings et al.’s FINER checklist asks whether the question is Feasible, Interesting, Novel, Ethical and Relevant (Harrer et al. 2022). Sometimes the honest answer is that a meta-analysis is not the right tool, because there are no studies, or because the question is already well answered.

Their worked example is a fictional drug, Chauvicepine, suspected of side effects in women that went unnoticed because the early trials enrolled mostly men. The problem becomes a question: do randomised placebo-controlled trials show that the drug increases negative side effects in women, compared with placebo? (Harrer et al. 2022)

Turn the question into eligibility criteria. The question has to become rules that decide, study by study, in or out. The usual scaffold is PICO (Harrer et al. 2022):

  • Population. Who must the studies include? Each word needs a definition you can apply to a published paper. “Young adults” could mean ages 18 to 30, or anyone recruited at a university. Is a condition diagnosed by a clinician, or is a self-report questionnaire enough?
  • Intervention (or exposure). Which treatments count: how long, delivered by whom, with what content? For a non-intervention question, how must the variable be measured?
  • Control or comparison. Placebo, waitlist, usual care, another treatment, or no comparison at all (as in a review of prevalence)?
  • Outcome. Which measure, on which scale, at what time point?

Harrer et al. add a few criteria PICO leaves out (Harrer et al. 2022):

  • Study design. Evidence-based medicine often admits only randomised trials, but that is a choice, not a rule.
  • Language and culture. Most research comes from WEIRD (western, educated, industrialised, rich, democratic) populations, and many reviews read only English-language papers. If you restrict by language, say so; if the question is about cultural differences, don’t restrict.
  • Publication type. Restricting to peer-reviewed journals feels safer but invites the file-drawer problem. Harrer et al. recommend at least including dissertations, which are rarely fraudulent and are increasingly indexed. Other eligibility criteria can still enforce methodological standards.

The result is a numbered list of inclusion and exclusion criteria. Their example comes from a review of sleep interventions for college students: randomised trials, of students enrolled in tertiary education, receiving a sleep-focused psychological intervention, compared against a passive control (waitlist or usual care), with sleep measured by standardised instruments as a target outcome, published in English or German (Harrer et al. 2022). Each clause is something a screener can check in a paper.

Bryman’s advice for the social sciences adds one step: assemble an advisory panel to help formulate the question and suggest search keywords (Clark et al. 2021). He also notes a difficulty that medicine has less of. Social-science fields often have no agreed key questions, because several theoretical approaches compete, so “the” question for a review is itself contestable (Clark et al. 2021).

Step 2: write the analysis plan, and register it

With the question and criteria set, write down what you will compute before you see any data (Harrer et al. 2022). An a priori analysis is specified before seeing the data; a post hoc (exploratory) one is chosen after. The plan should state at least:

  • which data you will extract and which effect size you will compute;
  • whether you will pool with a fixed- or random-effects model, based on how much the studies are expected to differ;
  • any planned subgroup analyses or meta-regressions, including the exact rule that assigns a study to a subgroup;
  • an a priori power analysis (below), and every other technique you intend to use.

Then make it public: upload the plan to the Open Science Framework or a preprint server, and register the review, for health-related outcomes preferably on PROSPERO, otherwise through OSF Registries. Once an OSF registration is complete, its search plan, hypotheses and analysis strategy can no longer be changed. A full protocol adds the background and more methodological detail, and has its own reporting guideline, PRISMA-P (Harrer et al. 2022).

The plan does two jobs. Others can verify the analyses were planned rather than found by trying things until something worked, and others can reproduce them. Harrer et al. are also clear that plans change, and that a change reported openly reads as professionalism, not failure (Harrer et al. 2022). The general case for preregistration, and its costs, are in open & reproducible science.

Step 3: search more widely than feels necessary

A systematic review aims to find all the evidence, so it searches in several ways at once (Harrer et al. 2022):

  • Previous reviews, whose reference lists are ready-made candidate lists.
  • References of included studies (a backward search).
  • Forward search: everything that has since cited a relevant study, via the journal’s page or Google Scholar.
  • Specialist journals in the field.
  • Bibliographic databases, always more than one, because databases overlap less than you would expect. Harrer et al.’s table lists core databases (PubMed, PsycInfo, CENTRAL, Embase, IBSS, ERIC), citation databases (Web of Science, Scopus, Google Scholar), ProQuest Dissertations, and study registries (WHO ICTRP, OSF Registries), which can turn up studies that were run but never published.

The search string. Combine the PICO terms with Boolean operators (terms joined by AND, synonyms by OR). Use truncation (sociolog* finds sociology, sociological and sociologist) and wildcards (randomi?ed finds both spellings). Err on the side of too many hits: Harrer et al. call around 3,000 hits manageable, and suggest checking a few hundred of them to see whether the string is finding relevant work at all. Save the final strings, include them in the preregistration, and report them (Harrer et al. 2022).

Bryman makes the same two points for social science: describe the strategy in enough detail for others to replicate it, and do not stop at peer-reviewed journal articles. Books, non-peer-reviewed journals and grey literature (conference papers, reports by organisations) also count (Clark et al. 2021). That is the practical defence against the file drawer. The statistical tools that try to detect the damage afterwards are in publication bias, and they work far less well than not having the bias in the first place.

Step 4: select studies in stages, two people at a time

Harrer et al. recommend three stages (Harrer et al. 2022):

  1. Remove duplicates. Pool every hit in a reference manager (Zotero, Mendeley, EndNote), de-duplicate, and record the counts before and after.
  2. Screen titles and abstracts. Remove what is plainly irrelevant. No reason is needed for each exclusion, only the count. If in doubt, keep it: deleting a relevant paper here wastes the whole search. Expect to remove more than 90% of references at this stage.
  3. Assess full texts. Read each remaining article against the criteria, and this time record the reason for every exclusion. If a paper lacks the information needed (the design is unclear, or the results cannot be turned into the planned effect size), contact the corresponding author, at least twice, before excluding it.

Double screening. Nearly all guidelines require at least two people to do each stage independently. The records either screener keeps go forward from the title stage; at the full-text stage each gives reasons, the two compare, disagreements are discussed, and a senior researcher named in advance settles any that remain (Harrer et al. 2022). The same applies to data extraction.

The flow diagram. The numbers go into a PRISMA flow chart: references from databases, from other sources, after de-duplication, removed on title and abstract, removed on full text (with reasons), and finally the studies in the qualitative synthesis (the systematic review) and in the quantitative one (the meta-analysis). The count of articles and of studies can differ, because one article may report two independent studies (Harrer et al. 2022).

Bryman’s worked example shows how steep the funnel is (Clark et al. 2021). Shepherd et al. (2006) reviewed the barriers to, and facilitators of, healthy eating among 11 to 16 year olds in the UK:

Stage Count
References found by the search 7,048
Reports kept after relevance screening 135 (116 studies: 75 intervention, 32 non-intervention, 9 prior reviews)
Met the full inclusion criteria for in-depth review 22 intervention + 8 non-intervention
Intervention studies judged methodologically sound 7

About 98% of the search results fell away at relevance screening alone, and the summary of what works in the end rests on seven studies.

Step 5: extract the data, and judge each study’s risk of bias

From each included study, extract three kinds of information (Harrer et al. 2022):

  • Study characteristics, for the table every good meta-analysis reports: at least first author, year and sample size, plus the PICO details (country, age, share female, intervention, control, outcomes), marking anything a study did not report.
  • The numbers for the effect size, plus any moderators the plan names.
  • Study quality or risk of bias.

Risk of bias is not the same as quality. Bias means systematic error in a study’s results or their interpretation. Risks of bias are features of how the study was run that make such error more likely. A study can meet every quality standard of its field and still be at risk of bias, so the risk-of-bias approach asks a narrower question: are this study’s results believable (Harrer et al. 2022)? The standard instruments come from Cochrane:

  • RoB 2 for randomised trials, which rates each of five domains, and the study overall, as “low”, “some concerns” or “high” risk (Harrer et al. 2022).
  • ROBINS-I for non-randomised studies of interventions, rated on a scale from “low” through “moderate” and “serious” to “critical” (Harrer et al. 2022).
  • QUADAS-2 for studies of diagnostic accuracy (Harrer et al. 2022).

Outside clinical trials, Harrer et al. admit, practice “resembles the Wild West”: in psychology, quality assessment is often inconsistent or skipped. Their suggestions are to check whether RoB 2 or ROBINS-I can be adapted, or, second best, to borrow the approach of previous high-quality reviews on similar topics (Harrer et al. 2022). Bryman’s healthy-eating example used its own seven criteria for non-intervention studies, such as a clear account of how the sample was formed, analysis by more than one researcher, and enough detail to see how the conclusions follow from the data (Clark et al. 2021).

What to do with the judgements. Bryman names the trade-off (Clark et al. 2021). Excluding every study below a quality threshold can leave a tiny sample. The alternative is to grade studies and carry the grades into the synthesis. In a meta-analysis that means a subgroup analysis by risk of bias or a sensitivity analysis restricted to low-risk studies.

Showing them. The Cochrane Handbook recommends two plots, which Chapter 15 of Harrer et al. (by Luke McGuinness) builds with the robvis package (Harrer et al. 2022):

  • A traffic-light plot: one row per study, one coloured dot per domain.
  • A summary bar plot: for each domain, the share at each risk level. By default it is weighted by a measure of each study’s precision, such as its weight in the meta-analysis, so it shows the share of the information at each risk level rather than the share of studies. That is the more honest picture when one large study carries most of the pool.
# Not run: the R calls Harrer et al. use (robvis)
rob_summary(data = rob_table, tool = "ROB2", overall = TRUE)   # weighted bars
rob_traffic_light(data = rob_table, tool = "ROB2")             # study x domain

The input table has the study label first, one column per domain, then an “Overall” judgement and a “Weight” column (Harrer et al. 2022).

Before you start: how many studies will you need?

Pooling studies buys power, but less than people assume. Many meta-analyses include fewer than ten studies; the median in Cochrane reviews is six. Subgroup analyses and meta-regression need more power still, and heterogeneity eats into it too (Harrer et al. 2022). So the plan should include an a priori power analysis. Three things drive it: how many studies you expect and how large they are, the smallest effect worth detecting (for depression treatments, Cuijpers et al. estimated an SMD as small as 0.24 may matter to patients), and the expected heterogeneity (Harrer et al. 2022).

The fixed-effect calculation treats the pool as one big study. With K studies of equal size, the pooled standard error is the single-study standard error divided by \sqrt{K}, and power follows from the usual z-test. Harrer et al.’s example: K = 10 studies, 25 participants per arm, and a true SMD of 0.2. The power is only 60.66%, short of the conventional 80% even with ten studies (Harrer et al. 2022).

Heterogeneity cannot be known before the data arrive, so Harrer et al. use Hedges and Pigott’s rule of thumb: multiply each study’s variance by 1.33 for low, 1.67 for moderate, or 2 for high heterogeneity. With moderate heterogeneity the same ten studies have 40.76% power (Harrer et al. 2022). The figure traces the whole curve.

import math
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def Phi(x):
    return 0.5 * (1 + math.erf(x / math.sqrt(2)))

def power(theta, K, n1, n2, mult=1.0, crit=1.96):
    # Harrer et al. eq 14.3: variance of one study's SMD, then pooled SE for K studies;
    # eqs 14.6-14.8: Hedges & Pigott's heterogeneity multiplier on that variance
    v = (n1 + n2) / (n1 * n2) + theta**2 / (2 * (n1 + n2))
    z = theta / math.sqrt(mult * v / K)
    return 1 - Phi(crit - z) + Phi(-crit - z)                 # eq 14.4

Subgroup differences need even more. Harrer et al.’s example expects one subgroup to show SMD 0.30 (SE 0.13) and the other 0.66 (SE 0.14). The test of that 0.36 difference has only 47% power; for 80% the difference would need to be about 0.54 (Harrer et al. 2022). This is why subgroup findings in small meta-analyses so often fail to reappear, and why the plan should say which subgroup tests are confirmatory.

Never compute power after the fact. Harrer et al. are blunt (citing Hoenig and Heisey): a post hoc power analysis is uninformative, because “observed power” is just a transformation of the p-value you already have. A non-significant result always has observed power of 50% or less. Reading it as evidence leads to the power approach paradox, where a smaller p-value, with its higher observed power, is taken as stronger evidence for the null (Harrer et al. 2022). The same trap in single studies is in statistical power.

Step 6: report it so it can be repeated

Reporting guidelines. For biomedical and intervention reviews, Harrer et al. strongly advise following PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses). For psychology and behavioural research there is the APA’s MARS (Meta-Analysis Reporting Standards). The Cochrane Handbook covers nearly every methodological detail, and Pigott and Polanin (2020) review standards for the social sciences (Harrer et al. 2022). The Campbell Collaboration plays Cochrane’s role for social-science reviews (Harrer et al. 2022). Though these are reporting standards, following them from the start shapes how the review is done.

Reproducibility. Chapter 16 of Harrer et al. covers the tools (Harrer et al. 2022):

  • an R project, so every file is referenced by a relative path and the folder works on anyone’s machine;
  • an R Markdown report that mixes the text, the code and its output, and re-renders when anything changes. This site uses its successor, Quarto (see reproducible analysis with Quarto);
  • an OSF repository holding the data and scripts, private until you choose to make it public, with collaborators added as contributors, and the OSF registration from Step 2.

They justify this with the ordinary life of a project: you find a bug and must rerun part of the analysis, a reviewer asks for another model, a collaborator takes over a piece, or you return after months away. A reproducible workflow makes each of these cheap.

Is a systematic review always the right tool?

Bryman reports the case for it: transparency, a more reliable basis for designing new research, and value for practitioners who need advice drawn from all the evidence, especially where studies conflict (Clark et al. 2021). He also reports three criticisms (Clark et al. 2021):

  • Not every question is “does X work?” The method grew up around intervention questions, and meta-analysis needs that cause-and-effect format. Systematic reviews have since been applied to other questions, such as Rees et al.’s (2013) review of young people’s perceptions of body size and weight, which had no cause-and-effect question at all.
  • It can become bureaucratic, more concerned with the procedure (search terms, databases, how candidates were sieved) than with the interpretation it produces.
  • It assumes quality can be judged objectively, which is especially doubtful for qualitative research, where there is little consensus on how to assess studies.

For a student project, Bryman expects a narrative review to be the norm, while noting that systematic methods can strengthen one (Clark et al. 2021). His student panellist Starr ran a systematic review for a postgraduate project on refugee students’ integration into Western higher education. She used the key concepts as database search terms, and afterwards narrowed her research question to fill a gap the review had documented (Clark et al. 2021).

Common pitfalls

WarningWhere this goes wrong
  • Criteria that cannot be applied to a paper. “Young adults” or “severe symptoms” means nothing until you say how a screener would tell from the article. Vague criteria let inclusion drift toward the expected answer. (Harrer et al. 2022)
  • One database, English only, journals only. Each restriction narrows the evidence, and the last one feeds the file drawer straight into the pool. State any restriction you keep. (Harrer et al. 2022; Clark et al. 2021)
  • Excluding at the title stage when in doubt. A wrongly excluded paper is gone for good; a wrongly kept one costs a few minutes at full-text stage. (Harrer et al. 2022)
  • One person screening. Single screening misses studies and lets one person’s view decide what the evidence is. Use two independent screeners and a pre-named arbiter. (Harrer et al. 2022)
  • Treating a quality score as a risk-of-bias judgement. A study can meet every convention of its field and still be at risk of bias. Ask whether its results are believable, domain by domain. (Harrer et al. 2022)
  • Dropping every weaker study. Bryman’s warning: strict exclusion can leave a review resting on a handful of studies (seven of 7,048 references, in his example). Grade them and analyse by grade instead. (Clark et al. 2021)
  • Assuming ten studies is plenty. With small studies and a small effect, ten studies can have 41% power. Run the power analysis before you start. (Harrer et al. 2022)
  • Post hoc power. Computing power from the observed effect after a null result tells you nothing the p-value did not. (Harrer et al. 2022)
  • An unregistered plan, or a silent change to it. Register the plan before data collection; if it has to change, report the change and why. (Harrer et al. 2022)

Appendix · the mathematics

Power of a fixed-effect meta-analysis. Power is the probability of rejecting H_0 when it is false, 1 - \beta (eq 14.1). For a true SMD \theta and K studies with group sizes n_1, n_2, the variance of one study’s SMD is approximately v = \frac{n_1+n_2}{n_1 n_2} + \frac{\theta^2}{2(n_1+n_2)}, and the pooled standard error is \sigma_\theta = \sqrt{v/K} (eq 14.3). With z = \theta/\sigma_\theta,

\text{Power} = 1 - \Phi(c_\alpha - z) + \Phi(-c_\alpha - z), \qquad c_{0.05} = 1.96 \quad \text{(eq 14.4)}.

In plain words: the pooled estimate is centred on z standard errors from zero; power is the chance it lands beyond \pm 1.96.

Example: v = 50/625 + 0.04/100 = 0.0804, \sigma_\theta = \sqrt{0.00804} = 0.0897, z = 2.23, power = 0.6066.

Random effects. Adding \tau^2 gives \sigma^*_\theta = \sqrt{(v + \tau^2)/K} (eq 14.5). Hedges and Pigott replace the unknown \tau^2 by a multiplier m on the variance, \sigma^*_\theta = \sqrt{m\,v/K} with m = 1.33, 1.67, 2 (eqs 14.6–14.8). Since m = 1 + \tau^2/v, the multipliers mean \tau^2 = 0.33v, 0.67v and v: between-study variance making up 0.33/1.33 = 25\%, 0.67/1.67 = 40\% and 1/2 = 50\% of each study’s total variance (our reading, an I^2-like share). For the example, moderate heterogeneity gives z = 0.2/\sqrt{1.67 \times 0.00804} = 1.73 and power 0.4076.

Studies needed. Ignoring the negligible lower tail, power 1-\beta requires z \ge c_\alpha + z_\beta, so

K \;\ge\; \frac{(c_\alpha + z_\beta)^2\, m\, v}{\theta^2}.

In plain words: halve the effect you want to detect and you need four times the studies; double the variance through heterogeneity and you need twice as many.

For 80% power, (1.96 + 0.8416)^2 = 7.85, so K \ge 7.85 \times 0.0804 / 0.04 = 15.8: 16 studies under fixed effect, and m times that (rounded up: 21, 27, 32) under the three heterogeneity levels, as in the figure.

Subgroup differences. For two independent subgroup estimates, the difference \Delta = \hat\theta_2 - \hat\theta_1 has standard error \sqrt{\text{SE}_1^2 + \text{SE}_2^2}. In the example \sqrt{0.13^2 + 0.14^2} = 0.191, z = 0.36/0.191 = 1.88, power 0.470, and 80% power needs \Delta \ge 2.80 \times 0.191 = 0.535. Harrer et al. print 0.536, a rounding difference.

Why post hoc power is empty. Plug the observed effect into the power formula and z becomes the observed test statistic, so “observed power” is a fixed, decreasing function of the p-value. At p = 0.05 exactly, z = 1.96 and power = 1 - \Phi(0) + \Phi(-3.92) \approx 0.50. Any non-significant result therefore has observed power at or below about 50%, which is the point Harrer et al. take from Hoenig and Heisey.

## Why it matters

The entries before this one take a table of effect sizes as given and ask how to pool it. In practice that
table is the hard part. Harrer et al. estimate from their own experience that the statistical analysis is at
most about **15% of the time** spent on a meta-analysis. The rest goes on specifying the question, searching,
selecting and coding studies [@harrer_doing_meta].

Those steps decide the answer as much as the model does. A review that searches only one database, keeps
only journal articles, or lets one enthusiastic expert decide which studies "count" can produce a precise,
well-modelled pooled estimate of the wrong thing. Harrer et al. list four classic problems of meta-analysis
[@harrer_doing_meta], and each step of a systematic review is a defence against at least one of them:

| Problem | What goes wrong | The step that defends against it |
|---|---|---|
| **Apples and oranges** | Studies too different to share one meaningful average | A precise question and eligibility criteria (PICO) |
| **Garbage in, garbage out** | Biased studies give a biased pool | Risk-of-bias assessment, sensitivity analyses |
| **File drawer** | Null results never published, so never found | Searching grey literature and trial registries |
| **Researcher agenda** | Choices bent, knowingly or not, toward the hoped-for answer | Analysis plan, preregistration, double screening |

Bryman's textbook makes the same contrast from the social-science side [@clark_bryman_2021]. A **narrative
review**, the ordinary "literature review" that opens a dissertation, summarises the work its author chooses.
The author decides what goes in. A **systematic review** follows a formal protocol that aims to gather *all*
the relevant research, and the inclusion decisions come from criteria written down beforehand. Its defining
feature, in the definition Bryman quotes from Tranfield et al. (2003), is an **audit trail**: a record of every
decision and procedure that lets someone else repeat the review.

## Three ways to combine studies

Harrer et al., following Cuijpers, distinguish three kinds of evidence synthesis [@harrer_doing_meta]:

- **Narrative review.** Written by an expert, with no fixed rules for which studies to include or how to
  reach conclusions. Useful as an overview when written in a balanced way, but open to the author's biases.
- **Systematic review.** Question set in advance, studies selected by an explicit, reproducible method, the
  validity of each study assessed against predefined standards. It aims to cover all the evidence.
- **Meta-analysis.** A systematic review that goes one step further and **combines the quantitative results**
  into one numerical estimate. This usually requires the studies to be more alike (same design, same kind of
  measurement or intervention) than a systematic review needs.

So most meta-analyses are a kind of systematic review, which is why papers are so often titled "a systematic
review and meta-analysis". Bryman draws the same line: a systematic review of quantitative studies that is
summarised quantitatively is a meta-analysis [@clark_bryman_2021]. When the studies are qualitative, or a mix,
the synthesis is instead a **narrative synthesis** that "tells the story" of the findings, often with simple
counts (the share of studies that examined a given issue), or, for qualitative studies, **meta-ethnography**
[@clark_bryman_2021].

A fourth form is **individual participant data (IPD) meta-analysis**, which pools the raw data from each study
rather than its published summary. It lets you apply the same analysis to every study and examine
participant-level moderators such as age, which aggregate data cannot. It is rare because the data are hard
to obtain: one study found IPD could be obtained for only about **64%** of eligible studies, and a later review
found a median of **eleven** included studies but IPD from a median of only **seven** [@harrer_doing_meta].

## Step 1: a question precise enough to decide on

**Is the question worth asking?** Harrer et al. suggest starting from a problem in a field you know: an open
dispute, a gap, a group (clinicians, schools, agencies) that needs an answer. Then check the literature: are
there primary studies to pool, and has a recent good review already answered it? Cummings et al.'s **FINER**
checklist asks whether the question is **F**easible, **I**nteresting, **N**ovel, **E**thical and **R**elevant
[@harrer_doing_meta]. Sometimes the honest answer is that a meta-analysis is not the right tool, because there
are no studies, or because the question is already well answered.

Their worked example is a fictional drug, *Chauvicepine*, suspected of side effects in women that went
unnoticed because the early trials enrolled mostly men. The problem becomes a question: do randomised
placebo-controlled trials show that the drug increases negative side effects in women, compared with placebo?
[@harrer_doing_meta]

**Turn the question into eligibility criteria.** The question has to become rules that decide, study by
study, in or out. The usual scaffold is **PICO** [@harrer_doing_meta]:

- **Population.** Who must the studies include? Each word needs a definition you can apply to a published
  paper. "Young adults" could mean ages 18 to 30, or anyone recruited at a university. Is a condition
  diagnosed by a clinician, or is a self-report questionnaire enough?
- **Intervention** (or exposure). Which treatments count: how long, delivered by whom, with what content? For
  a non-intervention question, how must the variable be measured?
- **Control or comparison.** Placebo, waitlist, usual care, another treatment, or no comparison at all (as in
  a review of prevalence)?
- **Outcome.** Which measure, on which scale, at what time point?

Harrer et al. add a few criteria PICO leaves out [@harrer_doing_meta]:

- **Study design.** Evidence-based medicine often admits only randomised trials, but that is a choice, not a
  rule.
- **Language and culture.** Most research comes from WEIRD (western, educated, industrialised, rich,
  democratic) populations, and many reviews read only English-language papers. If you restrict by language,
  say so; if the question is about cultural differences, don't restrict.
- **Publication type.** Restricting to peer-reviewed journals feels safer but invites the file-drawer
  problem. Harrer et al. recommend at least including **dissertations**, which are rarely fraudulent and are
  increasingly indexed. Other eligibility criteria can still enforce methodological standards.

The result is a numbered list of inclusion and exclusion criteria. Their example comes from a review of
sleep interventions for college students: randomised trials, of students enrolled in tertiary education,
receiving a sleep-focused psychological intervention, compared against a passive control (waitlist or usual
care), with sleep measured by standardised instruments as a target outcome, published in English or German
[@harrer_doing_meta]. Each clause is something a screener can check in a paper.

Bryman's advice for the social sciences adds one step: assemble an advisory **panel** to help formulate the
question and suggest search keywords [@clark_bryman_2021]. He also notes a difficulty that medicine has less
of. Social-science fields often have no agreed key questions, because several theoretical approaches compete,
so "the" question for a review is itself contestable [@clark_bryman_2021].

## Step 2: write the analysis plan, and register it

With the question and criteria set, write down what you will compute **before** you see any data
[@harrer_doing_meta]. An **a priori** analysis is specified before seeing the data; a **post hoc** (exploratory)
one is chosen after. The plan should state at least:

- which data you will extract and which [effect size](effect-sizes-for-synthesis.qmd) you will compute;
- whether you will pool with a [fixed- or random-effects model](pooling-effect-sizes.qmd), based on how much
  the studies are expected to differ;
- any planned [subgroup analyses or meta-regressions](subgroups-and-meta-regression.qmd), including the exact
  rule that assigns a study to a subgroup;
- an a priori **power analysis** (below), and every other technique you intend to use.

Then make it public: upload the plan to the Open Science Framework or a preprint server, and **register** the
review, for health-related outcomes preferably on **PROSPERO**, otherwise through OSF Registries. Once an OSF
registration is complete, its search plan, hypotheses and analysis strategy can no longer be changed. A full
**protocol** adds the background and more methodological detail, and has its own reporting guideline,
**PRISMA-P** [@harrer_doing_meta].

The plan does two jobs. Others can verify the analyses were planned rather than found by trying things
until something worked, and others can reproduce them. Harrer et al. are also clear that plans change, and
that a change reported openly reads as professionalism, not failure [@harrer_doing_meta]. The general case for
preregistration, and its costs, are in [open & reproducible science](open-and-reproducible-science.qmd#sec-preregistration).

## Step 3: search more widely than feels necessary

A systematic review aims to find **all** the evidence, so it searches in several ways at once
[@harrer_doing_meta]:

- **Previous reviews**, whose reference lists are ready-made candidate lists.
- **References of included studies** (a backward search).
- **Forward search**: everything that has since *cited* a relevant study, via the journal's page or Google
  Scholar.
- **Specialist journals** in the field.
- **Bibliographic databases**, always **more than one**, because databases overlap less than you would
  expect. Harrer et al.'s table lists core databases (PubMed, PsycInfo, CENTRAL, Embase, IBSS, ERIC), citation
  databases (Web of Science, Scopus, Google Scholar), ProQuest Dissertations, and **study registries** (WHO
  ICTRP, OSF Registries), which can turn up studies that were run but never published.

**The search string.** Combine the PICO terms with Boolean operators (terms joined by AND, synonyms by OR).
Use **truncation** (`sociolog*` finds sociology, sociological and sociologist) and **wildcards** (`randomi?ed`
finds both spellings). Err on the side of too many hits: Harrer et al. call around 3,000 hits manageable, and
suggest checking a few hundred of them to see whether the string is finding relevant work at all. Save the
final strings, include them in the preregistration, and report them [@harrer_doing_meta].

Bryman makes the same two points for social science: describe the strategy in enough detail for others to
replicate it, and do not stop at peer-reviewed journal articles. Books, non-peer-reviewed journals and
**grey literature** (conference papers, reports by organisations) also count [@clark_bryman_2021]. That is the
practical defence against the file drawer. The statistical tools that try to detect the damage afterwards are
in [publication bias](publication-bias-methods.qmd), and they work far less well than not having the bias in
the first place.

## Step 4: select studies in stages, two people at a time

Harrer et al. recommend three stages [@harrer_doing_meta]:

1. **Remove duplicates.** Pool every hit in a reference manager (Zotero, Mendeley, EndNote), de-duplicate,
   and record the counts before and after.
2. **Screen titles and abstracts.** Remove what is plainly irrelevant. No reason is needed for each
   exclusion, only the count. **If in doubt, keep it**: deleting a relevant paper here wastes the whole search.
   Expect to remove more than 90% of references at this stage.
3. **Assess full texts.** Read each remaining article against the criteria, and this time **record the reason**
   for every exclusion. If a paper lacks the information needed (the design is unclear, or the results cannot
   be turned into the planned effect size), contact the corresponding author, **at least twice**, before
   excluding it.

**Double screening.** Nearly all guidelines require at least two people to do each stage independently. The
records either screener keeps go forward from the title stage; at the full-text stage each gives reasons,
the two compare, disagreements are discussed, and a senior researcher named in advance settles any that
remain [@harrer_doing_meta]. The same applies to data extraction.

**The flow diagram.** The numbers go into a **PRISMA flow chart**: references from databases, from other
sources, after de-duplication, removed on title and abstract, removed on full text (with reasons), and finally
the studies in the qualitative synthesis (the systematic review) and in the quantitative one (the
meta-analysis). The count of **articles** and of **studies** can differ, because one article may report two
independent studies [@harrer_doing_meta].

Bryman's worked example shows how steep the funnel is [@clark_bryman_2021]. Shepherd et al. (2006) reviewed
the barriers to, and facilitators of, healthy eating among 11 to 16 year olds in the UK:

| Stage | Count |
|---|---|
| References found by the search | 7,048 |
| Reports kept after relevance screening | 135 (116 studies: 75 intervention, 32 non-intervention, 9 prior reviews) |
| Met the full inclusion criteria for in-depth review | 22 intervention + 8 non-intervention |
| Intervention studies judged methodologically sound | 7 |

About 98% of the search results fell away at relevance screening alone, and the summary of what works in the
end rests on seven studies.

## Step 5: extract the data, and judge each study's risk of bias

From each included study, extract three kinds of information [@harrer_doing_meta]:

- **Study characteristics**, for the table every good meta-analysis reports: at least first author, year and
  sample size, plus the PICO details (country, age, share female, intervention, control, outcomes), marking
  anything a study did not report.
- **The numbers for the effect size**, plus any moderators the plan names.
- **Study quality or risk of bias.**

**Risk of bias is not the same as quality.** Bias means systematic error in a study's results or their
interpretation. Risks of bias are features of how the study was run that make such error more likely. A study
can meet every quality standard of its field and still be at risk of bias, so the risk-of-bias approach asks a
narrower question: are this study's results **believable** [@harrer_doing_meta]? The standard instruments
come from Cochrane:

- **RoB 2** for randomised trials, which rates each of five domains, and the study overall, as "low", "some
  concerns" or "high" risk [@harrer_doing_meta].
- **ROBINS-I** for non-randomised studies of interventions, rated on a scale from "low" through "moderate" and
  "serious" to "critical" [@harrer_doing_meta].
- **QUADAS-2** for studies of diagnostic accuracy [@harrer_doing_meta].

Outside clinical trials, Harrer et al. admit, practice "resembles the Wild West": in psychology, quality
assessment is often inconsistent or skipped. Their suggestions are to check whether RoB 2 or ROBINS-I can be
adapted, or, second best, to borrow the approach of previous high-quality reviews on similar topics
[@harrer_doing_meta]. Bryman's healthy-eating example used its own seven criteria for non-intervention studies,
such as a clear account of how the sample was formed, analysis by more than one researcher, and enough
detail to see how the conclusions follow from the data [@clark_bryman_2021].

**What to do with the judgements.** Bryman names the trade-off [@clark_bryman_2021]. Excluding every study below
a quality threshold can leave a tiny sample. The alternative is to grade studies and carry the grades into the
synthesis. In a meta-analysis that means a [subgroup analysis by risk of bias](subgroups-and-meta-regression.qmd)
or a sensitivity analysis restricted to low-risk studies.

**Showing them.** The Cochrane Handbook recommends two plots, which Chapter 15 of Harrer et al. (by Luke
McGuinness) builds with the `robvis` package [@harrer_doing_meta]:

- A **traffic-light plot**: one row per study, one coloured dot per domain.
- A **summary bar plot**: for each domain, the share at each risk level. By default it is **weighted by a measure
  of each study's precision**, such as its weight in the meta-analysis, so it shows the share of the *information* at each risk level rather
  than the share of *studies*. That is the more honest picture when one large study carries most of the pool.

```r
# Not run: the R calls Harrer et al. use (robvis)
rob_summary(data = rob_table, tool = "ROB2", overall = TRUE)   # weighted bars
rob_traffic_light(data = rob_table, tool = "ROB2")             # study x domain
```

The input table has the study label first, one column per domain, then an "Overall" judgement and a
"Weight" column [@harrer_doing_meta].

## Before you start: how many studies will you need?

Pooling studies buys power, but less than people assume. Many meta-analyses include fewer than ten studies;
the median in Cochrane reviews is **six**. Subgroup analyses and meta-regression need more power still, and
heterogeneity eats into it too [@harrer_doing_meta]. So the plan should include an **a priori** power analysis.
Three things drive it: how many studies you expect and how large they are, the **smallest effect worth
detecting** (for depression treatments, Cuijpers et al. estimated an SMD as small as 0.24 may matter to
patients), and the expected heterogeneity [@harrer_doing_meta].

The fixed-effect calculation treats the pool as one big study. With $K$ studies of equal size, the pooled
standard error is the single-study standard error divided by $\sqrt{K}$, and power follows from the usual
$z$-test. Harrer et al.'s example: $K = 10$ studies, 25 participants per arm, and a true SMD of 0.2. The power
is only **60.66%**, short of the conventional 80% even with ten studies [@harrer_doing_meta].

Heterogeneity cannot be known before the data arrive, so Harrer et al. use Hedges and Pigott's rule of thumb:
multiply each study's variance by **1.33** for low, **1.67** for moderate, or **2** for high heterogeneity.
With moderate heterogeneity the same ten studies have **40.76%** power [@harrer_doing_meta]. The figure
traces the whole curve.

```{python}
#| label: fig-ma-power
#| fig-cap: "Power of a meta-analysis to detect a true SMD of 0.2, as the number of studies grows (each study has 25 participants per arm, two-sided α = 0.05), computed by hand from Harrer et al.'s formulas (eqs 14.3 to 14.8). The four curves are the fixed-effect model and Hedges and Pigott's low, moderate and high heterogeneity multipliers (1.33, 1.67, 2). The dots at K = 10 reproduce Harrer et al.'s two printed results: 60.66% (fixed effect) and 40.76% (moderate heterogeneity, in orange). Reaching the 80% line (dashed) takes 16 studies with no heterogeneity, 27 with moderate and 32 with high; the high multiplier doubles each study's variance, so it doubles the number of studies needed."
#| code-fold: true
#| code-summary: "Show the figure code"

import math
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def Phi(x):
    return 0.5 * (1 + math.erf(x / math.sqrt(2)))

def power(theta, K, n1, n2, mult=1.0, crit=1.96):
    # Harrer et al. eq 14.3: variance of one study's SMD, then pooled SE for K studies;
    # eqs 14.6-14.8: Hedges & Pigott's heterogeneity multiplier on that variance
    v = (n1 + n2) / (n1 * n2) + theta**2 / (2 * (n1 + n2))
    z = theta / math.sqrt(mult * v / K)
    return 1 - Phi(crit - z) + Phi(-crit - z)                 # eq 14.4

theta, n = 0.2, 25
Ks = np.arange(1, 41)
models = [("fixed effect", 1.0, "#161613"), ("low heterogeneity (×1.33)", 1.33, "#b7b6a8"),
          ("moderate (×1.67)", 1.67, "#e8531d"), ("high (×2)", 2.0, "#5e5e54")]

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7.4, 4.6))
for lab, m, col in models:
    p = np.array([power(theta, K, n, n, m) for K in Ks])
    ax.plot(Ks, 100 * p, color=col, lw=2.0 if m == 1.67 else 1.6, label=lab)
    k80 = Ks[np.argmax(p >= 0.8)]
    ax.plot(k80, 100 * power(theta, k80, n, n, m), "|", color=col, ms=10, mew=1.6)

for m, col, txt in [(1.0, "#161613", (2.0, 70)), (1.67, "#e8531d", (13.0, 22))]:
    p10 = 100 * power(theta, 10, n, n, m)                     # 60.66 and 40.76
    ax.plot(10, p10, "o", color=col, ms=7, zorder=5)
    ax.annotate(f"{p10:.2f}% at K = 10", (10, p10), xytext=txt, color=col, fontsize=9,
                arrowprops=dict(arrowstyle="-", color=col, lw=0.8))

ax.axhline(80, color="#8a8a7e", ls="--", lw=1.0)
ax.text(40.5, 81.5, "80% convention", ha="right", fontsize=8.5, color="#8a8a7e")
ax.set_xlim(0, 41)
ax.set_ylim(0, 100)
ax.set_xlabel("number of studies in the meta-analysis, K")
ax.set_ylabel("power to detect SMD = 0.2 (%)")
ax.set_title("Ten small studies are not enough to find a small effect", fontsize=10.5)
ax.legend(loc="lower right", fontsize=8.5, frameon=False)
fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

**Subgroup differences need even more.** Harrer et al.'s example expects one subgroup to show SMD 0.30
(SE 0.13) and the other 0.66 (SE 0.14). The test of that 0.36 difference has only **47%** power; for 80% the
difference would need to be about **0.54** [@harrer_doing_meta]. This is why subgroup findings in small
meta-analyses so often fail to reappear, and why the plan should say which subgroup tests are confirmatory.

**Never compute power after the fact.** Harrer et al. are blunt (citing Hoenig and Heisey): a post hoc power
analysis is uninformative, because "observed power" is just a transformation of the $p$-value you already
have. A non-significant result always has observed power of 50% or less. Reading it as evidence leads to the
**power approach paradox**, where a smaller $p$-value, with its higher observed power, is taken as stronger
evidence *for* the null [@harrer_doing_meta]. The same trap in single studies is in
[statistical power](../02-inference/statistical-power.qmd).

## Step 6: report it so it can be repeated

**Reporting guidelines.** For biomedical and intervention reviews, Harrer et al. strongly advise following
**PRISMA** (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses). For psychology and behavioural
research there is the APA's **MARS** (Meta-Analysis Reporting Standards). The **Cochrane Handbook** covers
nearly every methodological detail, and Pigott and Polanin (2020) review standards for the social sciences
[@harrer_doing_meta]. The **Campbell Collaboration** plays Cochrane's role for social-science reviews
[@harrer_doing_meta]. Though these are reporting standards, following them from the start shapes how the review
is done.

**Reproducibility.** Chapter 16 of Harrer et al. covers the tools [@harrer_doing_meta]:

- an **R project**, so every file is referenced by a relative path and the folder works on anyone's machine;
- an **R Markdown** report that mixes the text, the code and its output, and re-renders when anything
  changes. This site uses its successor, Quarto (see [reproducible analysis with
  Quarto](../../appendices/computing/reproducible-quarto.qmd));
- an **OSF repository** holding the data and scripts, private until you choose to make it public, with
  collaborators added as contributors, and the OSF registration from Step 2.

They justify this with the ordinary life of a project: you find a bug and must rerun part of the analysis, a
reviewer asks for another model, a collaborator takes over a piece, or you return after months away. A
reproducible workflow makes each of these cheap.

## Is a systematic review always the right tool?

Bryman reports the case for it: transparency, a more reliable basis for designing new research, and value for
practitioners who need advice drawn from all the evidence, especially where studies conflict
[@clark_bryman_2021]. He also reports three criticisms [@clark_bryman_2021]:

- **Not every question is "does X work?"** The method grew up around intervention questions, and meta-analysis
  needs that cause-and-effect format. Systematic reviews have since been applied to other questions, such as
  Rees et al.'s (2013) review of young people's perceptions of body size and weight, which had no
  cause-and-effect question at all.
- **It can become bureaucratic**, more concerned with the procedure (search terms, databases, how candidates
  were sieved) than with the interpretation it produces.
- **It assumes quality can be judged objectively**, which is especially doubtful for qualitative research,
  where there is little consensus on how to assess studies.

For a student project, Bryman expects a narrative review to be the norm, while noting that systematic
methods can strengthen one [@clark_bryman_2021]. His student panellist Starr ran a systematic review for a
postgraduate project on refugee students' integration into Western higher education. She used the key
concepts as database search terms, and afterwards narrowed her research question to fill a gap the review had
documented [@clark_bryman_2021].

## Common pitfalls

::: {.callout-warning title="Where this goes wrong"}
- **Criteria that cannot be applied to a paper.** "Young adults" or "severe symptoms" means nothing until you
  say how a screener would tell from the article. Vague criteria let inclusion drift toward the expected
  answer. [@harrer_doing_meta]
- **One database, English only, journals only.** Each restriction narrows the evidence, and the last one
  feeds the file drawer straight into the pool. State any restriction you keep. [@harrer_doing_meta;
  @clark_bryman_2021]
- **Excluding at the title stage when in doubt.** A wrongly excluded paper is gone for good; a wrongly kept one
  costs a few minutes at full-text stage. [@harrer_doing_meta]
- **One person screening.** Single screening misses studies and lets one person's view decide what the
  evidence is. Use two independent screeners and a pre-named arbiter. [@harrer_doing_meta]
- **Treating a quality score as a risk-of-bias judgement.** A study can meet every convention of its field
  and still be at risk of bias. Ask whether its results are believable, domain by domain. [@harrer_doing_meta]
- **Dropping every weaker study.** Bryman's warning: strict exclusion can leave a review resting on a handful of
  studies (seven of 7,048 references, in his example). Grade them and analyse by grade instead.
  [@clark_bryman_2021]
- **Assuming ten studies is plenty.** With small studies and a small effect, ten studies can have 41% power.
  Run the power analysis before you start. [@harrer_doing_meta]
- **Post hoc power.** Computing power from the observed effect after a null result tells you nothing the
  $p$-value did not. [@harrer_doing_meta]
- **An unregistered plan, or a silent change to it.** Register the plan before data collection; if it has to
  change, report the change and why. [@harrer_doing_meta]
:::

## Appendix · the mathematics {.unnumbered}

::: {.math-appendix}

**Power of a fixed-effect meta-analysis.** Power is the probability of rejecting $H_0$ when it is false,
$1 - \beta$ (eq 14.1). For a true SMD $\theta$ and $K$ studies with group sizes $n_1, n_2$, the variance of one
study's SMD is approximately $v = \frac{n_1+n_2}{n_1 n_2} + \frac{\theta^2}{2(n_1+n_2)}$, and the pooled standard
error is $\sigma_\theta = \sqrt{v/K}$ (eq 14.3). With $z = \theta/\sigma_\theta$,

$$
\text{Power} = 1 - \Phi(c_\alpha - z) + \Phi(-c_\alpha - z), \qquad c_{0.05} = 1.96 \quad \text{(eq 14.4)}.
$$

> **In plain words:** the pooled estimate is centred on $z$ standard errors from zero; power is the chance it
> lands beyond $\pm 1.96$.

Example: $v = 50/625 + 0.04/100 = 0.0804$, $\sigma_\theta = \sqrt{0.00804} = 0.0897$, $z = 2.23$, power
$= 0.6066$.

**Random effects.** Adding $\tau^2$ gives $\sigma^*_\theta = \sqrt{(v + \tau^2)/K}$ (eq 14.5). Hedges and Pigott
replace the unknown $\tau^2$ by a multiplier $m$ on the variance, $\sigma^*_\theta = \sqrt{m\,v/K}$ with
$m = 1.33, 1.67, 2$ (eqs 14.6–14.8). Since $m = 1 + \tau^2/v$, the multipliers mean $\tau^2 = 0.33v$, $0.67v$ and
$v$: between-study variance making up $0.33/1.33 = 25\%$, $0.67/1.67 = 40\%$ and $1/2 = 50\%$ of each study's
total variance (our reading, an $I^2$-like share). For the example, moderate heterogeneity gives
$z = 0.2/\sqrt{1.67 \times 0.00804} = 1.73$ and power $0.4076$.

**Studies needed.** Ignoring the negligible lower tail, power $1-\beta$ requires $z \ge c_\alpha + z_\beta$, so

$$
K \;\ge\; \frac{(c_\alpha + z_\beta)^2\, m\, v}{\theta^2}.
$$

> **In plain words:** halve the effect you want to detect and you need four times the studies; double the
> variance through heterogeneity and you need twice as many.

For 80% power, $(1.96 + 0.8416)^2 = 7.85$, so $K \ge 7.85 \times 0.0804 / 0.04 = 15.8$: 16 studies under fixed
effect, and $m$ times that (rounded up: 21, 27, 32) under the three heterogeneity levels, as in the figure.

**Subgroup differences.** For two independent subgroup estimates, the difference $\Delta = \hat\theta_2 -
\hat\theta_1$ has standard error $\sqrt{\text{SE}_1^2 + \text{SE}_2^2}$. In the example $\sqrt{0.13^2 + 0.14^2} =
0.191$, $z = 0.36/0.191 = 1.88$, power $0.470$, and 80% power needs $\Delta \ge 2.80 \times 0.191 = 0.535$.
Harrer et al. print 0.536, a rounding difference.

**Why post hoc power is empty.** Plug the observed effect into the power formula and $z$ becomes the observed
test statistic, so "observed power" is a fixed, decreasing function of the $p$-value. At $p = 0.05$ exactly,
$z = 1.96$ and power $= 1 - \Phi(0) + \Phi(-3.92) \approx 0.50$. Any non-significant result therefore has
observed power at or below about 50%, which is the point Harrer et al. take from Hoenig and Heisey.

:::
