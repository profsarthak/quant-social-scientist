Subgroups and Meta-Regression: Explaining the Disagreement

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source

Measuring heterogeneity says how much the studies disagree. Subgroup analysis and meta-regression ask why, by testing whether some kind of study gets bigger effects than another. They are one model, they have little power, and they can only ever show an association between studies.

Once you know the studies disagree, the next question is why. A subgroup analysis pools each kind of study separately (high vs low risk of bias, children vs adults) and tests whether the pooled effects differ by more than chance. A meta-regression does the same with a continuous study characteristic, such as publication year, and estimates a slope. The two are one model: a regression of effect sizes on study features, with each study weighted by its precision and room left for heterogeneity that the features don’t explain. Treat the results as observational. Decide the predictors before you see the data, and remember that a non-significant difference between subgroups does not show they are the same.

Why it matters

Measuring heterogeneity and hunting for outliers is post hoc. It happens after seeing the data, often because of the results, and it looks only at the numbers (Harrer et al. 2022). An influence analysis can tell you that three studies don’t fit. It cannot tell you why. Harrer et al.’s example: a meta-analysis finds that a treatment does nothing on average, except in three studies with a large effect. Perhaps all three used a slightly different version of the treatment, and that detail is what made it work. That would be an important discovery, and no outlier diagnostic could make it (Harrer et al. 2022).

Subgroup analysis (also called moderator analysis) and meta-regression are the tools for the why. They test specific hypotheses about which kinds of study produce bigger or smaller effects: one medication against another, short against long follow-up, one cultural region against another (Harrer et al. 2022). They change how heterogeneity is treated. It is no longer just noise around the average but variation that a scientific hypothesis might explain. At best that explains something about the world; at least it gives practical guidance for the next decision.

The hypotheses should be fixed a priori, in the analysis plan, and coded for every study before the analysis starts. Effects can differ for endless reasons, so restrict the list to the ones that matter for the question, which is where subject knowledge pays off (Harrer et al. 2022).

Subgroup analysis: the fixed-effects (plural) model

A subgroup analysis assumes the studies do not come from one population. They fall into groups, each with its own true overall effect, and the aim is to reject the null hypothesis that the groups’ effects are equal (Harrer et al. 2022). It works in two steps, following Borenstein and Higgins.

Step 1: pool within each subgroup. This is an ordinary meta-analysis per group, and the usual choice applies (see pooling). A common effect within a subgroup is usually as unrealistic as it is overall, so each group normally gets its own random-effects model and its own pooled effect \hat\mu_g (Harrer et al. 2022). Each group then also gets its own \hat\tau^2_g. In practice these are often replaced by a common \tau^2 pooled across groups. That is a practical choice: with five or fewer studies in a group (Borenstein et al.), a group’s own \tau^2 is too imprecise to trust, and a shared estimate is better.

Step 2: compare the subgroups. Borenstein et al.’s trick is to treat each subgroup’s pooled effect as if it were one large study (Harrer et al. 2022). With three subgroups you have, in effect, three big studies, and the question is the familiar heterogeneity question: do they differ by more than their sampling error allows? So the test is Cochran’s Q on the subgroup estimates, compared with a \chi^2 distribution on G-1 degrees of freedom. It is an omnibus test: significant when at least two subgroups (or some combination of them) differ, without saying which.

Why “fixed-effects (plural)”. Within subgroups the studies follow a random-effects model. The subgroups themselves are treated as fixed: they are the real and only categories the variable can take, not a random draw from a universe of possible subgroups, so there is nothing further to generalise to (Harrer et al. 2022). Employment status has two levels, employed and unemployed, and that is all. Other examples of fixed levels: age group, Western vs non-Western background, the type of control group, self-report vs expert rating, high/low/unclear study quality, school vs hospital vs home setting. The model therefore has several true effects (as in random effects) whose levels are fixed (unlike random effects), hence the “plural”.

Borenstein et al. point out that “fixed” means two things in this literature (Harrer et al. 2022). In “fixed-effect model” it means common: one true effect. In “fixed-effects (plural) model” it means not random. Because the model combines random effects within groups with fixed effects between them, it is also called a mixed-effects model, the same name as the regression models with random intercepts, and for the same reason.

If the subgroup levels are plausibly a random sample, the fixed-effects (plural) model is the wrong one. Studies from Israel, Italy, Mexico and mainland China are a handful of the world’s countries, not all of them. Country is then better modelled as a further random effect, which leads to a multilevel meta-analysis (Harrer et al. 2022).

A worked subgroup analysis: risk of bias

Harrer et al.’s running example is a meta-analysis of 18 trials of third-wave psychotherapies for perceived stress in students (overall g = 0.58, I^2 = 63\%; see pooling). Each trial is rated at high or low risk of bias, and the subgroup question is whether the two kinds of trial find different effects (Harrer et al. 2022).

Third-wave therapy trials by risk of bias, separate \tau^2 per subgroup (REML, Knapp–Hartung) (Harrer et al. 2022).
Subgroup k g 95% CI \hat\tau^2 I^2
High risk of bias 7 0.81 0.28 to 1.34 0.24 77%
Low risk of bias 11 0.43 0.28 to 0.58 0.01 26%
Test for subgroup differences Q = 2.84, 1 df, p = 0.09

The trials at high risk of bias report effects nearly twice as large, a common finding, because biased studies are more likely to overestimate a treatment’s effect (Harrer et al. 2022). They also disagree with each other far more (I^2 77% vs 26%). Yet the test for a difference is not significant (p = 0.09; “a trend”, in Harrer et al.’s words). With a common \tau^2 (0.069 in both groups) the estimates move closer together, 0.77 vs 0.47, and the test is further from significance (Q = 1.79, p = 0.18). A second Q for the remaining heterogeneity within groups (39.31 on 16 df, p = 0.001) says that risk of bias, whatever its role, leaves plenty unexplained. Harrer et al. prefer the separate estimates here, since each group has at least seven studies and there is no reason to think the two groups’ heterogeneity is equal. In this example both versions lead to the same conclusion.

The usual report is a table like the one above: the effect, its CI and p-value and I^2 for each subgroup, and one p-value for the subgroup difference. Read the columns separately. Both subgroup effects are significantly above zero, and the difference between them is not significant. Those are two different claims, and only the second is the subgroup test.

Meta-regression: the same model with a slope

A regression usually predicts a person’s outcome from that person’s characteristics. A meta-regression applies the same logic to whole studies: the outcome is a study’s effect size \hat\theta_k, and the predictor x_k is a feature of the study, such as the year it was run (Harrer et al. 2022). Individual participant data are usually unavailable, so the predictors must be study-level. The meta-analysis may contain thousands of people, but the regression has only as many data points as there are studies. The subgroup rule of thumb (at least about 10 studies) applies here too, though Borenstein et al. warn it is not an iron law.

The model is the random-effects model with a slope added:

\hat\theta_k = \theta + \beta x_k + \epsilon_k + \zeta_k ,

where \epsilon_k is the study’s sampling error and \zeta_k is the part of its true effect that the predictor doesn’t explain, with variance \tau^2 (Harrer et al. 2022). \theta and \beta carry no subscript k: they are the same for every study. They describe a fixed pattern underlying the data, a regression line, and \zeta_k is the random scatter of true effects around it. Fixed coefficients plus a random effect make it a mixed-effects model, the one subgroup analysis uses.

That is no coincidence: a subgroup analysis is a meta-regression with a dummy variable (Harrer et al. 2022). Code D_k = 0 for subgroup A and 1 for subgroup B. The intercept \theta is then the true effect in A, \beta is the difference between the groups, and the one equation is really two: B is A shifted up or down by \beta. Running the risk-of-bias example as a meta-regression gives an intercept of 0.76 (the high-risk group) and a coefficient of −0.29 for low risk, so the low-risk group sits at about 0.47, with p = 0.13 for the difference (Harrer et al. 2022). Those are exactly the common-\tau^2 subgroup results: a meta-regression fits one residual \tau^2 for all studies.

The random-effects model is a meta-regression with no slope. It predicts the same value, \hat\mu, for every study. That gives the natural way to judge a meta-regression (Harrer et al. 2022):

  1. Estimate the residual heterogeneity \tau^2 (by REML or another τ² estimator).
  2. Estimate \theta and \beta by weighted least squares, weighting each study by 1/(s_k^2 + \hat\tau^2) so that precise studies count for more (the same weighting idea as GLS).
  3. Ask how much of the heterogeneity the predictor removed. If the predictor helps, the true effects scatter less around the line than they did around \hat\mu, so \tau^2 shrinks. The share removed is the meta-regression’s R^2_*:

R^2_* = \frac{\hat\tau^2_{\text{REM}} - \hat\tau^2_{\text{MEM}}}{\hat\tau^2_{\text{REM}}} ,

the drop in \tau^2 from the random-effects model (REM) to the mixed-effects model (MEM), as a share. The asterisk is a reminder that this R^2 is about true effects, not observed data points.

Whether \beta is “significant” is a Wald-type test, \hat\beta / \text{SE}_{\hat\beta} against a normal distribution, or better, with the Knapp–Hartung adjustment against a t-distribution, which produces fewer false positives (Harrer et al. 2022).

A worked meta-regression: publication year

Do the third-wave therapy trials’ effects depend on when they were published? (The trials span 1978 to 2020.) Harrer et al.’s meta-regression on year (Harrer et al. 2022):

  • Residual \tau^2 = 0.019 (down from 0.082 without the predictor), so R^2_* = 77\%: publication year accounts for about three-quarters of the heterogeneity. The residual I^2 is 29%, down from 63%.
  • The test of moderators is significant: F(1, 16) = 9.38, p = 0.0075 (Knapp–Hartung).
  • The test for residual heterogeneity, Q_E = 27.83 on 16 df (p = 0.03), says some heterogeneity is still left (with the usual caution about Q-tests).
  • The slope is positive: effects grow with publication year. It is printed rounded as 0.01 per year, with CI 0.005 to 0.031. Recomputed, it is 0.018 per year (our arithmetic), so the fitted effect rises from about g = 0.09 for a 1980 trial to 0.45 in 2000 and 0.82 in 2020.
  • The intercept is −36.15: the predicted effect of a trial run in year 0. Nobody believes that number. Harrer et al. use it as a reminder that a model has to be useful, not a perfect picture of reality. Centring the predictor (year − 2000, say) would make the intercept meaningful.

Compare the risk-of-bias regression: R^2_* = 16\% and p = 0.13. By this measure year is much the stronger predictor of how the trials differ (Harrer et al. 2022). The figure shows both analyses on the reconstructed data.

# High risk of bias: the unique 7-study split that reproduces the printed subgroup Q values
high = np.isin(names, ["Call", "DanitzOrsillo", "Gallego", "Kuhlmann",
                       "Ratanasiripong", "Shapiro", "Song & Lindquist"])
v = ((hi - lo) / (2 * 1.959964))**2
K = len(g)
T975 = {6: 2.446912, 10: 2.228139, 16: 2.119905}       # t quantiles for Knapp-Hartung CIs

def mixed_model(X, y, var):
    """REML mixed-effects meta-regression (Fisher scoring); returns beta, Knapp-Hartung SEs, tau^2."""
    n, p = X.shape
    t2 = 0.05
    for _ in range(1000):
        W = np.diag(1 / (var + t2))
        A = np.linalg.inv(X.T @ W @ X)
        P = W - W @ X @ A @ X.T @ W
        r = y - X @ (A @ X.T @ W @ y)
        new = max(0.0, t2 + (r @ W @ W @ r - np.trace(P)) / np.trace(P @ P))
        if abs(new - t2) < 1e-12:
            break
        t2 = new
    W = np.diag(1 / (var + t2))
    A = np.linalg.inv(X.T @ W @ X)
    b = A @ X.T @ W @ y
    r = y - X @ b
    s2 = (r @ W @ r) / (n - p)                           # Knapp-Hartung scale factor
    return b, np.sqrt(np.diag(A) * s2), t2

def pooled(y, var, t2):
    """Random-effects mean and Knapp-Hartung 95% CI for one subgroup, given tau^2."""
    w = 1 / (var + t2)
    m = np.sum(w * y) / w.sum()
    se = np.sqrt(np.sum(w * (y - m)**2) / ((len(y) - 1) * w.sum()))
    return m, m - T975[len(y) - 1] * se, m + T975[len(y) - 1] * se

ones = np.ones((K, 1))
_, _, t2_total = mixed_model(ones, g, v)                 # 0.082: random-effects model
_, _, t2_common = mixed_model(np.column_stack([ones, ~high]), g, v)   # 0.069
b_year, _, t2_year = mixed_model(np.column_stack([ones, year]), g, v)  # 0.019
mu = np.sum(g / (v + t2_total)) / np.sum(1 / (v + t2_total))

Many predictors: multiple meta-regression

One predictor is rarely the whole story. Harrer et al.’s motivating question: do studies in prestigious journals report bigger effects (Harrer et al. 2022)? Perhaps because selective journals favour striking results. Or perhaps because prestigious journals publish better studies, and better studies find bigger effects. To tell these apart you need to control for study quality while looking at journal reputation, which means several predictors at once:

\hat\theta_k = \theta + \beta_1 x_{1k} + \dots + \beta_n x_{nk} + \epsilon_k + \zeta_k .

It can also include interactions, where one predictor’s slope depends on another. Effects might rise with publication year in high-quality studies while the line for low-quality studies stays flat. That is a product term \beta_3 x_{1k}x_{2k} in the equation, read just as in ordinary multiple regression (Harrer et al. 2022).

Harrer et al. demonstrate on simulated data (36 studies with journal reputation, study quality, publication year and continent) (Harrer et al. 2022):

  • Check collinearity first. The three continuous predictors correlate at 0.30, 0.33 and −0.16: related, but not enough to drop any.
  • Quality alone is weak: slope 0.036, p = 0.069, R^2_* = 7\%.
  • Adding reputation (a hierarchical step: the known predictor first, the new one second): reputation’s slope is 0.034, p < 0.001; quality’s falls to 0.011, p = 0.47; together they explain R^2_* = 67\%. So reputation predicts larger effects even with quality held fixed.
  • Is the bigger model better? A likelihood-ratio test says yes (\chi^2_1 = 19.11, p < 0.001), and so does the small-sample-corrected Akaike criterion (AICc 21.15 vs 37.73; lower is better, and it already penalises the extra parameter). Comparing models this way requires fitting both by ML, not REML.
  • An interaction, publication year × continent, comes out strongly positive (0.63, p < 0.001): effects rise over time, faster in North American studies. This model explains 100% of the heterogeneity, which happens only because the data were simulated. In real data, Harrer et al. say, a result like that should worry you: it suggests overfitting.

Overfitting: the central danger

A model overfits when it fits the data in hand very well and predicts new data badly. It has mistaken noise for signal and produces false positives (Harrer et al. 2022). Any regression that searches for the best-fitting line is exposed to this, and Higgins and Thompson give reasons why meta-regression is more exposed than most:

  • Few data points. The unit of analysis is the study, and there are usually few of them.
  • No test set. A meta-analysis tries to include every relevant study, so there are no spare studies to check the model’s predictions against (compare cross-validation).
  • Heterogeneity invites stories. Take two studies with clearly different effects. Every variable on which they differ is a candidate explanation, and most of those explanations will be spurious.
  • It is easy to keep trying. Add a predictor, drop one, try another, until something is significant. Meta- analysts want an explanation, and this kind of search greatly raises the chance of a spurious one. It is the garden of forking paths applied to moderators.

The defences Harrer et al. list (Harrer et al. 2022): few predictors (parsimony; prefer the model that fits well with fewer terms, using AIC or BIC to judge); predictors chosen in advance, written into the analysis plan (a model fitted after seeing the data is allowed, but must be reported as such); Knapp–Hartung tests when studies are few; and a permutation test of the final model.

Multicollinearity feeds the same problem. When one predictor is nearly a function of another, the coefficients become erratic and swing with small changes in the data, and the explained share is capped (Harrer et al. 2022). In meta-regression it is common. There is no hard rule, but a crude, often effective check is a correlation of 0.8 or more between predictors. Then drop one of the near-duplicates or combine them into one variable (see regression diagnostics).

How predictors get in

Harrer et al. describe four ways to build a multiple meta-regression (Harrer et al. 2022):

Approach How Verdict
Forced entry All predictors in at once The usual default; the predictors must still be chosen on theory
Hierarchical Known predictors first, in order of importance; new ones after, to see what they add Good, when the order comes from a clear rationale
Step-wise (forward / backward) A statistical criterion picks what goes in or out Widely discouraged (Chatfield; Whittingham et al.): a recipe for overfitting. Exploration only
Multi-model inference Fit every combination of predictors and compare them all Exploratory; shows which predictors matter across many good models

Permutation test. Shuffle the effect sizes across studies many times, refit the model each time, and ask how often a shuffled dataset gives a test statistic at least as large as the real one. If 50 of 1,000 do, p = 0.05 (Harrer et al. 2022). No test data are needed, which is why Higgins and Thompson recommend it before reporting any meta-regression. (The logic is the same as the ordinary permutation test.) In the reputation model, reputation’s p goes from below 0.001 to p_* = 0.001, still clearly significant. With very few studies a permutation test cannot reach p < 0.05 at all: it needs K > 4 (Viechtbauer et al.).

Multi-model inference. With four predictors there are 2^4 = 16 possible models. Fitting them all and ranking them by AICc, the best is continent + year + reputation (AICc 6.0, Akaike weight 0.54), with the same plus quality close behind (6.8, weight 0.38) (Harrer et al. 2022). No single model is clearly “the” model, but every one of the top five contains publication year. Averaged over all models, year is the most important predictor (importance 0.999), then continent (0.96) and reputation (0.94), with quality far behind (0.44). This is still exploration. Any model built from it is not an a priori hypothesis and has to be reported that way.

The limits: power, observation and the ecological trap

Subgroup analysis and meta-regression look powerful: a meta-analysis pools many more people than any one study. That impression is wrong for three reasons (Harrer et al. 2022).

Low power. Following Hedges and Pigott: heterogeneity within subgroups widens each subgroup’s CI, and widely overlapping CIs make a difference hard to detect even when it exists. And the differences being looked for are small. Treatment versus control is often a sizeable effect; self-report versus expert-rated studies of the same treatment will usually differ by much less, and detecting it needs much more power. A subgroup power analysis can show the smallest difference the analysis could detect, but it is a diagnostic, not proof of adequate power. Schwarzer et al.’s rule of thumb: subgroup analysis makes sense only with at least 10 studies (Harrer et al. 2022).

So absence of evidence is not evidence of absence. A non-significant subgroup test does not show that the subgroups have the same effect. “We found no difference” is not “the treatments are equivalent”. Harrer et al. call that claim “particularly explosive” when sponsors have an interest in showing two treatments are equally good; subgroup analysis is not usually an adequate way to show equivalence (Harrer et al. 2022).

Observation, not experiment. Even when every study is a randomised trial, the comparison between studies is not randomised (Harrer et al. 2022). The trials of treatment A may have used different control groups from the trials of treatment B, so A can look better when the two are equally effective. The study feature is confounded with everything else that differs between studies. The same is true of a meta-regression slope, since it is the same model: the effects of the therapy trials rise with publication year, but year is not a treatment anyone assigned. Heterogeneous effects within experiments raise the same issue one level down.

The ecological trap. Grouping studies by aggregate information is tempting (Harrer et al. 2022). Schwarzer et al.’s example is age: put studies with a mean age above 65 in an “elderly” subgroup and the rest in “adults”, and find bigger effects in the “elderly” group. It looks as if the treatment works better in older people. It doesn’t follow. A study with mean age 67 may include many people under 65, and one with mean age 58 many over 65. The bigger effects in the “elderly” subgroup could come entirely from its younger participants. It is even possible for studies with older samples to show bigger effects while, for each individual, the effect falls with age. This is ecological bias (Thompson and Higgins; Piantadosi et al.): using relationships between aggregates to infer relationships between individuals. It is the ecological fallacy of area statistics, with studies in place of areas, and a relative of Simpson’s paradox.

Harrer et al.’s rule is blunt: never use aggregate study information in subgroup analyses or meta-regression (Harrer et al. 2022). The exception is when every participant in a study falls in one category, for example trials that enrolled only under-18s against trials that enrolled only adults. That largely removes ecological bias, but confounding by other study differences remains.

Common pitfalls

WarningWhere this goes wrong
  • “No significant subgroup difference, so the subgroups are the same.” Subgroup tests usually have low power. Absence of evidence is not evidence of equivalence. (Harrer et al. 2022)
  • Reading a significant subgroup difference as causal. Studies are not randomised to their characteristics; a moderator is confounded with every other way the studies differ. (Harrer et al. 2022)
  • Grouping by study means or percentages. “Mean age over 65”, “mostly female samples”: ecological bias. Use a characteristic only if it holds for every participant. (Harrer et al. 2022)
  • Subgroups or meta-regression with a handful of studies. Below about 10 studies there is rarely enough information. (Harrer et al. 2022)
  • Comparing two subgroup p-values. “Significant in group A, not in group B” is not a subgroup difference. Only the test between groups addresses that. (Harrer et al. 2022)
  • Searching for moderators until one works. Adding and dropping predictors after seeing the data overfits. Name predictors in the analysis plan; label everything else exploratory. (Harrer et al. 2022)
  • Step-wise selection. Widely discouraged. If it is used at all, use it for exploration only. (Harrer et al. 2022)
  • Taking a very high R^2_* at face value. Explaining nearly all the heterogeneity with a few study features is a warning sign of overfitting, not a triumph. (Harrer et al. 2022)
  • Comparing REML fits with a likelihood-ratio test or AIC. Fit both models by ML when you want to compare them. (Harrer et al. 2022)
  • Treating a random sample of levels as fixed. If the subgroups are a few of many possible levels (countries, school districts), model them as a random effect instead. (Harrer et al. 2022)

Appendix · the mathematics

The mixed-effects meta-regression model. For K studies with effect \hat\theta_k, known sampling variance s_k^2 and predictor row \mathbf x_k^\top (a leading 1, then the predictors),

\hat\theta_k = \mathbf x_k^\top\boldsymbol\beta + \zeta_k + \epsilon_k,\qquad \zeta_k\sim\mathcal N(0,\tau^2),\quad \epsilon_k\sim\mathcal N(0,s_k^2).

Given \tau^2, the coefficients are weighted least squares with \mathbf W = \operatorname{diag}\{1/(s_k^2+\tau^2)\}:

\hat{\boldsymbol\beta} = (\mathbf X^\top\mathbf W\mathbf X)^{-1}\mathbf X^\top\mathbf W\hat{\boldsymbol\theta},\qquad \operatorname{Var}(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}) = (\mathbf X^\top\mathbf W\mathbf X)^{-1}.

In plain words: the line is fitted so that precise studies pull harder on it, and “precise” counts both the study’s own sampling error and the leftover spread of true effects.

With only an intercept, \hat\beta_0 = \sum w_k\hat\theta_k/\sum w_k, the random-effects mean. \tau^2 is estimated first (the figure uses REML by Fisher scoring, with \mathbf P = \mathbf W - \mathbf W\mathbf X(\mathbf X^\top\mathbf W\mathbf X)^{-1}\mathbf X^\top\mathbf W). For the matrix algebra see OLS in matrix form.

Knapp–Hartung. Multiply \operatorname{Var}(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}) by s^2_{\text{KH}} = \frac{1}{K-p}\sum_k w_k(\hat\theta_k - \mathbf x_k^\top\hat{\boldsymbol\beta})^2 and use t_{K-p} (or F_{q,\,K-p} for q coefficients jointly) instead of the normal. With one predictor, F = t^2: 3.06^2 \approx 9.38 in the year example.

Explained heterogeneity and residual Q.

R^2_* = \max\!\left(0,\ \frac{\hat\tau^2_{\text{REM}} - \hat\tau^2_{\text{MEM}}}{\hat\tau^2_{\text{REM}}}\right),\qquad Q_E = \sum_k \frac{(\hat\theta_k - \mathbf x_k^\top\tilde{\boldsymbol\beta})^2}{s_k^2}\ \sim\ \chi^2_{K-p}\ \text{if } \tau^2=0,

where \tilde{\boldsymbol\beta} is the fixed-effect (\tau^2 = 0) fit. Year example: (0.0820 - 0.0188)/0.0820 = 0.771. R^2_* can be negative in a sample (two \tau^2 estimates, each noisy), hence the truncation at zero.

The subgroup test. With pooled subgroup estimates \hat\mu_g and standard errors \text{SE}_g, w_g = 1/\text{SE}_g^2 and \bar\mu = \sum w_g\hat\mu_g / \sum w_g:

Q_{\text{between}} = \sum_{g=1}^{G} w_g(\hat\mu_g - \bar\mu)^2 \ \sim\ \chi^2_{G-1}\ \text{under } H_0:\ \mu_1=\dots=\mu_G .

In plain words: this is Cochran’s Q with each subgroup playing the part of one large study.

Which standard errors? (our arithmetic) In the ThirdWave output the subgroup estimates use Knapp–Hartung, and the printed Q = 2.84 is reproduced with the Knapp–Hartung standard errors (0.216 and 0.069); the plain random-effects ones give 2.94. With a common \tau^2 = 0.0691 the same recipe gives 1.79, as printed. With G = 2, the dummy-variable meta-regression tests the same hypothesis but uses one Knapp–Hartung scale factor for the whole model rather than one per subgroup, so its p (0.13) differs from the subgroup Q-test’s (0.18).

Dummy coding. With D_k\in\{0,1\}, \mathbb E[\hat\theta_k] = \theta + \beta D_k: \theta = \mu_A and \beta = \mu_B - \mu_A. The meta-regression’s single residual \tau^2 is the common-\tau^2 subgroup model: here \hat\tau^2 = 0.0691, \hat\theta = 0.7691, \hat\beta = -0.2992, and 0.7691 - 0.2992 = 0.4698, the common-\tau^2 low-risk estimate.

Recovering the risk-of-bias coding (our arithmetic). Harrer et al. print the labels of only the first seven studies. Of the \binom{11}{4} = 330 ways to choose the remaining four high-risk studies, exactly one reproduces the printed within-subgroup Q values (25.89 and 13.42): Kuhlmann, Ratanasiripong, Shapiro and Song & Lindquist. Ecological bias in one line (our algebra). Let study k’s individual-level effect be \alpha_k + \gamma\,a for a person of age a, and \bar a_k the study’s mean age. The study’s average effect is \alpha_k + \gamma\,\bar a_k. A regression of study effects on \bar a_k estimates \gamma + \operatorname{Cov}(\alpha_k, \bar a_k)/\operatorname{Var}(\bar a_k), so any association between the study intercepts and the mean age (different treatments, settings or designs in older samples) is mixed in with \gamma. The across-study slope can be positive while \gamma < 0.

Permutation p-value. For B random permutations of \hat{\boldsymbol\theta} (with s_k) against the predictor rows, p_* = \#\{b: |T_b| \ge |T_{\text{obs}}|\}/B, often computed with the observed data counted as one of the permutations.

## Why it matters

[Measuring heterogeneity](between-study-heterogeneity.qmd) and hunting for outliers is **post hoc**. It
happens after seeing the data, often *because* of the results, and it looks only at the numbers
[@harrer_doing_meta]. An influence analysis can tell you that three studies don't fit. It cannot tell you why.
Harrer et al.'s example: a meta-analysis finds that a treatment does nothing on average, except in three
studies with a large effect. Perhaps all three used a slightly different version of the treatment, and
that detail is what made it work. That would be an important discovery, and no outlier diagnostic could
make it [@harrer_doing_meta].

**Subgroup analysis** (also called **moderator analysis**) and **meta-regression** are the tools for the
*why*. They test specific hypotheses about which kinds of study produce bigger or smaller effects: one
medication against another, short against long follow-up, one cultural region against another
[@harrer_doing_meta]. They change how heterogeneity is treated. It is no longer just noise around the
average but variation that a scientific hypothesis might explain. At best that explains something about
the world; at least it gives practical guidance for the next decision.

The hypotheses should be fixed **a priori**, in the analysis plan, and coded for every study before the
analysis starts. Effects can differ for endless reasons, so restrict the list to the ones that matter for
the question, which is where subject knowledge pays off [@harrer_doing_meta].

## Subgroup analysis: the fixed-effects (plural) model

A subgroup analysis assumes the studies do not come from one population. They fall into groups, each with its
own true overall effect, and the aim is to reject the null hypothesis that the groups' effects are equal
[@harrer_doing_meta]. It works in two steps, following Borenstein and Higgins.

**Step 1: pool within each subgroup.** This is an ordinary meta-analysis per group, and the usual choice
applies (see [pooling](pooling-effect-sizes.qmd)). A common effect within a subgroup is usually as
unrealistic as it is overall, so each group normally gets its own **random-effects** model and its own
pooled effect $\hat\mu_g$ [@harrer_doing_meta]. Each group then also gets its own $\hat\tau^2_g$. In practice
these are often replaced by a **common $\tau^2$** pooled across groups. That is a practical choice: with five
or fewer studies in a group (Borenstein et al.), a group's own $\tau^2$ is too imprecise to trust, and a shared
estimate is better.

**Step 2: compare the subgroups.** Borenstein et al.'s trick is to **treat each subgroup's pooled effect as
if it were one large study** [@harrer_doing_meta]. With three subgroups you have, in effect, three big
studies, and the question is the familiar heterogeneity question: do they differ by more than their sampling
error allows? So the test is Cochran's $Q$ on the subgroup estimates, compared with a $\chi^2$ distribution
on $G-1$ degrees of freedom. It is an **omnibus** test: significant when at least two subgroups (or some
combination of them) differ, without saying which.

**Why "fixed-effects (plural)".** Within subgroups the studies follow a random-effects model. The subgroups
themselves are treated as **fixed**: they are the real and only categories the variable can take, not a
random draw from a universe of possible subgroups, so there is nothing further to generalise to
[@harrer_doing_meta]. Employment status has two levels, employed and unemployed, and that is all. Other
examples of fixed levels: age group, Western vs non-Western background, the type of control group, self-report
vs expert rating, high/low/unclear study quality, school vs hospital vs home setting. The model therefore has
several true effects (as in random effects) whose levels are fixed (unlike random effects), hence the
"plural".

Borenstein et al. point out that "fixed" means two things in this literature [@harrer_doing_meta]. In
"fixed-effect model" it means **common**: one true effect. In "fixed-effects (plural) model" it means **not
random**. Because the model combines random effects within groups with fixed effects between them, it is also
called a **mixed-effects model**, the same name as the [regression models with random
intercepts](../03-regression/mixed-effects-models.qmd), and for the same reason.

If the subgroup levels *are* plausibly a random sample, the fixed-effects (plural) model is the wrong one.
Studies from Israel, Italy, Mexico and mainland China are a handful of the world's countries, not all of
them. Country is then better modelled as a further random effect, which leads to a [multilevel
meta-analysis](multilevel-meta-analysis.qmd) [@harrer_doing_meta].

### A worked subgroup analysis: risk of bias

Harrer et al.'s running example is a meta-analysis of 18 trials of third-wave psychotherapies for
perceived stress in students (overall $g = 0.58$, $I^2 = 63\%$; see [pooling](pooling-effect-sizes.qmd)).
Each trial is rated at **high** or **low risk of bias**, and the subgroup question is whether the two kinds
of trial find different effects [@harrer_doing_meta].

| Subgroup | $k$ | $g$ | 95% CI | $\hat\tau^2$ | $I^2$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| High risk of bias | 7 | 0.81 | 0.28 to 1.34 | 0.24 | 77% |
| Low risk of bias | 11 | 0.43 | 0.28 to 0.58 | 0.01 | 26% |
| *Test for subgroup differences* | | | | | $Q = 2.84$, 1 df, $p = 0.09$ |

: Third-wave therapy trials by risk of bias, separate $\tau^2$ per subgroup (REML, Knapp–Hartung) [@harrer_doing_meta].

The trials at high risk of bias report effects nearly twice as large, a common finding, because biased
studies are more likely to overestimate a treatment's effect [@harrer_doing_meta]. They also disagree with
each other far more ($I^2$ 77% vs 26%). Yet the test for a difference is not significant ($p = 0.09$; "a
trend", in Harrer et al.'s words). With a **common** $\tau^2$ (0.069 in both groups) the estimates move
closer together, 0.77 vs 0.47, and the test is further from significance ($Q = 1.79$, $p = 0.18$). A second
$Q$ for the remaining heterogeneity *within* groups (39.31 on 16 df, $p = 0.001$) says that risk of bias,
whatever its role, leaves plenty unexplained. Harrer et al. prefer the separate estimates here, since each
group has at least seven studies and there is no reason to think the two groups' heterogeneity is equal. In
this example both versions lead to the same conclusion.

The usual report is a table like the one above: the effect, its CI and $p$-value and $I^2$ for each subgroup,
and one $p$-value for the subgroup difference. Read the columns separately. Both subgroup effects are
significantly above zero, **and** the difference between them is not significant. Those are two different
claims, and only the second is the subgroup test.

## Meta-regression: the same model with a slope

A regression usually predicts a person's outcome from that person's characteristics. A **meta-regression**
applies the same logic to whole studies: the outcome is a study's effect size $\hat\theta_k$, and the
predictor $x_k$ is a feature of the study, such as the year it was run [@harrer_doing_meta]. Individual
participant data are usually unavailable, so the predictors must be **study-level**. The meta-analysis may
contain thousands of people, but the regression has only as many data points as there are studies. The
subgroup rule of thumb (at least about 10 studies) applies here too, though Borenstein et al. warn it is not
an iron law.

The model is the random-effects model with a slope added:

$$\hat\theta_k = \theta + \beta x_k + \epsilon_k + \zeta_k ,$$

where $\epsilon_k$ is the study's sampling error and $\zeta_k$ is the part of its true effect that the predictor
doesn't explain, with variance $\tau^2$ [@harrer_doing_meta]. $\theta$ and $\beta$ carry no subscript $k$:
they are the same for every study. They describe a fixed pattern underlying the data, a **regression line**,
and $\zeta_k$ is the random scatter of true effects around it. Fixed coefficients plus a random effect make
it a mixed-effects model, the one subgroup analysis uses.

That is no coincidence: **a subgroup analysis is a meta-regression with a dummy variable**
[@harrer_doing_meta]. Code $D_k = 0$ for subgroup A and 1 for subgroup B. The intercept $\theta$ is then the
true effect in A, $\beta$ is the *difference* between the groups, and the one equation is really two: B is
A shifted up or down by $\beta$. Running the risk-of-bias example as a meta-regression gives an intercept of
0.76 (the high-risk group) and a coefficient of −0.29 for low risk, so the low-risk group sits at about 0.47,
with $p = 0.13$ for the difference [@harrer_doing_meta]. Those are exactly the **common-$\tau^2$** subgroup
results: a meta-regression fits one residual $\tau^2$ for all studies.

**The random-effects model is a meta-regression with no slope.** It predicts the same value, $\hat\mu$, for
every study. That gives the natural way to judge a meta-regression [@harrer_doing_meta]:

1. Estimate the residual heterogeneity $\tau^2$ (by REML or another [τ² estimator](pooling-effect-sizes.qmd)).
2. Estimate $\theta$ and $\beta$ by **weighted least squares**, weighting each study by
   $1/(s_k^2 + \hat\tau^2)$ so that precise studies count for more (the same weighting idea as
   [GLS](../03-regression/heteroskedasticity-and-gls.qmd)).
3. Ask how much of the heterogeneity the predictor removed. If the predictor helps, the true effects
   scatter less around the line than they did around $\hat\mu$, so $\tau^2$ shrinks. The share removed is the
   meta-regression's $R^2_*$:

$$R^2_* = \frac{\hat\tau^2_{\text{REM}} - \hat\tau^2_{\text{MEM}}}{\hat\tau^2_{\text{REM}}} ,$$

the drop in $\tau^2$ from the random-effects model (REM) to the mixed-effects model (MEM), as a share. The
asterisk is a reminder that this $R^2$ is about **true** effects, not observed data points.

Whether $\beta$ is "significant" is a Wald-type test, $\hat\beta / \text{SE}_{\hat\beta}$ against a normal
distribution, or better, with the **Knapp–Hartung** adjustment against a $t$-distribution, which produces fewer
false positives [@harrer_doing_meta].

### A worked meta-regression: publication year

Do the third-wave therapy trials' effects depend on when they were published? (The trials span 1978 to
2020.) Harrer et al.'s meta-regression on year [@harrer_doing_meta]:

- **Residual $\tau^2 = 0.019$** (down from 0.082 without the predictor), so **$R^2_* = 77\%$**: publication year
  accounts for about three-quarters of the heterogeneity. The residual $I^2$ is 29%, down from 63%.
- The **test of moderators** is significant: $F(1, 16) = 9.38$, $p = 0.0075$ (Knapp–Hartung).
- The **test for residual heterogeneity**, $Q_E = 27.83$ on 16 df ($p = 0.03$), says some heterogeneity is
  still left (with the usual caution about $Q$-tests).
- The **slope** is positive: effects grow with publication year. It is printed rounded as 0.01 per year, with
  CI 0.005 to 0.031. Recomputed, it is **0.018 per year** (our arithmetic), so the fitted effect rises from
  about $g = 0.09$ for a 1980 trial to 0.45 in 2000 and 0.82 in 2020.
- The **intercept** is −36.15: the predicted effect of a trial run in year 0. Nobody believes that number.
  Harrer et al. use it as a reminder that a model has to be useful, not a perfect picture of reality.
  Centring the predictor (year − 2000, say) would make the intercept meaningful.

Compare the risk-of-bias regression: $R^2_* = 16\%$ and $p = 0.13$. By this measure year is much the stronger
predictor of how the trials differ [@harrer_doing_meta]. The figure shows both analyses on the reconstructed
data.

    """REML mixed-effects meta-regression (Fisher scoring); returns beta, Knapp-Hartung SEs, tau^2."""
    n, p = X.shape

    for _ in range(1000):
        W = np.diag(1 / (var + t2))
        A = np.linalg.inv(X.T @ W @ X)
        P = W - W @ X @ A @ X.T @ W
        r = y - X @ (A @ X.T @ W @ y)
        new = max(0.0, t2 + (r @ W @ W @ r - np.trace(P)) / np.trace(P @ P))

## Many predictors: multiple meta-regression

One predictor is rarely the whole story. Harrer et al.'s motivating question: do studies in prestigious
journals report bigger effects [@harrer_doing_meta]? Perhaps because selective journals favour striking
results. Or perhaps because prestigious journals publish better studies, and better studies find bigger
effects. To tell these apart you need to **control for study quality** while looking at journal reputation,
which means several predictors at once:

$$\hat\theta_k = \theta + \beta_1 x_{1k} + \dots + \beta_n x_{nk} + \epsilon_k + \zeta_k .$$

It can also include **interactions**, where one predictor's slope depends on another. Effects might rise with
publication year in high-quality studies while the line for low-quality studies stays flat. That is a
product term $\beta_3 x_{1k}x_{2k}$ in the equation, read just as in [ordinary multiple
regression](../03-regression/multiple-regression.qmd) [@harrer_doing_meta].

Harrer et al. demonstrate on **simulated** data (36 studies with journal reputation, study quality,
publication year and continent) [@harrer_doing_meta]:

- **Check collinearity first.** The three continuous predictors correlate at 0.30, 0.33 and −0.16: related, but
  not enough to drop any.
- **Quality alone** is weak: slope 0.036, $p = 0.069$, $R^2_* = 7\%$.
- **Adding reputation** (a hierarchical step: the known predictor first, the new one second): reputation's
  slope is 0.034, $p < 0.001$; quality's falls to 0.011, $p = 0.47$; together they explain $R^2_* = 67\%$. So
  reputation predicts larger effects even with quality held fixed.
- **Is the bigger model better?** A likelihood-ratio test says yes ($\chi^2_1 = 19.11$, $p < 0.001$), and so
  does the small-sample-corrected Akaike criterion (AICc 21.15 vs 37.73; lower is better, and it already
  penalises the extra parameter). Comparing models this way requires fitting both by **ML**, not REML.
- **An interaction**, publication year × continent, comes out strongly positive (0.63, $p < 0.001$): effects rise
  over time, faster in North American studies. This model explains **100%** of the heterogeneity, which
  happens only because the data were simulated. In real data, Harrer et al. say, a result like that should
  worry you: it suggests overfitting.

### Overfitting: the central danger

A model **overfits** when it fits the data in hand very well and predicts new data badly. It has mistaken
noise for signal and produces false positives [@harrer_doing_meta]. Any regression that searches for the
best-fitting line is exposed to this, and Higgins and Thompson give reasons why meta-regression is more
exposed than most:

- **Few data points.** The unit of analysis is the study, and there are usually few of them.
- **No test set.** A meta-analysis tries to include every relevant study, so there are no spare studies to
  check the model's predictions against (compare [cross-validation](../04-predictive/cross-validation.qmd)).
- **Heterogeneity invites stories.** Take two studies with clearly different effects. Every variable on which
  they differ is a candidate explanation, and most of those explanations will be spurious.
- **It is easy to keep trying.** Add a predictor, drop one, try another, until something is significant. Meta-
  analysts want an explanation, and this kind of search greatly raises the chance of a spurious one. It is
  the [garden of forking paths](questionable-research-practices.qmd) applied to moderators.

The defences Harrer et al. list [@harrer_doing_meta]: **few predictors** (parsimony; prefer the model that fits
well with fewer terms, using AIC or BIC to judge); **predictors chosen in advance**, written into the
analysis plan (a model fitted after seeing the data is allowed, but must be reported as such);
**Knapp–Hartung** tests when studies are few; and a **permutation test** of the final model.

**Multicollinearity** feeds the same problem. When one predictor is nearly a function of another, the
coefficients become erratic and swing with small changes in the data, and the explained share is capped
[@harrer_doing_meta]. In meta-regression it is common. There is no hard rule, but a crude, often effective
check is a correlation of 0.8 or more between predictors. Then drop one of the near-duplicates or combine
them into one variable (see [regression diagnostics](../03-regression/regression-diagnostics.qmd)).

### How predictors get in

Harrer et al. describe four ways to build a multiple meta-regression [@harrer_doing_meta]:

| Approach | How | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| **Forced entry** | All predictors in at once | The usual default; the predictors must still be chosen on theory |
| **Hierarchical** | Known predictors first, in order of importance; new ones after, to see what they add | Good, when the order comes from a clear rationale |
| **Step-wise** (forward / backward) | A statistical criterion picks what goes in or out | Widely discouraged (Chatfield; Whittingham et al.): a recipe for overfitting. Exploration only |
| **Multi-model inference** | Fit every combination of predictors and compare them all | Exploratory; shows which predictors matter across many good models |

**Permutation test.** Shuffle the effect sizes across studies many times, refit the model each time, and ask
how often a shuffled dataset gives a test statistic at least as large as the real one. If 50 of 1,000 do,
$p = 0.05$ [@harrer_doing_meta]. No test data are needed, which is why Higgins and Thompson recommend it before
reporting any meta-regression. (The logic is the same as the [ordinary permutation
test](../02-inference/resampling-and-nonparametric-tests.qmd).) In the reputation model, reputation's $p$ goes
from below 0.001 to $p_* = 0.001$, still clearly significant. With very few studies a permutation test cannot
reach $p < 0.05$ at all: it needs $K > 4$ (Viechtbauer et al.).

**Multi-model inference.** With four predictors there are $2^4 = 16$ possible models. Fitting them all and
ranking them by AICc, the best is continent + year + reputation (AICc 6.0, Akaike weight 0.54), with the same
plus quality close behind (6.8, weight 0.38) [@harrer_doing_meta]. No single model is clearly "the" model, but
every one of the top five contains publication year. Averaged over all models, year is the most important
predictor (importance 0.999), then continent (0.96) and reputation (0.94), with quality far behind (0.44). This
is still exploration. Any model built from it is not an a priori hypothesis and has to be reported that way.

## The limits: power, observation and the ecological trap

Subgroup analysis and meta-regression look powerful: a meta-analysis pools many more people than any one
study. That impression is wrong for three reasons [@harrer_doing_meta].

**Low power.** Following Hedges and Pigott: heterogeneity *within* subgroups widens each subgroup's CI, and
widely overlapping CIs make a difference hard to detect even when it exists. And the differences being looked
for are small. Treatment versus control is often a sizeable effect; self-report versus expert-rated studies of
the same treatment will usually differ by much less, and detecting it needs much more power. A **subgroup power
analysis** can show the smallest difference the analysis could detect, but it is a diagnostic, not proof of
adequate power. Schwarzer et al.'s rule of thumb: subgroup analysis makes sense only with **at least 10
studies** [@harrer_doing_meta].

So **absence of evidence is not evidence of absence**. A non-significant subgroup test does not show that the
subgroups have the same effect. "We found no difference" is not "the treatments are equivalent". Harrer et al.
call that claim "particularly explosive" when sponsors have an interest in showing two treatments are
equally good; subgroup analysis is not usually an adequate way to show equivalence [@harrer_doing_meta].

**Observation, not experiment.** Even when every study is a randomised trial, the comparison *between*
studies is not randomised [@harrer_doing_meta]. The trials of treatment A may have used different control
groups from the trials of treatment B, so A can look better when the two are equally effective. The study
feature is confounded with everything else that differs between studies. The same is true of a
meta-regression slope, since it is the same model: the effects of the therapy trials rise with publication
year, but year is not a treatment anyone assigned. [Heterogeneous effects within
experiments](../05-causal-inference/heterogeneous-effects-in-experiments.qmd) raise the same issue one level
down.

**The ecological trap.** Grouping studies by **aggregate** information is tempting [@harrer_doing_meta].
Schwarzer et al.'s example is age: put studies with a mean age above 65 in an "elderly" subgroup and the rest
in "adults", and find bigger effects in the "elderly" group. It looks as if the treatment works better in
older people. It doesn't follow. A study with mean age 67 may include many people under 65, and one with mean
age 58 many over 65. The bigger effects in the "elderly" subgroup could come entirely from its younger
participants. It is even possible for studies with older samples to show bigger effects while, for each
individual, the effect *falls* with age. This is **ecological bias** (Thompson and Higgins; Piantadosi et al.):
using relationships between aggregates to infer relationships between individuals. It is the [ecological
fallacy](../00-overture/using-existing-data.qmd) of area statistics, with studies in place of areas, and a
relative of [Simpson's paradox](../01-foundations/famous-paradoxes.qmd).

Harrer et al.'s rule is blunt: **never use aggregate study information** in subgroup analyses or
meta-regression [@harrer_doing_meta]. The exception is when *every* participant in a study falls in one
category, for example trials that enrolled only under-18s against trials that enrolled only adults. That largely
removes ecological bias, but confounding by other study differences remains.

## Common pitfalls

::: {.callout-warning title="Where this goes wrong"}
- **"No significant subgroup difference, so the subgroups are the same."** Subgroup tests usually have low
  power. Absence of evidence is not evidence of equivalence. [@harrer_doing_meta]
- **Reading a significant subgroup difference as causal.** Studies are not randomised to their
  characteristics; a moderator is confounded with every other way the studies differ. [@harrer_doing_meta]
- **Grouping by study means or percentages.** "Mean age over 65", "mostly female samples": ecological bias.
  Use a characteristic only if it holds for every participant. [@harrer_doing_meta]
- **Subgroups or meta-regression with a handful of studies.** Below about 10 studies there is rarely enough
  information. [@harrer_doing_meta]
- **Comparing two subgroup $p$-values.** "Significant in group A, not in group B" is not a subgroup difference.
  Only the test *between* groups addresses that. [@harrer_doing_meta]
- **Searching for moderators until one works.** Adding and dropping predictors after seeing the data
  overfits. Name predictors in the analysis plan; label everything else exploratory. [@harrer_doing_meta]
- **Step-wise selection.** Widely discouraged. If it is used at all, use it for exploration only. [@harrer_doing_meta]
- **Taking a very high $R^2_*$ at face value.** Explaining nearly all the heterogeneity with a few study features
  is a warning sign of overfitting, not a triumph. [@harrer_doing_meta]
- **Comparing REML fits with a likelihood-ratio test or AIC.** Fit both models by ML when you want to compare
  them. [@harrer_doing_meta]
- **Treating a random sample of levels as fixed.** If the subgroups are a few of many possible levels
  (countries, school districts), model them as a random effect instead. [@harrer_doing_meta]
:::

## Appendix · the mathematics {.unnumbered}

::: {.math-appendix}

**The mixed-effects meta-regression model.** For $K$ studies with effect $\hat\theta_k$, known sampling
variance $s_k^2$ and predictor row $\mathbf x_k^\top$ (a leading 1, then the predictors),

$$\hat\theta_k = \mathbf x_k^\top\boldsymbol\beta + \zeta_k + \epsilon_k,\qquad \zeta_k\sim\mathcal N(0,\tau^2),\quad \epsilon_k\sim\mathcal N(0,s_k^2).$$

Given $\tau^2$, the coefficients are weighted least squares with $\mathbf W = \operatorname{diag}\{1/(s_k^2+\tau^2)\}$:

$$\hat{\boldsymbol\beta} = (\mathbf X^\top\mathbf W\mathbf X)^{-1}\mathbf X^\top\mathbf W\hat{\boldsymbol\theta},\qquad
\operatorname{Var}(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}) = (\mathbf X^\top\mathbf W\mathbf X)^{-1}.$$

> **In plain words:** the line is fitted so that precise studies pull harder on it, and "precise" counts
> both the study's own sampling error and the leftover spread of true effects.

With only an intercept, $\hat\beta_0 = \sum w_k\hat\theta_k/\sum w_k$, the random-effects mean. $\tau^2$ is
estimated first (the figure uses REML by Fisher scoring, with $\mathbf P = \mathbf W - \mathbf W\mathbf X(\mathbf X^\top\mathbf W\mathbf X)^{-1}\mathbf X^\top\mathbf W$).
For the matrix algebra see [OLS in matrix form](../../appendices/math/ols-in-matrix-form.qmd).

**Knapp–Hartung.** Multiply $\operatorname{Var}(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta})$ by
$s^2_{\text{KH}} = \frac{1}{K-p}\sum_k w_k(\hat\theta_k - \mathbf x_k^\top\hat{\boldsymbol\beta})^2$ and use
$t_{K-p}$ (or $F_{q,\,K-p}$ for $q$ coefficients jointly) instead of the normal. With one predictor,
$F = t^2$: $3.06^2 \approx 9.38$ in the year example.

**Explained heterogeneity and residual $Q$.**

$$R^2_* = \max\!\left(0,\ \frac{\hat\tau^2_{\text{REM}} - \hat\tau^2_{\text{MEM}}}{\hat\tau^2_{\text{REM}}}\right),\qquad
Q_E = \sum_k \frac{(\hat\theta_k - \mathbf x_k^\top\tilde{\boldsymbol\beta})^2}{s_k^2}\ \sim\ \chi^2_{K-p}\ \text{if } \tau^2=0,$$

where $\tilde{\boldsymbol\beta}$ is the fixed-effect ($\tau^2 = 0$) fit. Year example: $(0.0820 - 0.0188)/0.0820 = 0.771$.
$R^2_*$ can be negative in a sample (two $\tau^2$ estimates, each noisy), hence the truncation at zero.

**The subgroup test.** With pooled subgroup estimates $\hat\mu_g$ and standard errors $\text{SE}_g$,
$w_g = 1/\text{SE}_g^2$ and $\bar\mu = \sum w_g\hat\mu_g / \sum w_g$:

$$Q_{\text{between}} = \sum_{g=1}^{G} w_g(\hat\mu_g - \bar\mu)^2 \ \sim\ \chi^2_{G-1}\ \text{under } H_0:\ \mu_1=\dots=\mu_G .$$

> **In plain words:** this is Cochran's $Q$ with each subgroup playing the part of one large study.

*Which standard errors?* (our arithmetic) In the ThirdWave output the subgroup estimates use Knapp–Hartung, and
the printed $Q = 2.84$ is reproduced with the Knapp–Hartung standard errors (0.216 and 0.069); the plain
random-effects ones give 2.94. With a common $\tau^2 = 0.0691$ the same recipe gives 1.79, as printed. With
$G = 2$, the dummy-variable meta-regression tests the same hypothesis but uses one Knapp–Hartung scale
factor for the whole model rather than one per subgroup, so its $p$ (0.13) differs from the subgroup
$Q$-test's (0.18).

**Dummy coding.** With $D_k\in\{0,1\}$, $\mathbb E[\hat\theta_k] = \theta + \beta D_k$: $\theta = \mu_A$ and
$\beta = \mu_B - \mu_A$. The meta-regression's single residual $\tau^2$ is the common-$\tau^2$ subgroup model:
here $\hat\tau^2 = 0.0691$, $\hat\theta = 0.7691$, $\hat\beta = -0.2992$, and $0.7691 - 0.2992 = 0.4698$, the
common-$\tau^2$ low-risk estimate.

**Recovering the risk-of-bias coding** (our arithmetic). Harrer et al. print the labels of only the first
seven studies. Of the $\binom{11}{4} = 330$ ways to choose the remaining four high-risk studies, exactly one
reproduces the printed within-subgroup $Q$ values (25.89 and 13.42): Kuhlmann, Ratanasiripong, Shapiro and Song
& Lindquist. That coding then reproduces every printed subgroup and meta-regression result to the printed
precision (0.8126, 0.4300, $\tau^2$ 0.2423 and 0.0099, CIs, 0.7691/0.4698, $R^2_*$ 15.66%, $F$ 2.51, $Q_E$ 39.31).

**Ecological bias in one line** (our algebra). Let study $k$'s individual-level effect be $\alpha_k + \gamma\,a$ for a
person of age $a$, and $\bar a_k$ the study's mean age. The study's average effect is $\alpha_k + \gamma\,\bar a_k$. A
regression of study effects on $\bar a_k$ estimates $\gamma + \operatorname{Cov}(\alpha_k, \bar a_k)/\operatorname{Var}(\bar a_k)$, so
any association between the study intercepts and the mean age (different treatments, settings or designs in
older samples) is mixed in with $\gamma$. The across-study slope can be positive while $\gamma < 0$.

**Permutation $p$-value.** For $B$ random permutations of $\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}$ (with $s_k$) against the
predictor rows, $p_* = \#\{b: |T_b| \ge |T_{\text{obs}}|\}/B$, often computed with the observed data counted as one
of the permutations.
