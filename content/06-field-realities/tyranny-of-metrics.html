The Tyranny of Metrics

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source

The moment you turn a measure into a target, people optimise the measure — and the thing it was meant to track quietly slips away.

Everything before this taught you to measure well. This entry is the warning that measuring is never neutral. The instant a number carries stakes — pay, promotion, ranking, funding — the people being measured become self-conscious actors who optimise the number, not the goal beneath it. Test scores rise while learning stagnates; survival rates climb because surgeons turn away the sick. The metric improves; the world it represented does not. Knowing when not to trust a number is the final skill of the quantitative researcher.

Why it matters

The whole manual has been a machine for producing trustworthy numbers. Muller’s The Tyranny of Metrics supplies the conscience: a number that drives behaviour changes the behaviour it measures, often perversely. (Muller 2018) This is not a niche caution — it is the central risk of applied quantitative work, where your estimate doesn’t sit in a journal but becomes a target someone is paid to hit. The researcher who can build a metric and foresee how it will be gamed is the one who can be trusted with a real decision.

Metric fixation

Muller names the disease metric fixation: “the seemingly irresistible pressure to measure performance, to publicize it, and to reward it.” (Muller 2018) It rests on three beliefs: (Muller 2018)

  1. Replace judgment with numbers — that standardised, comparable metrics can and should substitute for the judgment of experienced practitioners.
  2. Accountability = transparency — that making the numbers public guarantees institutions serve their purpose.
  3. Motivate by extrinsic reward — that people work best when pay and penalties are tied to measured performance (pay-for-performance, league tables).

Each belief is plausible; together they license replacing professional judgment with a dashboard. The rest of the entry is what goes wrong when you do.

Goodhart’s law and Campbell’s law

Two “laws” name the mechanism. Muller notes they were formulated in the same year, 1975, on opposite sides of the Atlantic, apparently by independent discovery: (Muller 2018)

  • Campbell’s law (the American social psychologist Donald T. Campbell) — “the more any quantitative social indicator is used for social decision-making, the more subject it will be to corruption pressures and the more apt it will be to distort and corrupt the social processes it is intended to monitor.”
  • Goodhart’s law (the British economist C.A.E. Goodhart), in the form Muller quotes — “any measure used for control is unreliable”; or, as Muller glosses it, anything that can be measured and rewarded will be gamed. (The slogan you will hear more often — “when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure” — is a later popular paraphrase of the same idea, not Muller’s wording.)

The engine under both: people are self-conscious actors. Attach stakes to a measurement and they respond by skewing the data to maximise the score, whether or not the underlying reality improves. (Muller 2018) A measure passively observed and the same measure optimised against are different objects — the act of targeting corrupts the signal. The figure shows the signature: a proxy that faithfully tracks the goal until the day it becomes a target, then peels away.

Show the figure code 
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
t = np.arange(0, 24)
target_start = 12
# Before targeting, proxy and true goal move together; after, proxy is gamed up, goal drifts down.
true_goal = 50 + 1.2 * t + rng.normal(0, 1.0, t.size)
true_goal[target_start:] = true_goal[target_start-1] - 0.8 * (t[target_start:] - t[target_start-1]) \
                           + rng.normal(0, 1.0, t.size - target_start)
proxy = true_goal.copy()
proxy[target_start:] = true_goal[target_start-1] + 3.2 * (t[target_start:] - t[target_start-1]) \
                       + rng.normal(0, 1.0, t.size - target_start)

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7.2, 4.2))
ax.plot(t, proxy, "o-", color="#e8531d", lw=2.2, ms=3, label="measured proxy (the metric)")
ax.plot(t, true_goal, "o-", color="#5e5e54", lw=2.2, ms=3, label="true goal (what we care about)")
ax.axvline(target_start - 0.5, color="#b7b6a8", ls="--", lw=1.4)
ax.text(target_start - 0.3, ax.get_ylim()[1]*0.96, "metric becomes a target", fontsize=8, color="#5e5e54")
ax.fill_between(t[target_start:], true_goal[target_start:], proxy[target_start:],
                color="#e8531d", alpha=0.10)
ax.text(18, 70, "the gap =\nGoodhart corruption", fontsize=8.5, color="#e8531d", ha="center")
ax.set_xlabel("time"); ax.set_ylabel("performance")
ax.set_title("A measure tracks the goal — until it becomes a target")
ax.legend(fontsize=8, frameon=False, loc="upper left")
fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()

How metrics get gamed

When judgement is reduced to a narrow score, practitioners “juke the stats.” Muller catalogues the recurring tactics: (Muller 2018)

  • Teaching to the test. Effort shifts from the institution’s true purpose to whatever lifts the metric — narrowing a curriculum to the tested subjects and test-taking strategy.
  • Creaming / cherry-picking. Avoid the hard cases that might dent your success rate. Cardiac surgeons facing public scorecards refer the sickest patients elsewhere — improving their mortality numbers by not treating the people who most need treatment.
  • Lowering standards. Hit the target by making it easier — inflating graduation rates by making courses easier to pass.
  • Outright gaming and fraud. Misclassify or falsify: police downgrading felonies to misdemeanours (or thefts to “lost property”) so they don’t count against headline crime.
  • Short-termism. Sacrifice long-term investment (R&D, training) for an immediate numerical bump.
  • Goal displacement. The deepest pathology — the metric means replace the organisational ends they were meant to serve. Hitting the number becomes the mission.
NoteMuller’s case files
  • Education (No Child Left Behind). High-stakes testing narrowed curricula to math and English and, in several cities, produced systematic cheating — teachers altering answer sheets. (Muller 2018)
  • Healthcare (surgical scorecards). Public mortality rankings drove case-selection bias: surgeons declined high-risk patients to protect their stats. (Muller 2018)
  • Policing (Compstat / FBI reports). Pressure to “reduce crime” led to reclassifying offences downward and chasing easy street arrests over hard long-term cases. (Muller 2018)
  • The military (Vietnam body counts). McNamara’s reliance on body counts as a success metric produced fabricated numbers — and soldiers risking their lives to find corpses to hit the target. (Muller 2018)
  • Universities (rankings). League tables push schools toward glossy brochures aimed at the reputation survey, law schools toward massaging their admissions data, and all of them toward homogenisation — abandoning a distinctive mission to resemble the competitors the ranking rewards. (Muller 2018)

Medicine up close: a metric read with a statistician’s eye

Muller’s longest case is medicine, and it is worth slowing down on, because almost every failure in it is a failure this encyclopedia has already taught you to spot. He begins by separating three jobs that advocates run together: metrics as information for the clinician, metrics as public report cards for consumers, and metrics as the basis of pay-for-performance. (Muller 2018) The first mostly works; the last two are where things go wrong.

Report cards and the missing control group. New York pioneered public report cards for coronary bypass surgery, scoring surgeons and hospitals on 30-day mortality. Mortality duly fell. But only patients who were operated on enter the statistic, and some high-risk patients were declined, or sent out of state, where they no longer counted. (Muller 2018) That is sample selection produced by incentive: the denominator was managed, not the outcome. And the headline improvement fails the most basic causal check. The same improvement happened in neighbouring Massachusetts, which published no report cards. (Muller 2018) A before-and-after comparison credited the policy; the comparison state, the core idea of difference-in-differences, says the trend was there anyway.

Readmissions and the relabelled outcome. From 2009 Medicare publicly reported 30-day readmission rates for heart attack, heart failure, pneumonia and other conditions, and from 2012 it fined hospitals with above-average rates. Readmissions fell. But hospitals could also place a returning patient on “observation status”, billed as an outpatient and therefore not a readmission, or treat them in the emergency room. Medicare observation stays rose 96 percent between 2006 and 2013, and about half the fall in readmissions came from patients who had in fact come back. (Muller 2018) Muller is fair about it: some hospitals genuinely improved, and a later analysis found the hospitals that cut readmissions were not the same ones that increased observation stays. The honest verdict is mixed, and you can only reach it by asking how the outcome is recorded, not just whether it moved.

Risk adjustment is also a metric. By 2015 about three-quarters of reporting hospitals were being penalised, disproportionately major teaching hospitals, which see the hardest cases, and hospitals in poor areas. Readmission depends on patients’ health, support and behaviour after discharge, which the hospital barely controls. The fix is “risk adjustment”, but Muller’s point is that the risk model is “at least as prone to mismeasurement and manipulation” as the metric it corrects. (Muller 2018) Adjust badly and you penalise the institutions serving the sickest populations. This is the policy face of confounding: the comparison is between different patient populations, and no adjustment is better than the model behind it.

When the evidence is graded. Muller leans on the pro-metrics literature itself. A RAND review of pay-for-performance found that most studies measured process or intermediate outcomes, not whether patients recovered, and that “studies with stronger methodological designs were less likely to identify significant improvements.” (Muller 2018) Weaker designs finding bigger effects is the same signature you met in the replication crisis. He also flags the reverse-causation trap. Crediting the Cleveland Clinic’s excellence to its published outcome books is post hoc ergo propter hoc: it was excellent first, and a clinic with good numbers has reason to publish them. (Muller 2018)

And one that worked. The Michigan Keystone ICU project, led by Peter Pronovost, attacked central-line bloodstream infections, estimated at some 82,000 a year in the US in 2001 and costing $12,000 to $56,000 each, with almost 32,000 deaths. A short checklist, with monthly infection data fed back to ICU leaders and discussed with staff to learn from mistakes, cut infections by 66 percent. (Muller 2018) Muller’s diagnosis is that these were diagnostic metrics. What was measured matched what clinicians already cared about, the data went to the people doing the work, and the outcome was almost entirely under the hospital’s control. Everything the report cards got wrong, Keystone got right.

Surrogate outcomes: measuring the easy thing

A quieter failure: metric fixation pushes you to measure what is easy to count, not what matters. Organisations track inputs (money spent) or processes (workshops held) because the real outcomes are complex or delayed. (Muller 2018) Muller’s barb is worth memorising: “the ease of measuring may be inversely proportional to the significance of what is measured.” (Muller 2018) This is the field cousin of the surrogate-outcome and proxy-variable problems — only here the substitution is driven by managerial convenience, not statistical necessity.

The costs

Metric fixation is not free, even when it isn’t gamed: (Muller 2018)

  • A data arms race. Effort migrates from frontline work to documenting it — more managers and analysts, producers spending their hours on reports instead of the job.
  • Discouraged innovation. Real innovation needs experiments that might fail; metrics punish failure, so people retreat to safe, established, measurable goals.
  • Demoralisation. Professionals resent the loss of autonomy and a scoreboard that conflicts with their vocational ethos — driving the most creative people out.

Muller’s closing catalogue of “unintended but predictable” consequences adds several that a statistician should recognise on sight: (Muller 2018)

  • Diminishing utility. A new metric’s first harvest is real — it exposes the badly performing outliers. But the metric keeps being collected from everyone, long after the low-hanging fruit is gone, until the marginal cost of gathering it exceeds the marginal benefit.
  • Rule cascades. Each discovered form of gaming prompts a new rule to block it; complying with the accumulating rules slows the institution further.
  • Rewarding luck. Measure an outcome the person barely controls and you reward and punish chance. This is a statistical error before it is a moral one: small units are noisy, so they crowd both ends of any league table (the “little extremes” problem), and next year’s ranking regresses toward the mean.
  • Discouraging risk-taking. The intelligence analysts who eventually located Bin Laden worked the problem for years; measured at any point, their “failure rate” was 100 percent — until it wasn’t.
  • Discouraging cooperation. Tie a manager’s bonus to their own unit’s budget and ask whether they would move resources to another department for the good of the whole. A hospital CEO’s honest answer, as the medical reformer Donald Berwick recounts it: only “if he were crazy.” Metrics reward competition, not help.

Thick and thin: the ethos a metric can’t carry

Muller’s “demoralisation” has a sharper diagnosis in Vincent & Hooper’s account of research integrity, which splits any professional value system into two schemas (Vincent and Hooper 2025):

  • a thick ethos — the immersed, ethically-reflective judgement of a practitioner who has internalised why the work matters;
  • thin values — the rules, prohibitions, metrics, and incentives we need to communicate, assess, and enforce that ethos across a large, impersonal system.

Thin values are unavoidable at scale — you cannot run a hospital or a research sector on unspoken virtue alone. The danger is that, as a value is “thinned” for mass transmission, three pathologies appear (Vincent and Hooper 2025):

  • Mistaking the map for the territory — people obey the codified rule but never absorb the ethos it was a shorthand for; the metric becomes the whole of the value rather than a pointer to it.
  • Proxy failure — because thin values must be simple to monitor, they become proxies, and targeting them reproduces exactly Goodhart’s and Campbell’s laws above (Vincent and Hooper 2025). This is the same corruption, named from the incentive side.
  • Crowding-out — the behavioural-economics mechanism behind Muller’s demoralisation: extrinsic metrics and rewards can displace the intrinsic motivation (the thick ethos) they were meant to reinforce, so the scoreboard actively erodes the vocation it measures (Vincent and Hooper 2025).

Their prescription is not to abolish thin values but to hold an “essential tension”: keep the metrics continually grounded in — and answerable to — the thicker ethos they serve, through ongoing reflection on whether a given number still promotes the goal rather than supplanting it (Vincent and Hooper 2025). That is the same conclusion Muller reaches from the management side, arrived at from ethics.

The balanced verdict: when metrics work

Muller is not anti-measurement — he is anti-fixation. (Muller 2018) His own success stories share a pattern. Compstat worked as a tool for deciding where to deploy police, and failed when threats of demotion were used to push reported crime down. Standardised tests helped when teachers used them to adjust their teaching, and failed when they became the main basis for reward and punishment. Keystone worked because its measures matched professional values. (Muller 2018) Metrics are a valuable complement to judgment, never a replacement for it. They work best when they are: (Muller 2018)

  • Internal and diagnostic — used by practitioners themselves to spot outliers and refine practice, not as a public cudgel.
  • Low-stakes — not the sole basis of pay or promotion, so the incentive to game is weak. (Researchers trust the NAEP school tests precisely because nobody’s fortunes ride on them.)
  • Bottom-up — designed with the people measured, who then trust the metric’s validity.

The core claim underneath: measurement is not an alternative to judgment, because measurement demands judgment, about whether to measure, what to measure, what the result signifies, whether to attach rewards, and to whom to release it. (Muller 2018)

Muller’s checklist

Muller closes with ten questions to ask before building or trusting a performance metric. Compressed, they read like a design review: (Muller 2018)

# Ask What the answer tells you
1 What kind of thing is being measured? The closer it is to inanimate matter, the more measurable. People react to being measured, most of all when rewards are attached, and react helpfully only to the extent they share the goal.
2 How useful is the information? Measurable is not the same as worth measuring. Is it a real proxy for what you want to know? If not, don’t measure it (validity).
3 How useful are more metrics? Metrics are best at finding outliers, weak at ranking the middle and top; the marginal metric eventually costs more than it tells you.
4 What does not measuring cost? Other information may exist: client, patient or parent judgement (how often parents ask for a particular teacher), or the verdict of a charity’s own beneficiaries.
5 For what purpose, and transparent to whom? The key split: internal monitoring by practitioners vs external reward and punishment. Crime data to position squad cars is not crime data to decide a commander’s promotion. Low-stakes use is often the more effective.
6 What does collecting it cost? Mostly opportunity cost: every hour spent documenting is an hour taken from the work itself.
7 Why do the people at the top want it? Often because leaders parachuted in lack local knowledge, which argues for hiring from within.
8 Who develops the measures? Build them bottom-up with the teachers, nurses and “cop on the beat” who hold the tacit knowledge. A metric works to the extent the measured believe in it.
9 How will even a good measure be corrupted? Pay doctors per procedure and they over-treat; per patient seen and they rush; on outcomes and they cream. Every scheme has a failure mode — choosing among them is itself a judgement.
10 What are the limits of the possible? Not every problem is soluble, and fewer by metrics. Transparency can make a problem more visible without making it more solvable (limits of quantification).

Two of these deserve a researcher’s underline. Question 5 is the one that separates Keystone from the report cards: the same number can be diagnostic or corrosive depending on who holds it and what rides on it. And question 9 is Goodhart as a design constraint. You don’t find a measure that can’t be gamed; you choose the gaming you can best live with and watch for it.

The mature stance the whole book has built toward: a number is an input to judgment, not a substitute for it. The skill is holding both — rigorous measurement and the wisdom to know its limits.

Common pitfalls

WarningWhere this goes wrong

  • Attaching high stakes to a single number. Goodhart guarantees it will be gamed; the tighter the incentive, the faster the corruption. (Muller 2018)

  • Measuring the proxy and forgetting the goal. Optimise a surrogate and you can improve it while the real outcome decays — verify the proxy still tracks the thing you care about. (Muller 2018)

  • Counting inputs/processes as if they were outcomes. Workshops held is not learning gained; spend follows ease of measurement, not importance. (Muller 2018)

  • Ignoring gaming when designing a metric. If you can’t say how people will game it, you haven’t finished designing it. (Muller 2018)

  • Replacing professional judgment wholesale. Metrics inform judgment; they don’t substitute for the experience that knows which cases the number misreads. (Muller 2018)

  • Crediting the metric for a trend it didn’t cause. New York’s bypass mortality fell after report cards, and so did Massachusetts’s without them. A before-and-after comparison with no control group is not evidence that publishing numbers works. (Muller 2018)

  • Trusting a metric without asking how the outcome is recorded. Readmissions fell partly because returning patients were relabelled as “observation” stays. When a number is rewarded, audit the definition as well as the level. (Muller 2018)

Appendix · the mechanism

Goodhart formalised. Let the true goal be G and a proxy M=G+\eta with measurement slack \eta an agent can influence at cost. Passively, \eta\approx0 and \operatorname{Corr}(M,G)\approx1 — the proxy is informative. Introduce a reward R(M) for high M. A rational agent now invests effort to raise \eta (gaming) up to where marginal reward equals marginal cost, independently of G. The observed M rises, but \operatorname{Corr}(M,G) collapses — the very act of using M for control destroys its correlation with G. The proxy was a good measure because no one was optimising it; the optimisation is what breaks it.

Why surrogates drift. A surrogate S validated to satisfy \mathbb E[G\mid S]=f(S) under observational conditions need not preserve that relation under intervention on S — exactly the \operatorname{do} vs conditioning distinction. Optimising S intervenes on it, and the conditional relation that licensed S as a stand-in can fail — the managerial twin of reward hacking.

::: {.lead-kernel}
Everything before this taught you to *measure* well. This entry is the warning that measuring is never
neutral. The instant a number carries stakes — pay, promotion, ranking, funding — the people being
measured become self-conscious actors who optimise **the number**, not the goal beneath it. Test scores
rise while learning stagnates; survival rates climb because surgeons turn away the sick. The metric
improves; the world it represented does not. Knowing *when not to trust a number* is the final skill of
the quantitative researcher.
:::

## Why it matters

The whole manual has been a machine for producing trustworthy numbers. Muller's *The Tyranny of
Metrics* supplies the conscience: a number that drives behaviour **changes the behaviour it
measures**, often perversely. [@muller_tyranny] This is not a niche caution — it is the central risk
of *applied* quantitative work, where your estimate doesn't sit in a journal but becomes a target
someone is paid to hit. The researcher who can build a metric *and* foresee how it will be gamed is the
one who can be trusted with a real decision.

## Metric fixation

Muller names the disease **metric fixation**: "the seemingly irresistible pressure to measure
performance, to publicize it, and to reward it." [@muller_tyranny] It rests on three beliefs:
[@muller_tyranny]

1. **Replace judgment with numbers** — that standardised, comparable metrics can and *should* substitute
   for the judgment of experienced practitioners.
2. **Accountability = transparency** — that making the numbers public guarantees institutions serve their
   purpose.
3. **Motivate by extrinsic reward** — that people work best when pay and penalties are tied to measured
   performance (pay-for-performance, league tables).

Each belief is plausible; together they license replacing professional judgment with a dashboard. The
rest of the entry is what goes wrong when you do.

## Goodhart's law and Campbell's law

Two "laws" name the mechanism. Muller notes they were formulated in the same year, **1975**, on
opposite sides of the Atlantic, apparently by independent discovery: [@muller_tyranny]

- **Campbell's law** (the American social psychologist Donald T. Campbell) — *"the more any quantitative
  social indicator is used for social decision-making, the more subject it will be to corruption pressures
  and the more apt it will be to distort and corrupt the social processes it is intended to monitor."*
- **Goodhart's law** (the British economist C.A.E. Goodhart), in the form Muller quotes — *"any measure
  used for control is unreliable"*; or, as Muller glosses it, anything that can be measured and rewarded
  will be gamed. (The slogan you will hear more often — *"when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be
  a good measure"* — is a later popular paraphrase of the same idea, not Muller's wording.)

The engine under both: **people are self-conscious actors.** Attach stakes to a measurement and they
respond by **skewing the data to maximise the score**, whether or not the underlying reality improves.
[@muller_tyranny] A measure passively *observed* and the same measure *optimised against* are different
objects — the act of targeting corrupts the signal. The figure shows the signature: a proxy that
faithfully tracks the goal until the day it becomes a target, then peels away.

```{python}
#| label: fig-goodhart
#| fig-cap: "Goodhart's law in action. Before the metric carries stakes (left of the dashed line), the measured proxy tracks the true goal it was meant to capture. Once it becomes a target — tied to pay, ranking, or funding — effort flows into the proxy itself: it keeps climbing (gamed) while the true outcome stalls or declines. The gap that opens after targeting is the corruption Goodhart and Campbell describe."
#| code-fold: true
#| code-summary: "Show the figure code"

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
t = np.arange(0, 24)
target_start = 12
# Before targeting, proxy and true goal move together; after, proxy is gamed up, goal drifts down.
true_goal = 50 + 1.2 * t + rng.normal(0, 1.0, t.size)
true_goal[target_start:] = true_goal[target_start-1] - 0.8 * (t[target_start:] - t[target_start-1]) \
                           + rng.normal(0, 1.0, t.size - target_start)
proxy = true_goal.copy()
proxy[target_start:] = true_goal[target_start-1] + 3.2 * (t[target_start:] - t[target_start-1]) \
                       + rng.normal(0, 1.0, t.size - target_start)

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7.2, 4.2))
ax.plot(t, proxy, "o-", color="#e8531d", lw=2.2, ms=3, label="measured proxy (the metric)")
ax.plot(t, true_goal, "o-", color="#5e5e54", lw=2.2, ms=3, label="true goal (what we care about)")
ax.axvline(target_start - 0.5, color="#b7b6a8", ls="--", lw=1.4)
ax.text(target_start - 0.3, ax.get_ylim()[1]*0.96, "metric becomes a target", fontsize=8, color="#5e5e54")
ax.fill_between(t[target_start:], true_goal[target_start:], proxy[target_start:],
                color="#e8531d", alpha=0.10)
ax.text(18, 70, "the gap =\nGoodhart corruption", fontsize=8.5, color="#e8531d", ha="center")
ax.set_xlabel("time"); ax.set_ylabel("performance")
ax.set_title("A measure tracks the goal — until it becomes a target")
ax.legend(fontsize=8, frameon=False, loc="upper left")
fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

## How metrics get gamed

When judgement is reduced to a narrow score, practitioners "juke the stats." Muller catalogues the
recurring tactics: [@muller_tyranny]

- **Teaching to the test.** Effort shifts from the institution's true purpose to whatever lifts the
  metric — narrowing a curriculum to the tested subjects and test-taking strategy.
- **Creaming / cherry-picking.** Avoid the hard cases that might dent your success rate. Cardiac surgeons
  facing public scorecards refer the **sickest patients elsewhere** — improving their mortality numbers
  by *not treating the people who most need treatment*.
- **Lowering standards.** Hit the target by making it easier — inflating graduation rates by making
  courses easier to pass.
- **Outright gaming and fraud.** Misclassify or falsify: police downgrading **felonies to
  misdemeanours** (or thefts to "lost property") so they don't count against headline crime.
- **Short-termism.** Sacrifice long-term investment (R&D, training) for an immediate numerical bump.
- **Goal displacement.** The deepest pathology — the **metric means replace the organisational ends**
  they were meant to serve. Hitting the number *becomes* the mission.

::: {.callout-note title="Muller's case files"}
- **Education (No Child Left Behind).** High-stakes testing narrowed curricula to math and English and,
  in several cities, produced **systematic cheating** — teachers altering answer sheets. [@muller_tyranny]
- **Healthcare (surgical scorecards).** Public mortality rankings drove **case-selection bias**:
  surgeons declined high-risk patients to protect their stats. [@muller_tyranny]
- **Policing (Compstat / FBI reports).** Pressure to "reduce crime" led to **reclassifying** offences
  downward and chasing easy street arrests over hard long-term cases. [@muller_tyranny]
- **The military (Vietnam body counts).** McNamara's reliance on **body counts** as a success metric
  produced fabricated numbers — and soldiers risking their lives to find corpses to hit the target.
  [@muller_tyranny]
- **Universities (rankings).** League tables push schools toward glossy brochures aimed at the
  reputation survey, law schools toward massaging their admissions data, and all of them toward
  **homogenisation** — abandoning a distinctive mission to resemble the competitors the ranking rewards.
  [@muller_tyranny]
:::

## Medicine up close: a metric read with a statistician's eye

Muller's longest case is medicine, and it is worth slowing down on, because almost every failure in it is
a failure this encyclopedia has already taught you to spot. He begins by separating three jobs that
advocates run together: metrics as **information for the clinician**, metrics as **public report cards**
for consumers, and metrics as the basis of **pay-for-performance**. [@muller_tyranny] The first mostly
works; the last two are where things go wrong.

**Report cards and the missing control group.** New York pioneered public report cards for coronary
bypass surgery, scoring surgeons and hospitals on **30-day mortality**. Mortality duly fell. But only
patients who were *operated on* enter the statistic, and some high-risk patients were declined, or sent
out of state, where they no longer counted. [@muller_tyranny] That is
[sample selection](../03-regression/censoring-truncation-and-selection.qmd) produced by incentive: the
denominator was managed, not the outcome. And the headline improvement fails the most basic causal check.
The **same improvement happened in neighbouring Massachusetts, which published no report cards.**
[@muller_tyranny] A before-and-after comparison credited the policy; the comparison state, the core idea of
[difference-in-differences](../05-causal-inference/difference-in-differences.qmd), says the trend was
there anyway.

**Readmissions and the relabelled outcome.** From 2009 Medicare publicly reported 30-day readmission rates
for heart attack, heart failure, pneumonia and other conditions, and from **2012** it fined hospitals with
above-average rates. Readmissions fell. But hospitals could also place a returning patient on
**"observation status"**, billed as an outpatient and therefore not a readmission, or treat them in the
emergency room. Medicare observation stays rose **96 percent between 2006 and 2013**, and about **half**
the fall in readmissions came from patients who had in fact come back. [@muller_tyranny] Muller is fair
about it: some hospitals genuinely improved, and a later analysis found the hospitals that cut readmissions
were not the same ones that increased observation stays. The honest verdict is *mixed*, and you can only
reach it by asking **how the outcome is recorded**, not just whether it moved.

**Risk adjustment is also a metric.** By 2015 about **three-quarters** of reporting hospitals were being
penalised, disproportionately major teaching hospitals, which see the hardest cases, and hospitals in poor
areas. Readmission depends on patients' health, support and behaviour after discharge, which the hospital
barely controls. The fix is "risk adjustment", but Muller's point is that the risk model is "at least as
prone to mismeasurement and manipulation" as the metric it corrects. [@muller_tyranny] Adjust badly and you
penalise the institutions serving the sickest populations. This is the policy face of
[confounding](../03-regression/regression-and-causality.qmd): the comparison is between different patient
populations, and no adjustment is better than the model behind it.

**When the evidence is graded.** Muller leans on the pro-metrics literature itself. A RAND review of
pay-for-performance found that most studies measured process or intermediate outcomes, not whether patients
recovered, and that **"studies with stronger methodological designs were less likely to identify
significant improvements."** [@muller_tyranny] Weaker designs finding bigger effects is the same
signature you met in the [replication crisis](../02-inference/replication-crisis.qmd). He also flags the
reverse-causation trap. Crediting the Cleveland Clinic's excellence to its published outcome books is
*post hoc ergo propter hoc*: it was excellent first, and a clinic with good numbers has reason to publish
them. [@muller_tyranny]

**And one that worked.** The Michigan **Keystone** ICU project, led by Peter Pronovost, attacked
central-line bloodstream infections, estimated at some **82,000** a year in the US in 2001 and costing
\$12,000 to \$56,000 each, with almost 32,000 deaths. A short checklist, with monthly infection data fed
back to ICU leaders and **discussed with staff to learn from mistakes**, cut infections by **66 percent**.
[@muller_tyranny] Muller's diagnosis is that these were *diagnostic* metrics. What was measured matched what
clinicians already cared about, the data went to the people doing the work, and the outcome was almost
entirely under the hospital's control. Everything the report cards got wrong, Keystone got right.

## Surrogate outcomes: measuring the easy thing

A quieter failure: metric fixation pushes you to measure **what is easy to count**, not what matters.
Organisations track **inputs** (money spent) or **processes** (workshops held) because the real
**outcomes** are complex or delayed. [@muller_tyranny] Muller's barb is worth memorising: *"the ease of
measuring may be inversely proportional to the significance of what is measured."* [@muller_tyranny]
This is the field cousin of the [surrogate-outcome](../05-causal-inference/causal-machine-learning.qmd)
and [proxy-variable](../03-regression/regression-and-causality.qmd) problems — only here the substitution
is driven by managerial convenience, not statistical necessity.

## The costs

Metric fixation is not free, even when it isn't gamed: [@muller_tyranny]

- **A data arms race.** Effort migrates from frontline work to *documenting* it — more managers and
  analysts, producers spending their hours on reports instead of the job.
- **Discouraged innovation.** Real innovation needs experiments that might *fail*; metrics punish
  failure, so people retreat to safe, established, measurable goals.
- **Demoralisation.** Professionals resent the loss of autonomy and a scoreboard that conflicts with
  their vocational ethos — driving the most creative people out.

Muller's closing catalogue of "unintended but predictable" consequences adds several that a
statistician should recognise on sight: [@muller_tyranny]

- **Diminishing utility.** A new metric's first harvest is real — it exposes the badly performing
  **outliers**. But the metric keeps being collected from *everyone*, long after the low-hanging fruit is
  gone, until the marginal cost of gathering it exceeds the marginal benefit.
- **Rule cascades.** Each discovered form of gaming prompts a new rule to block it; complying with the
  accumulating rules slows the institution further.
- **Rewarding luck.** Measure an outcome the person barely controls and you reward and punish *chance*.
  This is a statistical error before it is a moral one: small units are noisy, so they crowd both ends
  of any league table (the ["little extremes"](../01-foundations/famous-paradoxes.qmd) problem), and next
  year's ranking [regresses toward the mean](../01-foundations/famous-paradoxes.qmd).
- **Discouraging risk-taking.** The intelligence analysts who eventually located Bin Laden worked the
  problem for years; measured at any point, their "failure rate" was 100 percent — until it wasn't.
- **Discouraging cooperation.** Tie a manager's bonus to their own unit's budget and ask whether they
  would move resources to another department for the good of the whole. A hospital CEO's honest answer,
  as the medical reformer Donald Berwick recounts it: only "if he were crazy." Metrics reward
  competition, not help.

## Thick and thin: the ethos a metric can't carry

Muller's "demoralisation" has a sharper diagnosis in Vincent & Hooper's account of research integrity,
which splits any professional value system into two schemas [@vincent_hooper_integrity]:

- a **thick ethos** — the immersed, ethically-reflective judgement of a practitioner who has internalised
  *why* the work matters;
- **thin values** — the rules, prohibitions, metrics, and incentives we need to *communicate, assess, and
  enforce* that ethos across a large, impersonal system.

Thin values are unavoidable at scale — you cannot run a hospital or a research sector on unspoken virtue
alone. The danger is that, as a value is "thinned" for mass transmission, three pathologies appear
[@vincent_hooper_integrity]:

- **Mistaking the map for the territory** — people obey the codified rule but never absorb the ethos it
  was a shorthand for; the metric becomes the whole of the value rather than a pointer to it.
- **Proxy failure** — because thin values must be simple to monitor, they become *proxies*, and targeting
  them reproduces exactly **Goodhart's and Campbell's laws** above [@vincent_hooper_integrity]. This is the
  same corruption, named from the incentive side.
- **Crowding-out** — the behavioural-economics mechanism behind Muller's demoralisation: extrinsic metrics
  and rewards can **displace the intrinsic motivation** (the thick ethos) they were meant to reinforce, so
  the scoreboard actively erodes the vocation it measures [@vincent_hooper_integrity].

Their prescription is not to abolish thin values but to hold an **"essential tension"**: keep the metrics
continually **grounded in — and answerable to — the thicker ethos they serve**, through ongoing reflection
on whether a given number still promotes the goal rather than supplanting it [@vincent_hooper_integrity].
That is the same conclusion Muller reaches from the management side, arrived at from ethics.

## The balanced verdict: when metrics work

Muller is **not** anti-measurement — he is anti-*fixation*. [@muller_tyranny] His own success stories
share a pattern. Compstat worked as a tool for deciding *where* to deploy police, and failed when threats
of demotion were used to push reported crime down. Standardised tests helped when teachers used them to
adjust their teaching, and failed when they became the main basis for reward and punishment. Keystone
worked because its measures matched professional values. [@muller_tyranny] Metrics are a valuable
**complement** to judgment, never a **replacement** for it. They work best when they are:
[@muller_tyranny]

- **Internal and diagnostic** — used by practitioners themselves to spot outliers and refine practice,
  not as a public cudgel.
- **Low-stakes** — not the sole basis of pay or promotion, so the incentive to game is weak. (Researchers
  trust the NAEP school tests precisely because nobody's fortunes ride on them.)
- **Bottom-up** — designed *with* the people measured, who then trust the metric's validity.

The core claim underneath: measurement is not an alternative to judgment, because **measurement demands
judgment**, about whether to measure, what to measure, what the result signifies, whether to attach
rewards, and to whom to release it. [@muller_tyranny]

### Muller's checklist

Muller closes with ten questions to ask before building or trusting a performance metric. Compressed,
they read like a design review: [@muller_tyranny]

| # | Ask | What the answer tells you |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **What kind of thing is being measured?** | The closer it is to inanimate matter, the more measurable. People react to being measured, most of all when rewards are attached, and react *helpfully* only to the extent they share the goal. |
| 2 | **How useful is the information?** | Measurable is not the same as worth measuring. Is it a real **proxy** for what you want to know? If not, don't measure it ([validity](../00-overture/concepts-and-measurement.qmd)). |
| 3 | **How useful are *more* metrics?** | Metrics are best at finding **outliers**, weak at ranking the middle and top; the marginal metric eventually costs more than it tells you. |
| 4 | **What does *not* measuring cost?** | Other information may exist: client, patient or parent judgement (how often parents ask for a particular teacher), or the verdict of a charity's own beneficiaries. |
| 5 | **For what purpose, and transparent to whom?** | The key split: **internal monitoring by practitioners** vs **external reward and punishment**. Crime data to position squad cars is not crime data to decide a commander's promotion. Low-stakes use is often the more effective. |
| 6 | **What does collecting it cost?** | Mostly opportunity cost: every hour spent documenting is an hour taken from the work itself. |
| 7 | **Why do the people at the top want it?** | Often because leaders parachuted in lack local knowledge, which argues for hiring from within. |
| 8 | **Who develops the measures?** | Build them bottom-up with the teachers, nurses and "cop on the beat" who hold the tacit knowledge. A metric works to the extent the measured believe in it. |
| 9 | **How will even a good measure be corrupted?** | Pay doctors per procedure and they over-treat; per patient seen and they rush; on outcomes and they **cream**. Every scheme has a failure mode — choosing among them is itself a judgement. |
| 10 | **What are the limits of the possible?** | Not every problem is soluble, and fewer by metrics. Transparency can make a problem more visible without making it more solvable ([limits of quantification](limits-of-quantification.qmd)). |

Two of these deserve a researcher's underline. Question 5 is the one that separates Keystone from the
report cards: the *same* number can be diagnostic or corrosive depending on who holds it and what rides on
it. And question 9 is Goodhart as a design constraint. You don't find a measure that can't be gamed; you
choose the gaming you can best live with and watch for it.

The mature stance the whole book has built toward: a number is an input to judgment, not a substitute
for it. The skill is holding both — rigorous measurement *and* the wisdom to know its limits.

## Common pitfalls

::: {.callout-warning title="Where this goes wrong"}

- **Attaching high stakes to a single number.** Goodhart guarantees it will be gamed; the tighter the
  incentive, the faster the corruption. [@muller_tyranny]

- **Measuring the proxy and forgetting the goal.** Optimise a surrogate and you can improve it while the
  real outcome decays — verify the proxy still tracks the thing you care about. [@muller_tyranny]

- **Counting inputs/processes as if they were outcomes.** Workshops held is not learning gained; spend
  follows ease of measurement, not importance. [@muller_tyranny]

- **Ignoring gaming when designing a metric.** If you can't say *how* people will game it, you haven't
  finished designing it. [@muller_tyranny]

- **Replacing professional judgment wholesale.** Metrics inform judgment; they don't substitute for the
  experience that knows which cases the number misreads. [@muller_tyranny]

- **Crediting the metric for a trend it didn't cause.** New York's bypass mortality fell after report
  cards, and so did Massachusetts's without them. A before-and-after comparison with no control group is
  not evidence that publishing numbers works. [@muller_tyranny]

- **Trusting a metric without asking how the outcome is recorded.** Readmissions fell partly because
  returning patients were relabelled as "observation" stays. When a number is rewarded, audit the
  *definition* as well as the level. [@muller_tyranny]

:::

## Appendix · the mechanism {.unnumbered}

::: {.math-appendix}

**Goodhart formalised.** Let the true goal be $G$ and a proxy $M=G+\eta$ with measurement slack $\eta$
an agent can influence at cost. *Passively*, $\eta\approx0$ and $\operatorname{Corr}(M,G)\approx1$ — the
proxy is informative. Introduce a reward $R(M)$ for high $M$. A rational agent now invests effort to
raise $\eta$ (gaming) up to where marginal reward equals marginal cost, *independently of* $G$. The
observed $M$ rises, but $\operatorname{Corr}(M,G)$ **collapses** — the very act of using $M$ for control
destroys its correlation with $G$. The proxy was a good measure *because* no one was optimising it; the
optimisation is what breaks it.

**Why surrogates drift.** A surrogate $S$ validated to satisfy $\mathbb E[G\mid S]=f(S)$ under
*observational* conditions need not preserve that relation under *intervention* on $S$ — exactly the
[$\operatorname{do}$ vs conditioning](../05-causal-inference/directed-acyclic-graphs.qmd) distinction.
Optimising $S$ intervenes on it, and the conditional relation that licensed $S$ as a stand-in can fail —
the managerial twin of [reward hacking](../04-predictive/interpretability-and-ml.qmd).

:::
