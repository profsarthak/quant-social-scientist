Pattern Formation & Reaction–Diffusion

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source

Spreading out usually smooths things over. Yet when two things spread at different speeds, a perfectly even landscape can break up by itself into spots and stripes with a typical spacing. The same equations describe slime-mould cells clumping and people gathering into cities.

Diffusion is the great leveller: heat evens out, ink spreads, crowds thin. Turing’s surprise was that diffusion can also create structure. If one ingredient boosts itself locally while a second, faster- spreading ingredient holds it back over a wider area, an even state that would otherwise be stable breaks into a pattern, and the pattern has a predictable spacing. Sayama shows that a simple model of people moving towards economic activity is mathematically the same as a classic model of slime-mould aggregation. Clumping, and the typical distance between clumps, follows from a single inequality.

Why it matters

Social scientists see spatial patterns everywhere: cities spaced at rough intervals, clusters of firms, neighbourhoods that differ block by block. The natural reflex is to explain each clump by something special about that place, such as a river, a port or a policy. Continuous field models offer a rival explanation that is worth ruling out first. A landscape can be identical everywhere and still break up into clumps because of how things move and interact, with nothing special about any one location (Sayama 2015).

This matters in three ways. First, it is a mechanism: Sayama derives a population–economy model in which people drift towards places with more economic activity, and finds conditions under which an even spread of people is unstable and towns form on their own (Sayama 2015). Second, it gives a testable quantity, a characteristic length between clumps, that follows from the parameters. Third, it is a warning that “this place is different because it is special” and “this place is different because symmetry broke there by chance” can leave the same map. That is the same identification problem met in El Farol & coordination, where opposite motives produce the same cities.

From cells to a continuous field

Cellular automata describe space as a grid of cells. A continuous field model takes the grid to its limit: the state of the system is a smooth surface, such as population density p(x,t), defined at every point in space and every moment in time. Its rules are partial differential equations (PDEs), which are ordinary rates of change that are also allowed to depend on the shape of the surface nearby (Sayama 2015). Sayama notes that PDEs are far older than CA, but their use for self-organisation took off around the same time, sparked by Turing’s 1952 paper on the chemical basis of morphogenesis (as reported in Sayama (2015)).

Two pieces of shape do almost all the work:

  • The gradient points uphill. It is the direction in which the surface rises most steeply, and its length is how steep that rise is.
  • The Laplacian measures how far a point sits below the average of its neighbours. Sayama’s plain-language recipe, once space is a grid: add up the differences between each neighbour and yourself (Sayama 2015). Positive means you are in a dip, negative means you are on a hump.

This recipe is exactly the one used for diffusion along the links of a network in dynamics on networks, where the graph Laplacian plays the same role.

Two ways to move: diffusion and drift

Sayama builds every spatial model from a single template, the transport equation: the density at a point changes by whatever flows in minus whatever flows out, plus anything created or destroyed there (Sayama 2015). The modelling choices are all about what drives the flow.

Diffusion. Random wandering at the micro level becomes, at the macro level, a flow from high to low: humps flatten and dips fill until the surface is level. Sayama’s argument for writing the flow as proportional to the slope alone (Fick’s first law) is an intuition about levels: smoothing should depend on the shape of the surface, not on how high it sits. Raising the whole landscape by 10 should not make it level out faster (Sayama 2015). The result is the diffusion equation, in which density rises where the Laplacian is positive (dips fill) and falls where it is negative (humps shrink).

Drift towards a signal. Sometimes everyone at a spot moves in the same direction because they all sense the same thing. Then the flow is density times velocity, and the velocity points up the gradient of whatever attracts them (Sayama 2015).

People, money and slime mould

Sayama’s worked example is a model you can build from five plain assumptions (Sayama 2015):

  1. The total population does not change over the period modelled.
  2. More people in a place means more economic activity there.
  3. Without people, economic activity decays.
  4. Both people and economic activity diffuse gradually.
  5. People are drawn towards places where the economy is more active.

Assumptions 1–3 are local: people p are conserved, and money m is produced at rate \alpha p and decays at rate \beta m. Assumption 4 adds a diffusion term to each. Assumption 5 adds a drift of people up the gradient of money, which Sayama jokingly calls “moneytaxis” (Sayama 2015):

\frac{\partial p}{\partial t} = D_p\nabla^2 p-\gamma\,\nabla\cdot(p\,\nabla m),\qquad \frac{\partial m}{\partial t} = D_m\nabla^2 m+\alpha p-\beta m .

The punchline is that this is, symbol for symbol, the Keller–Segel model of chemotaxis, proposed by Evelyn Keller and Lee Segel to explain how the slime mould Dictyostelium discoideum aggregates when food runs out (as reported in Sayama (2015)). Swap “cells” for people and “cAMP”, the chemical signal the cells secrete and follow, for money, and the assumptions are identical. Sayama’s simulations show spots of aggregated cells forming from an almost even start, a typical distance between neighbouring spots, and he draws the parallel with the typical spacing between major cities on a map (Sayama 2015).

When does an even spread break up?

An even spread of people with a matching level of activity is always an equilibrium: nothing is flowing anywhere. The question is whether it is stable. Sayama answers it with the same move used for non-spatial systems: nudge the equilibrium and see whether the nudge grows or dies away. The new trick is to make the nudge a sine wave across space. A wave stays a wave under the Laplacian (it is only rescaled), so each wavelength can be studied on its own, as an ordinary two-variable system (Sayama 2015).

The result is one inequality. Aggregation happens when

\chi\,a_{eq}\,f \;>\; \mu\,k ,

written in the slime-mould notation: \chi is how strongly cells follow the signal, a_{eq} the average density, f the rate at which cells produce the signal, \mu how fast cells diffuse and k how fast the signal decays (Sayama 2015). Read in the population–economy language, Sayama’s interpretation is that concentration into cities is more likely when

  • people respond more strongly to economic activity (stronger “moneytaxis”, \chi),
  • there are more people overall (a_{eq}), or
  • people generate economic value faster (f),

and less likely when people and economic activity spread out faster, or when activity decays more quickly (k) (Sayama 2015).

The same calculation also tells you which wavelengths grow. Ripples longer than a critical length \ell_c grow, and shorter ripples die out, so \ell_c sets the characteristic distance between the first clumps (Sayama 2015). With Sayama’s parameters (\mu = D = 10^{-4}, f = k = a_{eq} = 1) the threshold is \chi_c = 10^{-4}, and his simulations switch from smooth to clumped right at that value (Sayama 2015).

Turing’s paradox: diffusion that creates structure

Reaction–diffusion systems are the special case in which each ingredient has local “reaction” terms plus plain diffusion of itself, with no drift (Sayama 2015). That clean separation is why they are so popular: it is easy to take any non-spatial model (an epidemic, a predator–prey system) and spread it over space, and their stability analysis has a shortcut (see the appendix) (Sayama 2015).

Sayama’s showcase is a linear version of Turing’s model with two chemicals, u and v. In the parameter setting he uses, u boosts both itself and v, while v suppresses both. Without diffusion the even state is stable: any disturbance dies out. Add diffusion, which is normally a smoothing force, and the even state can become unstable, so a pattern grows out of tiny random noise. This is diffusion-induced (Turing) instability (Sayama 2015).

The trick is that the two ingredients spread at different speeds. If the suppressor v spreads much faster than the booster u, then a small local excess of u grows on the spot, while the v it produces leaks away to hold down u in a ring around it. This is short-range activation and long-range inhibition (Sayama 2015). Sayama works out the threshold exactly: with his reaction terms, v must spread at least 4.5 times faster than u. His simulations show a sharp switch between an even state and a pattern across that ratio, which is a Turing bifurcation (Sayama 2015).

The analysis also predicts the size of the pattern. The wavelength that grows fastest is about 0.113 units, so a unit square should hold about nine ridges and valleys, and Sayama’s simulation does (Sayama 2015). Our hand-built version below reproduces both predictions.

Show the figure code (dispersion relation + 2-D simulation, by hand) 
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from matplotlib.colors import LinearSegmentedColormap

# Sayama's linear Turing model: du/dt = a(u-h)+b(v-k)+Du*lap(u), dv/dt = c(u-h)+d(v-k)+Dv*lap(v)
a, b, c, d = 1.0, -1.0, 2.0, -1.5
J = np.array([[a, b], [c, d]])
Du = 1e-4

def growth(omega, rho):
    """Largest real eigenvalue of J - diag(Du, rho*Du) * omega^2, for each spatial frequency."""
    out = np.empty_like(omega)
    for i, w in enumerate(omega):
        out[i] = np.linalg.eigvals(J - np.diag([Du, rho * Du]) * w**2).real.max()
    return out

omega = np.linspace(20, 160, 1400)
ell = 2 * np.pi / omega                                  # wavelength of each ripple

fig, (axL, axR) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(10.4, 4.5), gridspec_kw={"width_ratios": [1.25, 1]})
for rho, col, ls, lab in [(3, "#b7b6a8", "-", "ratio 3: stays even"),
                          (4.5, "#8a8a7e", "--", "ratio 4.5: critical"),
                          (6, "#e8531d", "-", "ratio 6: pattern grows")]:
    axL.plot(ell, growth(omega, rho), color=col, ls=ls, lw=2.2, label=lab)
g6 = growth(omega, 6)
best = ell[g6.argmax()]
axL.axhline(0, color="#161613", lw=1)
axL.axvline(best, color="#e8531d", lw=1, ls=":")
axL.annotate(f"fastest: wavelength {best:.3f}", xy=(best, g6.max()), xytext=(0.15, 0.09),
             fontsize=9, arrowprops=dict(arrowstyle="-", color="#5e5e54", lw=0.8))
axL.set_xlim(0.04, 0.31); axL.set_ylim(-0.32, 0.16)
axL.set_xlabel("wavelength of the ripple (units of space)")
axL.set_ylabel("growth rate (largest real eigenvalue)")
axL.set_title("Which ripples grow?")
axL.legend(frameon=False, fontsize=8.5, loc="lower right")

# 2-D simulation on the unit square, discretised as a CA (Sayama, Code 13.8), periodic edges
n, dt, rho = 100, 0.02, 6
dh = 1 / n
rng = np.random.default_rng(3)
u = 1 + rng.uniform(-0.03, 0.03, (n, n))
v = 1 + rng.uniform(-0.03, 0.03, (n, n))
lap = lambda z: (np.roll(z, 1, 0) + np.roll(z, -1, 0) + np.roll(z, 1, 1) + np.roll(z, -1, 1) - 4 * z) / dh**2
for _ in range(int(40 / dt)):
    ru, rv = a * (u - 1) + b * (v - 1), c * (u - 1) + d * (v - 1)
    u, v = u + (ru + Du * lap(u)) * dt, v + (rv + rho * Du * lap(v)) * dt

ink = LinearSegmentedColormap.from_list("bone_ink", ["#f4f3ec", "#b7b6a8", "#161613"])
axR.imshow(u, cmap=ink, origin="lower", extent=(0, 1, 0, 1), interpolation="bilinear")
axR.set_title("activator after 40 time units (ratio 6)")
axR.set_xlabel("x"); axR.set_ylabel("y"); axR.grid(False)
fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()

Two further lessons from Sayama’s tour, both visible in the figure’s code. The pattern needs noise: started exactly at the even state, nothing would break the symmetry, so a small random perturbation is part of the model, not a nuisance (Sayama 2015). And because the pattern’s spacing comes from the diffusion constants, scaling matters: Sayama’s rescaling exercise on a spatial predator–prey model cuts six parameters to two, the ratio of predator decay to prey growth and the ratio of the two diffusion speeds, which is a reminder that ratios, not raw rates, usually decide what happens (Sayama 2015).

Beyond the linear model

Sayama closes the chapter with two nonlinear reaction–diffusion systems that show how rich the behaviour can get (Sayama 2015):

  • The Belousov–Zhabotinsky reaction, a chemical mixture that oscillates for a long time before settling down. In a shallow dish, oscillations that start out of step in different places become travelling waves and spirals. Its simplified “Oregonator” model has an activator and an inhibitor, like Turing’s, but the local dynamics oscillate.
  • The Gray–Scott model, built on a self-catalysing reaction, whose patterns for different feed and removal rates include spots that grow, divide and die, looking strikingly like living cells.

For a social scientist the lesson is not the chemistry. It is that a model with two interacting quantities and two spreading speeds already has stable states, waves, spots and stripes available to it, and which one you see is decided by a few parameter ratios.

Common pitfalls

WarningWhere this goes wrong
  • Explaining every clump by local specialness. An even landscape can break up by itself. Before attributing a city or cluster to the site, ask whether a symmetry-breaking mechanism alone could produce the observed spacing. (Sayama 2015)
  • Treating diffusion as always smoothing. With two ingredients spreading at different speeds, diffusion can be the cause of structure. Stability without space does not guarantee stability with it. (Sayama 2015)
  • Reading a pattern’s spacing as a fact about places. The characteristic distance comes from the ratio of spreading speeds and reaction rates. Change those and the spacing changes everywhere at once. (Sayama 2015)
  • Forgetting the noise. A perfectly even start never breaks symmetry, while a large perturbation can crash the simulation. The size of the initial noise is a modelling choice to report. (Sayama 2015)
  • Taking big time steps. Discretised PDEs accumulate numerical error quickly. Sayama shows a smooth transport simulation that goes visibly wrong when the time step rises from 0.01 to 0.1. Check that results survive a smaller step. (Sayama 2015)
  • Over-reading the analogy. That people and slime-mould cells obey the same equations is a statement about a model’s structure, not evidence that the social mechanism is real. It shows what the mechanism would do. Whether it operates is an empirical question.

Appendix · the mathematics

Transport and diffusion. With concentration c, flux J and source/sink s, \partial c/\partial t=-\nabla\cdot J+s. Writing the flux as density times velocity gives \partial c/\partial t=-\nabla\cdot(c\,w)+s. Fick’s first law sets J=-\alpha\nabla c, so with constant \alpha, \frac{\partial c}{\partial t}=\alpha\nabla^2 c+s . The rejected alternative, flux -c\,\alpha\nabla c, would make smoothing faster on a uniformly raised surface (Sayama 2015).

Discretising. Forward Euler in time, f(x,t+\Delta t)=f(x,t)+F(\cdot)\,\Delta t; central differences in space, \partial f/\partial x\approx[f(x+\Delta x)-f(x-\Delta x)]/(2\Delta x), and in n dimensions \nabla^2 f\approx\frac{\sum_{\text{nearest neighbours}}f-2n\,f(x)}{\Delta x^2}. For plain diffusion this explicit scheme also needs a small enough step. The standard numerical-analysis requirement in 2-D (our addition, not Sayama’s) is D\,\Delta t/\Delta x^2\le 1/4, which the figure meets (6\times10^{-4}\cdot0.02/10^{-4}=0.12).

Linear stability of a field model. Write f=f_{eq}+\sin(\omega x+\phi)\,\Delta f(t). Because \partial^2/\partial x^2 maps a sine wave to -\omega^2 times itself, the sine is an eigenfunction of the Laplacian (the infinite-dimensional analogue of an eigenvector), and each \omega yields an ordinary linear system for the amplitudes. For Keller–Segel, dropping products of small terms gives \frac{d}{dt}\begin{pmatrix}\Delta a\\\Delta c\end{pmatrix}= \begin{pmatrix}-\mu\omega^2 & \chi a_{eq}\omega^2\\ f & -D\omega^2-k\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}\Delta a\\\Delta c\end{pmatrix}. The trace is negative, so instability needs a negative determinant: \chi a_{eq}\omega^2 f>\mu\omega^2(D\omega^2+k), i.e. (\chi a_{eq}f-\mu k)/(\mu D)>\omega^2. A positive \omega^2 exists if and only if \chi a_{eq}f>\mu k, and the growing wavelengths satisfy \ell=\frac{2\pi}{\omega}>2\pi\sqrt{\frac{\mu D}{\chi a_{eq}f-\mu k}}=\ell_c (Sayama 2015). With Sayama’s values and, say, \chi=3\times10^{-4}, \ell_c\approx0.044 (our arithmetic).

The reaction–diffusion shortcut. For \partial f_i/\partial t=R_i(f)+D_i\nabla^2 f_i, the same sine-wave substitution gives \frac{d\,\Delta f}{dt}=\big(J-D\,\omega^2\big)\Delta f, where J is the Jacobian of the reaction terms at the even state and D the diagonal matrix of diffusion constants (Sayama 2015). Stability for every \omega needs a positive determinant and a negative trace (the trace–determinant rule).

The Turing condition. For J=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix} stable on its own (\det J>0, \operatorname{tr}J<0), the trace of J-D\omega^2 stays negative, so only the determinant can change sign. With z=\omega^2 it is negative when g(z)=-D_uD_vz^2+(aD_v+dD_u)z-\det J>0 for some z>0. The intercept -\det J is negative, so the parabola’s peak must sit at positive z and rise above zero: aD_v+dD_u>2\sqrt{D_uD_v\det J} (Sayama 2015). With \rho=D_v/D_u this becomes a\rho+d>2\sqrt{\rho\det J}; for (1,-1,2,-1.5), \rho-1.5>2\sqrt{0.5\rho}, i.e. \rho^2-5\rho+2.25>0 with \rho>1.5, so \rho>4.5 (Sayama 2015).

The fastest-growing wavelength. Maximising the larger eigenvalue \lambda_+=\tfrac12\Big[-(0.5+7\times10^{-4}\omega^2)+\sqrt{2.5\times10^{-7}\omega^4+2.5\times10^{-3}\omega^2-1.75}\Big] over z=\omega^2 gives z\approx3083, \omega\approx55.5 and \ell=2\pi/\omega\approx0.113 (Sayama 2015). Our numerical check gives the same peak, with growth rate \lambda_+\approx0.114 and an unstable band of wavelengths from about 0.080 to 0.171.

::: {.lead-kernel}
Diffusion is the great leveller: heat evens out, ink spreads, crowds thin. Turing's surprise was that
**diffusion can also create structure**. If one ingredient boosts itself locally while a second, faster-
spreading ingredient holds it back over a wider area, an even state that would otherwise be stable breaks
into a pattern, and the pattern has a **predictable spacing**. Sayama shows that a simple model of people
moving towards economic activity is mathematically the same as a classic model of slime-mould
aggregation. Clumping, and the typical distance between clumps, follows from a single inequality.
:::

## Why it matters

Social scientists see spatial patterns everywhere: cities spaced at rough intervals, clusters of firms,
neighbourhoods that differ block by block. The natural reflex is to explain each clump by something
special about that place, such as a river, a port or a policy. Continuous field models offer a rival
explanation that is worth ruling out first. A landscape can be **identical everywhere** and still break up
into clumps because of how things move and interact, with nothing special about any one location
[@sayama_complex].

This matters in three ways. First, it is a mechanism: Sayama derives a population–economy model in which
people drift towards places with more economic activity, and finds conditions under which an even spread
of people is unstable and towns form on their own [@sayama_complex]. Second, it gives a testable
quantity, a **characteristic length** between clumps, that follows from the parameters. Third, it is a
warning that "this place is different because it is special" and "this place is different because
symmetry broke there by chance" can leave the same map. That is the same identification problem met in
[El Farol & coordination](el-farol-and-coordination.qmd#sec-city-formation), where opposite motives
produce the same cities.

## From cells to a continuous field

[Cellular automata](cellular-automata.qmd) describe space as a grid of cells. A **continuous field model**
takes the grid to its limit: the state of the system is a smooth surface, such as population density
$p(x,t)$, defined at every point in space and every moment in time. Its rules are **partial differential
equations** (PDEs), which are ordinary rates of change that are also allowed to depend on the *shape* of
the surface nearby [@sayama_complex]. Sayama notes that PDEs are far older than CA, but their use for
self-organisation took off around the same time, sparked by Turing's 1952 paper on the chemical basis of
morphogenesis (as reported in @sayama_complex).

Two pieces of shape do almost all the work:

- The **gradient** points uphill. It is the direction in which the surface rises most steeply, and its
  length is how steep that rise is.
- The **Laplacian** measures how far a point sits *below the average of its neighbours*. Sayama's
  plain-language recipe, once space is a grid: add up the differences between each neighbour and
  yourself [@sayama_complex]. Positive means you are in a dip, negative means you are on a hump.

This recipe is exactly the one used for diffusion along the links of a network in
[dynamics on networks](dynamics-on-networks.qmd), where the graph Laplacian plays the same role.

## Two ways to move: diffusion and drift

Sayama builds every spatial model from a single template, the **transport equation**: the density at a
point changes by whatever flows in minus whatever flows out, plus anything created or destroyed there
[@sayama_complex]. The modelling choices are all about *what drives the flow*.

**Diffusion.** Random wandering at the micro level becomes, at the macro level, a flow from high to low:
humps flatten and dips fill until the surface is level. Sayama's argument for writing the flow as
proportional to the slope alone (Fick's first law) is an intuition about levels: smoothing should depend
on the *shape* of the surface, not on how high it sits. Raising the whole landscape by 10 should not make
it level out faster [@sayama_complex]. The result is the **diffusion equation**, in which density rises
where the Laplacian is positive (dips fill) and falls where it is negative (humps shrink).

**Drift towards a signal.** Sometimes everyone at a spot moves in the same direction because they all
sense the same thing. Then the flow is *density times velocity*, and the velocity points up the gradient
of whatever attracts them [@sayama_complex].

## People, money and slime mould

Sayama's worked example is a model you can build from five plain assumptions [@sayama_complex]:

1. The total population does not change over the period modelled.
2. More people in a place means more economic activity there.
3. Without people, economic activity decays.
4. Both people and economic activity diffuse gradually.
5. People are drawn towards places where the economy is more active.

Assumptions 1–3 are local: people $p$ are conserved, and money $m$ is produced at rate $\alpha p$ and
decays at rate $\beta m$. Assumption 4 adds a diffusion term to each. Assumption 5 adds a drift of people
up the gradient of money, which Sayama jokingly calls "moneytaxis" [@sayama_complex]:

$$
\frac{\partial p}{\partial t} = D_p\nabla^2 p-\gamma\,\nabla\cdot(p\,\nabla m),\qquad
\frac{\partial m}{\partial t} = D_m\nabla^2 m+\alpha p-\beta m .
$$

The punchline is that this is, symbol for symbol, the **Keller–Segel model** of *chemotaxis*, proposed by
Evelyn Keller and Lee Segel to explain how the slime mould *Dictyostelium discoideum* aggregates when food
runs out (as reported in @sayama_complex). Swap "cells" for people and "cAMP", the chemical signal the
cells secrete and follow, for money, and the assumptions are identical. Sayama's simulations show spots
of aggregated cells forming from an almost even start, a typical distance between neighbouring spots, and
he draws the parallel with the typical spacing between major cities on a map [@sayama_complex].

## When does an even spread break up? {#sec-keller-segel-threshold}

An even spread of people with a matching level of activity is always an equilibrium: nothing is flowing
anywhere. The question is whether it is **stable**. Sayama answers it with the same move used for
non-spatial systems: nudge the equilibrium and see whether the nudge grows or dies away. The new trick is
to make the nudge a **sine wave** across space. A wave stays a wave under the Laplacian (it is only
rescaled), so each wavelength can be studied on its own, as an ordinary two-variable system
[@sayama_complex].

The result is one inequality. Aggregation happens when

$$
\chi\,a_{eq}\,f \;>\; \mu\,k ,
$$

written in the slime-mould notation: $\chi$ is how strongly cells follow the signal, $a_{eq}$ the average
density, $f$ the rate at which cells produce the signal, $\mu$ how fast cells diffuse and $k$ how fast the
signal decays [@sayama_complex]. Read in the population–economy language, Sayama's interpretation is that
concentration into cities is **more likely** when

- people respond more strongly to economic activity (stronger "moneytaxis", $\chi$),
- there are more people overall ($a_{eq}$), or
- people generate economic value faster ($f$),

and **less likely** when people and economic activity spread out faster, or when activity decays more
quickly ($k$) [@sayama_complex].

The same calculation also tells you *which* wavelengths grow. Ripples longer than a critical length
$\ell_c$ grow, and shorter ripples die out, so $\ell_c$ sets the characteristic distance between the first
clumps [@sayama_complex]. With Sayama's parameters ($\mu = D = 10^{-4}$, $f = k = a_{eq} = 1$) the
threshold is $\chi_c = 10^{-4}$, and his simulations switch from smooth to clumped right at that value
[@sayama_complex].

## Turing's paradox: diffusion that creates structure {#sec-turing}

**Reaction–diffusion systems** are the special case in which each ingredient has local "reaction" terms
plus plain diffusion of itself, with no drift [@sayama_complex]. That clean separation is why they are so
popular: it is easy to take any non-spatial model (an epidemic, a predator–prey system) and spread it over
space, and their stability analysis has a shortcut (see the appendix) [@sayama_complex].

Sayama's showcase is a linear version of Turing's model with two chemicals, $u$ and $v$. In the
parameter setting he uses, $u$ boosts both itself and $v$, while $v$ suppresses both. Without diffusion
the even state is **stable**: any disturbance dies out. Add diffusion, which is normally a smoothing
force, and the even state can become **unstable**, so a pattern grows out of tiny random noise. This is
**diffusion-induced (Turing) instability** [@sayama_complex].

The trick is that the two ingredients spread at **different speeds**. If the suppressor $v$ spreads much
faster than the booster $u$, then a small local excess of $u$ grows on the spot, while the $v$ it
produces leaks away to hold down $u$ in a ring around it. This is **short-range activation and long-range
inhibition** [@sayama_complex]. Sayama works out the threshold exactly: with his reaction terms, $v$ must
spread at least **4.5 times** faster than $u$. His simulations show a sharp switch between an even state
and a pattern across that ratio, which is a **Turing bifurcation** [@sayama_complex].

The analysis also predicts the **size** of the pattern. The wavelength that grows fastest is about
$0.113$ units, so a unit square should hold about nine ridges and valleys, and Sayama's simulation does
[@sayama_complex]. Our hand-built version below reproduces both predictions.

```{python}
#| label: fig-turing-pattern
#| fig-cap: "Sayama's linear Turing model, rebuilt by hand. Left: how fast a ripple of each wavelength grows (above zero) or decays (below zero), from the eigenvalues of the linearised system. When the inhibitor spreads 3 times faster than the activator (pale), every wavelength decays and the even state survives. At the critical ratio of 4.5 (dashed olive) one wavelength just balances. At a ratio of 6 (orange) a band of wavelengths grows, fastest at about 0.113. Right: the activator after 40 time units of the ratio-6 model on a unit square, started from the even state plus ±3% noise (darker = more). Counting gives about nine stripes and spots across each side, as predicted. The colour scale is stretched to the range of the final pattern, because in this linear model the amplitude keeps growing; only the shape and spacing are meaningful."
#| code-fold: true
#| code-summary: "Show the figure code (dispersion relation + 2-D simulation, by hand)"

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from matplotlib.colors import LinearSegmentedColormap

# Sayama's linear Turing model: du/dt = a(u-h)+b(v-k)+Du*lap(u), dv/dt = c(u-h)+d(v-k)+Dv*lap(v)
a, b, c, d = 1.0, -1.0, 2.0, -1.5
J = np.array([[a, b], [c, d]])
Du = 1e-4

def growth(omega, rho):
    """Largest real eigenvalue of J - diag(Du, rho*Du) * omega^2, for each spatial frequency."""
    out = np.empty_like(omega)
    for i, w in enumerate(omega):
        out[i] = np.linalg.eigvals(J - np.diag([Du, rho * Du]) * w**2).real.max()
    return out

omega = np.linspace(20, 160, 1400)
ell = 2 * np.pi / omega                                  # wavelength of each ripple

fig, (axL, axR) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(10.4, 4.5), gridspec_kw={"width_ratios": [1.25, 1]})
for rho, col, ls, lab in [(3, "#b7b6a8", "-", "ratio 3: stays even"),
                          (4.5, "#8a8a7e", "--", "ratio 4.5: critical"),
                          (6, "#e8531d", "-", "ratio 6: pattern grows")]:
    axL.plot(ell, growth(omega, rho), color=col, ls=ls, lw=2.2, label=lab)
g6 = growth(omega, 6)
best = ell[g6.argmax()]
axL.axhline(0, color="#161613", lw=1)
axL.axvline(best, color="#e8531d", lw=1, ls=":")
axL.annotate(f"fastest: wavelength {best:.3f}", xy=(best, g6.max()), xytext=(0.15, 0.09),
             fontsize=9, arrowprops=dict(arrowstyle="-", color="#5e5e54", lw=0.8))
axL.set_xlim(0.04, 0.31); axL.set_ylim(-0.32, 0.16)
axL.set_xlabel("wavelength of the ripple (units of space)")
axL.set_ylabel("growth rate (largest real eigenvalue)")
axL.set_title("Which ripples grow?")
axL.legend(frameon=False, fontsize=8.5, loc="lower right")

# 2-D simulation on the unit square, discretised as a CA (Sayama, Code 13.8), periodic edges
n, dt, rho = 100, 0.02, 6
dh = 1 / n
rng = np.random.default_rng(3)
u = 1 + rng.uniform(-0.03, 0.03, (n, n))
v = 1 + rng.uniform(-0.03, 0.03, (n, n))
lap = lambda z: (np.roll(z, 1, 0) + np.roll(z, -1, 0) + np.roll(z, 1, 1) + np.roll(z, -1, 1) - 4 * z) / dh**2
for _ in range(int(40 / dt)):
    ru, rv = a * (u - 1) + b * (v - 1), c * (u - 1) + d * (v - 1)
    u, v = u + (ru + Du * lap(u)) * dt, v + (rv + rho * Du * lap(v)) * dt

ink = LinearSegmentedColormap.from_list("bone_ink", ["#f4f3ec", "#b7b6a8", "#161613"])
axR.imshow(u, cmap=ink, origin="lower", extent=(0, 1, 0, 1), interpolation="bilinear")
axR.set_title("activator after 40 time units (ratio 6)")
axR.set_xlabel("x"); axR.set_ylabel("y"); axR.grid(False)
fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

Two further lessons from Sayama's tour, both visible in the figure's code. The pattern needs **noise**:
started exactly at the even state, nothing would break the symmetry, so a small random perturbation is
part of the model, not a nuisance [@sayama_complex]. And because the pattern's spacing comes from the
diffusion constants, **scaling** matters: Sayama's rescaling exercise on a spatial predator–prey model
cuts six parameters to two, the ratio of predator decay to prey growth and the ratio of the two diffusion
speeds, which is a reminder that ratios, not raw rates, usually decide what happens [@sayama_complex].

## Beyond the linear model

Sayama closes the chapter with two nonlinear reaction–diffusion systems that show how rich the behaviour
can get [@sayama_complex]:

- **The Belousov–Zhabotinsky reaction**, a chemical mixture that oscillates for a long time before
  settling down. In a shallow dish, oscillations that start out of step in different places become
  travelling waves and spirals. Its simplified "Oregonator" model has an activator and an inhibitor, like
  Turing's, but the local dynamics oscillate.
- **The Gray–Scott model**, built on a self-catalysing reaction, whose patterns for different feed and
  removal rates include spots that grow, divide and die, looking strikingly like living cells.

For a social scientist the lesson is not the chemistry. It is that a model with two interacting
quantities and two spreading speeds already has stable states, waves, spots and stripes available to it,
and which one you see is decided by a few parameter ratios.

## Common pitfalls

::: {.callout-warning title="Where this goes wrong"}
- **Explaining every clump by local specialness.** An even landscape can break up by itself. Before
  attributing a city or cluster to the site, ask whether a symmetry-breaking mechanism alone could produce
  the observed spacing. [@sayama_complex]
- **Treating diffusion as always smoothing.** With two ingredients spreading at different speeds,
  diffusion can be the *cause* of structure. Stability without space does not guarantee stability with
  it. [@sayama_complex]
- **Reading a pattern's spacing as a fact about places.** The characteristic distance comes from the
  ratio of spreading speeds and reaction rates. Change those and the spacing changes everywhere at once.
  [@sayama_complex]
- **Forgetting the noise.** A perfectly even start never breaks symmetry, while a large perturbation can
  crash the simulation. The size of the initial noise is a modelling choice to report. [@sayama_complex]
- **Taking big time steps.** Discretised PDEs accumulate numerical error quickly. Sayama shows a smooth
  transport simulation that goes visibly wrong when the time step rises from 0.01 to 0.1. Check that
  results survive a smaller step. [@sayama_complex]
- **Over-reading the analogy.** That people and slime-mould cells obey the *same equations* is a
  statement about a model's structure, not evidence that the social mechanism is real. It shows what the
  mechanism *would* do. Whether it operates is an empirical question.
:::

## Appendix · the mathematics {.unnumbered}

::: {.math-appendix}

**Transport and diffusion.** With concentration $c$, flux $J$ and source/sink $s$,
$\partial c/\partial t=-\nabla\cdot J+s$. Writing the flux as density times velocity gives
$\partial c/\partial t=-\nabla\cdot(c\,w)+s$. Fick's first law sets $J=-\alpha\nabla c$, so with constant
$\alpha$,
$$\frac{\partial c}{\partial t}=\alpha\nabla^2 c+s .$$
The rejected alternative, flux $-c\,\alpha\nabla c$, would make smoothing faster on a uniformly raised
surface [@sayama_complex].

**Discretising.** Forward Euler in time, $f(x,t+\Delta t)=f(x,t)+F(\cdot)\,\Delta t$; central differences
in space, $\partial f/\partial x\approx[f(x+\Delta x)-f(x-\Delta x)]/(2\Delta x)$, and in $n$ dimensions
$$\nabla^2 f\approx\frac{\sum_{\text{nearest neighbours}}f-2n\,f(x)}{\Delta x^2}.$$
For plain diffusion this explicit scheme also needs a small enough step. The standard numerical-analysis
requirement in 2-D (our addition, not Sayama's) is $D\,\Delta t/\Delta x^2\le 1/4$, which the figure meets
($6\times10^{-4}\cdot0.02/10^{-4}=0.12$).

**Linear stability of a field model.** Write $f=f_{eq}+\sin(\omega x+\phi)\,\Delta f(t)$. Because
$\partial^2/\partial x^2$ maps a sine wave to $-\omega^2$ times itself, the sine is an *eigenfunction* of
the Laplacian (the infinite-dimensional analogue of an eigenvector), and each $\omega$ yields an ordinary
linear system for the amplitudes. For Keller–Segel, dropping products of small terms gives
$$\frac{d}{dt}\begin{pmatrix}\Delta a\\\Delta c\end{pmatrix}=
\begin{pmatrix}-\mu\omega^2 & \chi a_{eq}\omega^2\\ f & -D\omega^2-k\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}\Delta a\\\Delta c\end{pmatrix}.$$
The trace is negative, so instability needs a negative determinant:
$\chi a_{eq}\omega^2 f>\mu\omega^2(D\omega^2+k)$, i.e.
$(\chi a_{eq}f-\mu k)/(\mu D)>\omega^2$. A positive $\omega^2$ exists if and only if $\chi a_{eq}f>\mu k$,
and the growing wavelengths satisfy
$$\ell=\frac{2\pi}{\omega}>2\pi\sqrt{\frac{\mu D}{\chi a_{eq}f-\mu k}}=\ell_c$$
[@sayama_complex]. With Sayama's values and, say, $\chi=3\times10^{-4}$, $\ell_c\approx0.044$ (our
arithmetic).

**The reaction–diffusion shortcut.** For $\partial f_i/\partial t=R_i(f)+D_i\nabla^2 f_i$, the same
sine-wave substitution gives
$$\frac{d\,\Delta f}{dt}=\big(J-D\,\omega^2\big)\Delta f,$$
where $J$ is the Jacobian of the reaction terms at the even state and $D$ the diagonal matrix of
diffusion constants [@sayama_complex]. Stability for every $\omega$ needs a positive determinant and a
negative trace (the [trace–determinant](phase-planes-and-limit-cycles.qmd) rule).

**The Turing condition.** For $J=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}$ stable on its own
($\det J>0$, $\operatorname{tr}J<0$), the trace of $J-D\omega^2$ stays negative, so only the determinant
can change sign. With $z=\omega^2$ it is negative when
$g(z)=-D_uD_vz^2+(aD_v+dD_u)z-\det J>0$ for some $z>0$. The intercept $-\det J$ is negative, so the
parabola's peak must sit at positive $z$ and rise above zero:
$$aD_v+dD_u>2\sqrt{D_uD_v\det J}$$
[@sayama_complex]. With $\rho=D_v/D_u$ this becomes $a\rho+d>2\sqrt{\rho\det J}$; for
$(1,-1,2,-1.5)$, $\rho-1.5>2\sqrt{0.5\rho}$, i.e. $\rho^2-5\rho+2.25>0$ with $\rho>1.5$, so $\rho>4.5$
[@sayama_complex].

**The fastest-growing wavelength.** Maximising the larger eigenvalue
$$\lambda_+=\tfrac12\Big[-(0.5+7\times10^{-4}\omega^2)+\sqrt{2.5\times10^{-7}\omega^4+2.5\times10^{-3}\omega^2-1.75}\Big]$$
over $z=\omega^2$ gives $z\approx3083$, $\omega\approx55.5$ and $\ell=2\pi/\omega\approx0.113$
[@sayama_complex]. Our numerical check gives the same peak, with growth rate $\lambda_+\approx0.114$ and an
unstable band of wavelengths from about $0.080$ to $0.171$.

:::
