Markets Without an Auctioneer

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source

Textbook general equilibrium needs someone to announce the price. Remove that someone, let neighbours haggle, and a price still emerges — but as a statistical cloud, with trade left on the table and identical people ending up unequal.

Give agents textbook preferences, let them barter only with whoever is standing next to them, and the average price drifts to the general-equilibrium level, but the market never quite gets there. Prices stay dispersed, the quantity traded falls short of what would clear the market, and agents who start out identical finish with different welfare. Epstein and Axtell grow this result on the Sugarscape and use it to question the welfare theorems behind the case for laissez-faire.

Why it matters

The textbook case for leaving markets alone runs in two steps. First, market processes produce an equilibrium price. Second, at that price, decentralised utility maximisation produces a Pareto-optimal allocation, one where nobody can be made better off without making someone worse off. Epstein and Axtell point out that if the first step fails, the second is left without support, and “the theoretical case for pure market solutions is weakened” (Epstein and Axtell 1996).

The theory that delivers step one, general equilibrium, has an awkward piece of machinery at its centre: a Walrasian auctioneer who collects everyone’s demands, computes the market-clearing price vector, and announces it, after which everyone trades at exactly that price. Epstein and Axtell call the image “quite unrealistic”. No person or institution could hold complete knowledge of preferences and endowments, or the computing power to find the prices. Even if someone did, why would every agent agree to be a price-taker (Epstein and Axtell 1996)?

For a social scientist the question generalises well beyond economics. Many aggregate quantities we measure — a market price, a going wage, a “norm” — are treated as a single number. This entry shows what it looks like when such a number is really the average of many local negotiations, and what that average hides.

The auctioneer versus the market square

Epstein and Axtell take their alternative picture from David Kreps’s microeconomics textbook. Instead of an auctioneer, imagine consumers wandering around a large market square with their possessions on their backs. When two meet, they look at what each has and try to arrange a mutually agreeable trade, then wander off looking for the next one (Epstein and Axtell 1996). “We implement trade in precisely this fashion,” they write. Nobody uses an auctioneer or any similar artifice (Epstein and Axtell 1996).

The move is deliberately conservative. Their agents are otherwise as neoclassical as possible: they live for ever, have fixed, well-behaved preferences, reveal those preferences truthfully, and trade only if a trade makes both of them better off. The only change from the textbook is that prices are set locally, by bargaining between neighbours, rather than by a central price system (Epstein and Axtell 1996). Whatever goes wrong is therefore down to decentralisation, not to irrational agents. (Economists will recognise a non-Walrasian, Edgeworth-style barter process. The authors note it is more decentralised than the usual version because prices differ across pairs within the same round (Epstein and Axtell 1996).)

Two goods and a welfare function

To trade you need two things, so the Sugarscape gets a second resource, spice, piled in two mountains opposite the two sugar mountains. Each agent now has a sugar metabolism and a spice metabolism, and dies if either stock runs out (Epstein and Axtell 1996).

How should an agent decide which good it needs more? Epstein and Axtell’s answer is grounded in survival. An agent holding w_1 sugar and w_2 spice, burning m_1 and m_2 per period, would starve of sugar in \tau_1 = w_1/m_1 periods and of spice in \tau_2 = w_2/m_2 periods if it gathered nothing more. Whichever clock is shorter is the good it should seek. A Cobb–Douglas welfare function with exponents set by the metabolisms captures exactly this, and makes preferences depend on the agent’s biology (Epstein and Axtell 1996).

The agent’s marginal rate of substitution (MRS) is how much spice it regards as worth one unit of sugar. For this welfare function it comes out as a strikingly simple ratio, the two starvation clocks:

\text{MRS} = \frac{\tau_2}{\tau_1} = \frac{w_2/m_2}{w_1/m_1}.

An agent with MRS below one is relatively short of spice, and one above one is short of sugar (Epstein and Axtell 1996).

Rule T: haggling between neighbours

When two neighbours meet, they compare MRSs. If they are equal, there is nothing to gain. Otherwise the agent who values sugar more (higher MRS) buys sugar and pays in spice. Whether a given agent is a buyer or a seller is not fixed; it depends on who it happens to meet (Epstein and Axtell 1996).

Any price between the two MRSs is acceptable to both. Following Albin and Foley, Epstein and Axtell settle on the geometric mean of the two, p=\sqrt{\text{MRS}_A\,\text{MRS}_B}, which treats “10 spice for 1 sugar” and “10 sugar for 1 spice” as equally far from parity. (They report that drawing a random price from the interval gives qualitatively the same results.) Rule T then runs as follows (Epstein and Axtell 1996):

  1. Both agents compute their MRS. If they are equal, stop.
  2. Spice flows from the higher-MRS agent to the lower; sugar flows the other way.
  3. The price is the geometric mean of the two MRSs.
  4. One unit changes hands: p spice for 1 sugar if p>1, or 1 spice for 1/p sugar if p<1.
  5. If the trade makes both agents better off and does not make their MRSs cross over, do it and go back to step 1. Otherwise stop.

The no-crossing condition stops a pair from flipping between buyer and seller for ever. In an Edgeworth box for two agents starting at (5, 8) and (15, 2), repeated application of T walks them step by step towards the contract curve and stops just short of it. Each pair therefore reaches a local, pairwise near-Pareto optimum (Epstein and Axtell 1996). The authors admit T is “hardly simple”, since it needs partial derivatives and square roots. They defend it as “the simplest local rule in the neoclassical spirit” (Epstein and Axtell 1996).

What emerges: a statistical equilibrium

Put 200 immortal agents on the sugar–spice landscape. Metabolisms and vision are uniform between 1 and 5, and initial endowments are uniform between 25 and 50 of each good. Because everything is symmetric, the general-equilibrium price of sugar in spice is about 1 (Epstein and Axtell 1996).

Over 1,000 periods the agents make nearly 150,000 trades. Prices start out widely scattered and then bunch around the market-clearing level of 1. The standard deviation of the log average price settles at about 0.05 by t=1000, and with infinitely lived agents it never reaches a stationary value but keeps creeping down. Trade volume per period is roughly lognormal, with a few big periods and many small ones (Epstein and Axtell 1996). Their verdict: a price equilibrium “is essentially attained” and “economic equilibrium emerges from the bottom up” (Epstein and Axtell 1996). In one sense this is a stronger result than general equilibrium theory gives, because the dynamics of price formation are fully accounted for and there is no mythical auctioneer (Epstein and Axtell 1996).

Trade also raises the carrying capacity. An agent rich in sugar but about to die of spice starvation, next to one in the opposite position, would both die without trade. A swap keeps both alive. More agents survive at every level of mean vision (Epstein and Axtell 1996).

But this is a statistical equilibrium, not a Walrasian one. In general equilibrium theory everyone trades at one announced price, in a single step. Here each trade happens at a locally negotiated price that depends on who your neighbour happens to be. Predicting the next trade price would need the state of the entire society, so it can only be done probabilistically. This is the “statistical equilibrium” that Duncan Foley had proposed (Epstein and Axtell 1996).

Local efficiency, global inefficiency

The headline result goes against intuition. Epstein and Axtell query every agent each period for how much sugar it would supply or demand at each price, sum these into supply and demand curves, and compare the market-clearing price and quantity with what actually happened. The actual price moves around the equilibrium price, but the actual quantity traded is always less than the market-clearing quantity (Epstein and Axtell 1996). Agents can only trade with neighbours, so there is always “pent-up” demand. If agents could reach beyond their neighbourhoods, everyone could be made better off.

The authors put it in one sentence: rule T produces exchanges that are nearly Pareto-optimal locally, “the resulting market has far from optimal welfare properties globally” (Epstein and Axtell 1996). Two consequences follow. First, “getting the price right” is not sufficient for allocative efficiency: the market is generating essentially the general-equilibrium price on average and still leaving gains unclaimed. Second, the average price that economists study may be “simply emergent statistically from micro-heterogeneity”, a number no agent uses (Epstein and Axtell 1996).

Why doesn’t the shortfall vanish with time? Because the economy keeps moving. Exchange takes time to converge, and meanwhile gathering and metabolism change everyone’s holdings, which moves the very equilibrium the trading is converging towards. Exchange that is slow relative to production and consumption is enough to keep the economy far from equilibrium permanently (Epstein and Axtell 1996). They connect this to Franklin Fisher’s point that the set of equilibria is path-dependent.

Horizontal inequality and the welfare theorems

General equilibrium has a strong fairness property built in: differences in final welfare always trace back to differences in initial endowments. Foley calls the violation of this horizontal inequality: agents with identical abilities, preferences and endowments end up at different welfare levels simply because they traded at different prices. It cannot happen in Walrasian equilibrium, and it does happen in statistical equilibrium (Epstein and Axtell 1996).

That undermines the First Welfare Theorem, the “invisible hand” result that Walrasian equilibria are Pareto-efficient. Following Foley, Epstein and Axtell argue that in statistical equilibrium the theorem should say that a market approximates but cannot achieve a Pareto-efficient allocation, and that price dispersion measures how far it falls short. The Second Welfare Theorem needs a similar amendment. Horizontal inequality, they expect, rises with price dispersion (Epstein and Axtell 1996). Their conclusion: “the philosophical underpinning for laissez-faire policies appears to be weak” for such markets. It also opens a role for regulation the textbook rules out: when trade happens at many prices, Foley argues that price floors and ceilings can protect agents from the worst deals and reduce the horizontal inequality the market itself creates (Epstein and Axtell 1996).

Thinner markets and less neoclassical agents

Price dispersion is the dial. Turn it up and every problem above gets worse. Epstein and Axtell show several ways to turn it (Epstein and Axtell 1996):

Change to the model What happens to prices
Vision fixed at 1 (agents barely move, a “thin” market) Average over roughly 100,000 trades is still 1.0, but “nothing like the ‘law of one price’ obtains”. Dispersion is of the same magnitude as econometric studies of real price dispersion, even though this is not a search model
Vision uniform on 1–15 (agents mix widely) Equilibrium approached quickly. The authors liken it to information-rich financial markets
Finite lives, max age uniform on 60–100 Newborns arrive with valuations unrelated to the market’s, keeping dispersion high, “at considerable variance with Walrasian general equilibrium”
Long lives, max age uniform on 980–1020 Dispersion falls: more time for each agent’s valuations to fall into line
Evolving preferences (cultural transmission changes the welfare exponents) The mean price follows “a kind of random walk”; variance never settles; volume rises about fivefold, since agents keep finding they hold goods they no longer value
Sex and culture together “So much variation in price that equilibrium seems lost forever”

Two further results matter for inequality research. With finite lives, trade makes the wealth distribution more unequal (a higher Gini coefficient) even as it supports more agents, a trade-off the agent-based modelling entry follows into demography. And if agents may lend to fund children, with loans of 10 periods at 10% under the credit rule, whole hierarchies of lenders, borrowers and agents who are both emerge, up to five levels deep (Epstein and Axtell 1996).

Markets as computation

Epstein and Axtell end with a reframing. The auctioneer is essentially an algorithm for computing prices, and an odd one for a theory of decentralisation, because it needs centralised information: everyone must report their demands to it. Real markets have no auctioneer. Prices emerge from interactions that run in parallel and asynchronously, like nodes in a large computer whose wiring keeps changing (Epstein and Axtell 1996). That raises questions general equilibrium theory cannot pose: under what conditions does the computation end in a market-clearing price, and how would any agent know it had ended? They suggest viewing trade not as a social computation of prices but as “a distributed algorithm for the production of agent welfare”, whose performance depends on the trading rule and the network of who meets whom (Epstein and Axtell 1996).

Seeing it

The figure strips the model to its exchange core: no landscape, no gathering, no metabolism, just 400 agents with Sugarscape-style Cobb–Douglas preferences trading under rule T until no profitable trade is left. Sixty of them are clones: identical preferences and identical endowments. In one market agents trade only with their four fixed lattice neighbours (local); in the other they meet four random partners each round (well mixed). Because this economy is pure exchange, the Walrasian price and allocation can be computed exactly and used as the benchmark.

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def bilateral_market(local, seed=3, side=20, n_clone=60, rounds=12):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    n = side * side
    m1 = rng.integers(1, 6, n).astype(float)          # sugar metabolism
    m2 = rng.integers(1, 6, n).astype(float)          # spice metabolism
    w1 = rng.uniform(25, 50, n)                       # sugar holdings
    w2 = rng.uniform(25, 50, n)                       # spice holdings
    clones = rng.choice(n, n_clone, replace=False)    # identical agents
    m1[clones], m2[clones], w1[clones], w2[clones] = 1.0, 4.0, 45.0, 30.0
    a = m1 / (m1 + m2)                                # Cobb-Douglas exponent on sugar

    # Walrasian benchmark for this pure-exchange economy (closed form)
    pW = np.sum(a * w2) / np.sum((1 - a) * w1)
    wealth = pW * w1 + w2
    welfare_W = (a * wealth / pW) ** a * ((1 - a) * wealth) ** (1 - a)

    U = lambda i, s, c: s ** a[i] * c ** (1 - a[i])
    MRS = lambda i, s, c: (a[i] / (1 - a[i])) * c / s
    grid = np.arange(n).reshape(side, side)
    nbrs = np.array([[grid[(r + dr) % side, (c + dc) % side]
                      for dr, dc in ((1, 0), (-1, 0), (0, 1), (0, -1))]
                     for r in range(side) for c in range(side)])
    others = np.arange(n)

    for _ in range(rounds):
        for i in rng.permutation(n):
            partners = nbrs[i] if local else rng.choice(others[others != i], 4, replace=False)
            for j in rng.permutation(partners):
                while True:                            # rule T, one unit at a time
                    mi, mj = MRS(i, w1[i], w2[i]), MRS(j, w1[j], w2[j])
                    if abs(mi - mj) < 1e-12:
                        break
                    A, B = (i, j) if mi > mj else (j, i)   # A buys sugar, pays spice
                    p = np.sqrt(mi * mj)                    # geometric-mean price
                    ds, dc = (1.0, p) if p > 1 else (1.0 / p, 1.0)
                    if w2[A] <= dc or w1[B] <= ds:
                        break
                    better = (U(A, w1[A] + ds, w2[A] - dc) > U(A, w1[A], w2[A]) and
                              U(B, w1[B] - ds, w2[B] + dc) > U(B, w1[B], w2[B]))
                    no_cross = MRS(A, w1[A] + ds, w2[A] - dc) >= MRS(B, w1[B] - ds, w2[B] + dc)
                    if not (better and no_cross):
                        break
                    w1[A] += ds; w2[A] -= dc; w1[B] -= ds; w2[B] += dc

    final_mrs = (a / (1 - a)) * w2 / w1
    welfare = U(np.arange(n), w1, w2)
    return dict(pW=pW, mrs=final_mrs, clone_w=welfare[clones], clone_W=welfare_W[clones][0])

loc, mix = bilateral_market(True), bilateral_market(False)

The left panel is the “statistical equilibrium”: valuations cluster near the Walrasian price, but a Walrasian market would put every one of them exactly on the line. The spread that remains is unclaimed gains from trade, and it is several times larger when the market is thin. The right panel is horizontal inequality in miniature. Every clone gains from trading, yet the clones end up spread over a wide range, and most finish below the welfare the textbook equilibrium would have given every one of them. The only difference between them is whom they happened to meet, in what order, and at what prices.

Common pitfalls

WarningWhere this goes wrong

  • “The average price converged, so the market is in equilibrium.” The mean can sit on the general-equilibrium price while quantities fall short of market-clearing and welfare is left unclaimed. Getting the price right is not sufficient for allocative efficiency (Epstein and Axtell 1996).

  • Reading the average as the price. In a decentralised market there is no single price. With vision 1 the average over about 100,000 trades was 1.0, yet “nothing like the ‘law of one price’” held (Epstein and Axtell 1996). Report the dispersion, not just the mean.

  • Blaming irrationality. Epstein and Axtell’s agents are textbook-rational, truthful and immortal. The shortfall comes from locality alone. Adding realism (finite lives, changing tastes) makes it worse, not better (Epstein and Axtell 1996).

  • Treating equal starting points as a guarantee of equal outcomes. In Walrasian equilibrium they are; in a market of bilateral trades they are not. Identical agents can diverge through the prices they happen to get (Epstein and Axtell 1996).

  • Over-reading the policy conclusion. The result weakens the theoretical case for laissez-faire and opens room for regulation such as price floors and ceilings. It does not show that any particular intervention helps. Epstein and Axtell describe their own evidence as mixed on whether equilibrium prices and optimal allocations are achievable (Epstein and Axtell 1996).

Appendix · the mathematics

The welfare function and its MRS. With metabolisms m_1,m_2 and m_T=m_1+m_2, the Sugarscape welfare function is the Cobb–Douglas W(w_1,w_2)=w_1^{\,m_1/m_T}\,w_2^{\,m_2/m_T}. Differentiating, \text{MRS}=\frac{\partial W/\partial w_1}{\partial W/\partial w_2} =\frac{(m_1/m_T)\,W/w_1}{(m_2/m_T)\,W/w_2}=\frac{m_1 w_2}{m_2 w_1}=\frac{w_2/m_2}{w_1/m_1}=\frac{\tau_2}{\tau_1}. Buying sugar (raising w_1, lowering w_2) lowers an agent’s MRS; selling sugar raises it. That is why repeated trade between a high-MRS buyer and a low-MRS seller pushes their MRSs together, and why the no-crossing rule is the natural place to stop.

Why the price lies between the MRSs, and why the geometric mean. Agent A (higher MRS) would give up to \text{MRS}_A spice for a unit of sugar; B needs at least \text{MRS}_B. Any price in [\text{MRS}_B,\text{MRS}_A] benefits both at the margin. In logs, \pi=\ln p, the geometric mean p=\sqrt{\text{MRS}_A\text{MRS}_B} is the midpoint \pi=\tfrac12(\ln\text{MRS}_A+\ln\text{MRS}_B), which treats a price of 10 and a price of 1/10 as equally far from parity. This is why Epstein and Axtell report statistics of \ln p.

The Walrasian benchmark in the figure. Write \alpha_i=m_{1i}/m_{Ti} and let p be the price of sugar in spice. Agent i with endowment (e_{1i},e_{2i}) has wealth p\,e_{1i}+e_{2i} in spice units and, with Cobb–Douglas preferences, spends a share \alpha_i on sugar: x_{1i}=\alpha_i(p\,e_{1i}+e_{2i})/p. Market clearing in sugar, \sum_i x_{1i}=\sum_i e_{1i}, gives p^{*}=\frac{\sum_i \alpha_i\,e_{2i}}{\sum_i (1-\alpha_i)\,e_{1i}} . At p^* every agent’s MRS equals p^*. That is the spike the figure’s left panel measures departures from. Clones share \alpha_i and e_i, so all reach the same Walrasian welfare.

Why horizontal inequality breaks the First Welfare Theorem’s logic. The theorem’s proof uses one price vector for everyone: each agent’s chosen bundle is the best it can afford at that price, so any bundle someone strictly prefers must cost more than they have, and summing these inequalities rules out a Pareto improvement. With bilateral trade, agent i’s sequence of trades happens at prices p_{i1},p_{i2},\dots that differ across agents. The single budget constraint is gone, and identical agents facing different price sequences reach different bundles. Foley’s revision uses the dispersion of transaction prices as a measure of how far the allocation falls short of efficiency.

*Give agents textbook preferences, let them barter only with whoever is standing next to them, and the
average price drifts to the general-equilibrium level, but the market never quite gets there.* Prices stay
dispersed, the quantity traded falls short of what would clear the market, and agents who start out
identical finish with different welfare. Epstein and Axtell grow this result on the Sugarscape and use it
to question the welfare theorems behind the case for laissez-faire.

## Why it matters

The textbook case for leaving markets alone runs in two steps. First, market processes produce an
**equilibrium price**. Second, at that price, decentralised utility maximisation produces a **Pareto-optimal**
allocation, one where nobody can be made better off without making someone worse off. Epstein and Axtell
point out that if the first step fails, the second is left without support, and "the theoretical case
for pure market solutions is weakened" [@epstein_axtell].

The theory that delivers step one, general equilibrium, has an awkward piece of machinery at its centre: a
**Walrasian auctioneer** who collects everyone's demands, computes the market-clearing price vector, and
announces it, after which everyone trades at exactly that price. Epstein and Axtell call the image "quite
unrealistic". No person or institution could hold complete knowledge of preferences and endowments, or the
computing power to find the prices. Even if someone did, why would every agent agree to be a price-taker
[@epstein_axtell]?

For a social scientist the question generalises well beyond economics. Many aggregate quantities we
measure — a market price, a going wage, a "norm" — are treated as a single number. This entry shows what
it looks like when such a number is really the average of many local negotiations, and what that average
hides.

## The auctioneer versus the market square

Epstein and Axtell take their alternative picture from David Kreps's microeconomics textbook. Instead of an
auctioneer, imagine consumers wandering around a large market square with their possessions on their
backs. When two meet, they look at what each has and try to arrange a mutually agreeable trade, then
wander off looking for the next one [@epstein_axtell]. "We implement trade in precisely this fashion," they
write. Nobody uses an auctioneer or any similar artifice [@epstein_axtell].

The move is deliberately conservative. Their agents are otherwise **as neoclassical as possible**: they
live for ever, have fixed, well-behaved preferences, reveal those preferences truthfully, and trade only if
a trade makes both of them better off. The *only* change from the textbook is that prices are set **locally**,
by bargaining between neighbours, rather than by a central price system [@epstein_axtell]. Whatever goes
wrong is therefore down to decentralisation, not to irrational agents. (Economists will recognise a
non-Walrasian, Edgeworth-style barter process. The authors note it is more decentralised than the usual
version because prices differ across pairs within the same round [@epstein_axtell].)

## Two goods and a welfare function

To trade you need two things, so the Sugarscape gets a second resource, **spice**, piled in two mountains
opposite the two sugar mountains. Each agent now has a sugar metabolism and a spice metabolism, and dies
if *either* stock runs out [@epstein_axtell].

How should an agent decide which good it needs more? Epstein and Axtell's answer is grounded in survival.
An agent holding $w_1$ sugar and $w_2$ spice, burning $m_1$ and $m_2$ per period, would starve of sugar in
$\tau_1 = w_1/m_1$ periods and of spice in $\tau_2 = w_2/m_2$ periods if it gathered nothing more. Whichever
clock is shorter is the good it should seek. A Cobb–Douglas **welfare function** with exponents set by
the metabolisms captures exactly this, and makes preferences depend on the agent's biology [@epstein_axtell].

The agent's **marginal rate of substitution** (MRS) is how much spice it regards as worth one unit of
sugar. For this welfare function it comes out as a strikingly simple ratio, the two starvation clocks:

$$
\text{MRS} = \frac{\tau_2}{\tau_1} = \frac{w_2/m_2}{w_1/m_1}.
$$

An agent with MRS below one is relatively short of spice, and one above one is short of sugar
[@epstein_axtell].

## Rule T: haggling between neighbours

When two neighbours meet, they compare MRSs. If they are equal, there is nothing to gain. Otherwise the
agent who values sugar more (higher MRS) buys sugar and pays in spice. Whether a given agent is a buyer or
a seller is not fixed; it depends on who it happens to meet [@epstein_axtell].

Any price between the two MRSs is acceptable to both. Following Albin and Foley, Epstein and Axtell settle
on the **geometric mean** of the two, $p=\sqrt{\text{MRS}_A\,\text{MRS}_B}$, which treats "10 spice for 1
sugar" and "10 sugar for 1 spice" as equally far from parity. (They report that drawing a random price from
the interval gives qualitatively the same results.) Rule **T** then runs as follows [@epstein_axtell]:

1. Both agents compute their MRS. If they are equal, stop.
2. Spice flows from the higher-MRS agent to the lower; sugar flows the other way.
3. The price is the geometric mean of the two MRSs.
4. One unit changes hands: $p$ spice for 1 sugar if $p>1$, or 1 spice for $1/p$ sugar if $p<1$.
5. If the trade makes **both** agents better off and does **not** make their MRSs cross over, do it and go
   back to step 1. Otherwise stop.

The no-crossing condition stops a pair from flipping between buyer and seller for ever. In an Edgeworth box
for two agents starting at (5, 8) and (15, 2), repeated application of T walks them step by step towards
the contract curve and stops just short of it. Each pair therefore reaches a *local*, pairwise near-Pareto
optimum [@epstein_axtell]. The authors admit T is "hardly simple", since it needs partial derivatives and
square roots. They defend it as "the simplest local rule in the neoclassical spirit" [@epstein_axtell].

## What emerges: a statistical equilibrium

Put **200** immortal agents on the sugar–spice landscape. Metabolisms and vision are uniform between 1 and 5,
and initial endowments are uniform between 25 and 50 of each good. Because everything is symmetric, the
general-equilibrium price of sugar in spice is about **1** [@epstein_axtell].

Over 1,000 periods the agents make nearly **150,000 trades**. Prices start out widely scattered and then
bunch around the market-clearing level of 1. The standard deviation of the log average price settles at
about **0.05** by $t=1000$, and with infinitely lived agents it never reaches a stationary value but keeps
creeping down. Trade volume per period is roughly lognormal, with a few big periods and many small ones
[@epstein_axtell]. Their verdict: a price equilibrium "is essentially attained" and "economic equilibrium
emerges from the bottom up" [@epstein_axtell]. In one sense this is a stronger result than general
equilibrium theory gives, because the dynamics of price formation are fully accounted for and there is no
mythical auctioneer [@epstein_axtell].

Trade also **raises the carrying capacity**. An agent rich in sugar but about to die of spice starvation,
next to one in the opposite position, would both die without trade. A swap keeps both alive. More agents
survive at every level of mean vision [@epstein_axtell].

But this is a **statistical** equilibrium, not a Walrasian one. In general equilibrium theory everyone trades
at one announced price, in a single step. Here each trade happens at a locally negotiated price that
depends on who your neighbour happens to be. Predicting the next trade price would need the state of the
entire society, so it can only be done probabilistically. This is the "statistical equilibrium" that Duncan
Foley had proposed [@epstein_axtell].

## Local efficiency, global inefficiency

The headline result goes against intuition. Epstein and Axtell query every agent each period for how much
sugar it would supply or demand at each price, sum these into supply and demand curves, and compare the
market-clearing price and quantity with what actually happened. The **actual price moves around the
equilibrium price**, but the **actual quantity traded is always less** than the market-clearing quantity
[@epstein_axtell]. Agents can only trade with neighbours, so there is always "pent-up" demand. If agents
could reach beyond their neighbourhoods, everyone could be made better off.

The authors put it in one sentence: rule T produces exchanges that are nearly Pareto-optimal *locally*,
"the resulting market has far from optimal welfare properties globally" [@epstein_axtell]. Two
consequences follow. First, "getting the price right" is **not sufficient** for allocative efficiency: the
market is generating essentially the general-equilibrium price on average and still leaving gains unclaimed.
Second, the average price that economists study may be "simply emergent statistically from
micro-heterogeneity", a number no agent uses [@epstein_axtell].

Why doesn't the shortfall vanish with time? Because the economy keeps moving. Exchange takes time to
converge, and meanwhile gathering and metabolism change everyone's holdings, which moves the very
equilibrium the trading is converging towards. Exchange that is slow relative to production and consumption
is enough to keep the economy **far from equilibrium** permanently [@epstein_axtell]. They connect this to
Franklin Fisher's point that the set of equilibria is path-dependent.

## Horizontal inequality and the welfare theorems

General equilibrium has a strong fairness property built in: differences in final welfare always trace back
to differences in initial endowments. Foley calls the violation of this **horizontal inequality**: agents
with identical abilities, preferences and endowments end up at different welfare levels simply because they
traded at different prices. It cannot happen in Walrasian equilibrium, and it does happen in statistical
equilibrium [@epstein_axtell].

That undermines the **First Welfare Theorem**, the "invisible hand" result that Walrasian equilibria are
Pareto-efficient. Following Foley, Epstein and Axtell argue that in statistical equilibrium the theorem
should say that a market *approximates but cannot achieve* a Pareto-efficient allocation, and that **price
dispersion** measures how far it falls short. The Second Welfare Theorem needs a similar amendment.
Horizontal inequality, they expect, rises with price dispersion [@epstein_axtell]. Their conclusion:
"the philosophical underpinning for laissez-faire policies appears to be weak" for such markets. It also
opens a role for regulation the textbook rules out: when trade happens at many prices, Foley argues that
price floors and ceilings can protect agents from the worst deals and reduce the horizontal inequality the
market itself creates [@epstein_axtell].

## Thinner markets and less neoclassical agents

Price dispersion is the dial. Turn it up and every problem above gets worse. Epstein and Axtell show
several ways to turn it [@epstein_axtell]:

| Change to the model | What happens to prices |
|---|---|
| **Vision fixed at 1** (agents barely move, a "thin" market) | Average over roughly 100,000 trades is still 1.0, but "nothing like the 'law of one price' obtains". Dispersion is of the same magnitude as econometric studies of real price dispersion, even though this is not a search model |
| **Vision uniform on 1–15** (agents mix widely) | Equilibrium approached quickly. The authors liken it to information-rich financial markets |
| **Finite lives**, max age uniform on 60–100 | Newborns arrive with valuations unrelated to the market's, keeping dispersion high, "at considerable variance with Walrasian general equilibrium" |
| **Long lives**, max age uniform on 980–1020 | Dispersion falls: more time for each agent's valuations to fall into line |
| **Evolving preferences** (cultural transmission changes the welfare exponents) | The mean price follows "a kind of random walk"; variance never settles; volume rises about fivefold, since agents keep finding they hold goods they no longer value |
| **Sex and culture together** | "So much variation in price that equilibrium seems lost forever" |

Two further results matter for inequality research. With finite lives, **trade makes the wealth distribution
more unequal** (a higher Gini coefficient) even as it supports more agents, a trade-off the
[agent-based modelling](agent-based-modelling.qmd) entry follows into demography. And if agents may
**lend** to fund children, with loans of 10 periods at 10% under the credit rule, whole hierarchies of lenders, borrowers and agents who
are both emerge, up to five levels deep [@epstein_axtell].

## Markets as computation

Epstein and Axtell end with a reframing. The auctioneer is essentially an **algorithm for computing prices**,
and an odd one for a theory of decentralisation, because it needs **centralised information**: everyone must
report their demands to it. Real markets have no auctioneer. Prices emerge from interactions that run in
parallel and asynchronously, like nodes in a large computer whose wiring keeps changing [@epstein_axtell].
That raises questions general equilibrium theory cannot pose: under what conditions does the computation
end in a market-clearing price, and how would any agent know it had ended? They suggest viewing trade not
as a social computation of prices but as "a distributed algorithm for the production of agent welfare",
whose performance depends on the trading rule and the network of who meets whom [@epstein_axtell].

## Seeing it

The figure strips the model to its exchange core: no landscape, no gathering, no metabolism, just 400
agents with Sugarscape-style Cobb–Douglas preferences trading under rule T until no profitable trade is left.
Sixty of them are **clones**: identical preferences and identical endowments. In one market agents trade
only with their four fixed lattice neighbours (local); in the other they meet four random partners each
round (well mixed). Because this economy is pure exchange, the Walrasian price and allocation can be computed
exactly and used as the benchmark.

```{python}
#| label: fig-markets-without-auctioneer
#| fig-cap: "Rule T without an auctioneer (a stripped-down, pure-exchange re-implementation; numbers are this simulation's, not the book's). Left: once trading stops, a Walrasian equilibrium would give every agent the same marginal rate of substitution, equal to the market-clearing price (orange line). Bilateral trade leaves a spread of internal valuations around it, a much wider spread when agents can only trade with fixed neighbours (ink) than when they mix (grey), so profitable trades remain that nobody can reach. Right: sixty clones with identical preferences and endowments. Under Walrasian equilibrium they would all reach the same welfare (orange line). After bilateral trade each lands somewhere different, and most land below it: horizontal inequality created by the market itself."
#| code-fold: true
#| code-summary: "Show the figure code"

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def bilateral_market(local, seed=3, side=20, n_clone=60, rounds=12):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    n = side * side
    m1 = rng.integers(1, 6, n).astype(float)          # sugar metabolism
    m2 = rng.integers(1, 6, n).astype(float)          # spice metabolism
    w1 = rng.uniform(25, 50, n)                       # sugar holdings
    w2 = rng.uniform(25, 50, n)                       # spice holdings
    clones = rng.choice(n, n_clone, replace=False)    # identical agents
    m1[clones], m2[clones], w1[clones], w2[clones] = 1.0, 4.0, 45.0, 30.0
    a = m1 / (m1 + m2)                                # Cobb-Douglas exponent on sugar

    # Walrasian benchmark for this pure-exchange economy (closed form)
    pW = np.sum(a * w2) / np.sum((1 - a) * w1)
    wealth = pW * w1 + w2
    welfare_W = (a * wealth / pW) ** a * ((1 - a) * wealth) ** (1 - a)

    U = lambda i, s, c: s ** a[i] * c ** (1 - a[i])
    MRS = lambda i, s, c: (a[i] / (1 - a[i])) * c / s
    grid = np.arange(n).reshape(side, side)
    nbrs = np.array([[grid[(r + dr) % side, (c + dc) % side]
                      for dr, dc in ((1, 0), (-1, 0), (0, 1), (0, -1))]
                     for r in range(side) for c in range(side)])
    others = np.arange(n)

    for _ in range(rounds):
        for i in rng.permutation(n):
            partners = nbrs[i] if local else rng.choice(others[others != i], 4, replace=False)
            for j in rng.permutation(partners):
                while True:                            # rule T, one unit at a time
                    mi, mj = MRS(i, w1[i], w2[i]), MRS(j, w1[j], w2[j])
                    if abs(mi - mj) < 1e-12:
                        break
                    A, B = (i, j) if mi > mj else (j, i)   # A buys sugar, pays spice
                    p = np.sqrt(mi * mj)                    # geometric-mean price
                    ds, dc = (1.0, p) if p > 1 else (1.0 / p, 1.0)
                    if w2[A] <= dc or w1[B] <= ds:
                        break
                    better = (U(A, w1[A] + ds, w2[A] - dc) > U(A, w1[A], w2[A]) and
                              U(B, w1[B] - ds, w2[B] + dc) > U(B, w1[B], w2[B]))
                    no_cross = MRS(A, w1[A] + ds, w2[A] - dc) >= MRS(B, w1[B] - ds, w2[B] + dc)
                    if not (better and no_cross):
                        break
                    w1[A] += ds; w2[A] -= dc; w1[B] -= ds; w2[B] += dc

    final_mrs = (a / (1 - a)) * w2 / w1
    welfare = U(np.arange(n), w1, w2)
    return dict(pW=pW, mrs=final_mrs, clone_w=welfare[clones], clone_W=welfare_W[clones][0])

loc, mix = bilateral_market(True), bilateral_market(False)

fig, (ax1, ax2) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(9.6, 4.0))

bins = np.linspace(-0.6, 0.6, 41)
ax1.hist(np.log(mix["mrs"] / mix["pW"]), bins=bins, histtype="stepfilled", color="#b7b6a8",
         alpha=0.8, label=f"well mixed (sd {np.std(np.log(mix['mrs'])):.3f})")
ax1.hist(np.log(loc["mrs"] / loc["pW"]), bins=bins, histtype="step", color="#161613", lw=1.6,
         label=f"local only (sd {np.std(np.log(loc['mrs'])):.3f})")
ax1.axvline(0, color="#e8531d", lw=2)
ax1.text(0.02, ax1.get_ylim()[1] * 0.93, "Walrasian:\nevery MRS here", color="#e8531d", fontsize=8, va="top")
ax1.set_xlabel("log( final MRS / Walrasian price )")
ax1.set_ylabel("agents")
ax1.set_title("Valuations never fully converge")
ax1.legend(fontsize=8, frameon=False, loc="upper left")

cbins = np.linspace(30, 52, 23)
ax2.hist(mix["clone_w"], bins=cbins, histtype="stepfilled", color="#b7b6a8", alpha=0.8, label="well mixed")
ax2.hist(loc["clone_w"], bins=cbins, histtype="step", color="#161613", lw=1.6, label="local only")
ax2.axvline(loc["clone_W"], color="#e8531d", lw=2)
ax2.text(loc["clone_W"] + 0.3, ax2.get_ylim()[1] * 0.93, "Walrasian\nwelfare", color="#e8531d", fontsize=8, va="top")
ax2.axvline(45 ** 0.2 * 30 ** 0.8, color="#8a8a7e", ls="--", lw=1.2)
ax2.text(45 ** 0.2 * 30 ** 0.8 - 0.3, ax2.get_ylim()[1] * 0.93, "before\ntrade", color="#8a8a7e",
         fontsize=8, va="top", ha="right")
ax2.set_xlabel("final welfare of 60 identical clones")
ax2.set_title("Identical agents, unequal outcomes")
ax2.legend(fontsize=8, frameon=False, loc="upper right")

fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

The left panel is the "statistical equilibrium": valuations cluster near the Walrasian price, but a

difference between them is whom they happened to meet, in what order, and at what prices.

## Common pitfalls

::: {.callout-warning title="Where this goes wrong"}

- **"The average price converged, so the market is in equilibrium."** The mean can sit on the
  general-equilibrium price while quantities fall short of market-clearing and welfare is left unclaimed.
  Getting the price right is not sufficient for allocative efficiency [@epstein_axtell].

- **Reading the average as the price.** In a decentralised market there is no single price. With vision 1
  the average over about 100,000 trades was 1.0, yet "nothing like the 'law of one price'" held
  [@epstein_axtell]. Report the dispersion, not just the mean.

- **Blaming irrationality.** Epstein and Axtell's agents are textbook-rational, truthful and immortal. The
  shortfall comes from locality alone. Adding realism (finite lives, changing tastes) makes it worse, not
  better [@epstein_axtell].

- **Treating equal starting points as a guarantee of equal outcomes.** In Walrasian equilibrium they are;
  in a market of bilateral trades they are not. Identical agents can diverge through the prices they happen
  to get [@epstein_axtell].

- **Over-reading the policy conclusion.** The result weakens the *theoretical* case for laissez-faire and
  opens room for regulation such as price floors and ceilings. It does not show that any particular
  intervention helps. Epstein and Axtell describe their own evidence as mixed on whether equilibrium
  prices and optimal allocations are achievable [@epstein_axtell].

:::

## Appendix · the mathematics {.unnumbered}

::: {.math-appendix}

**The welfare function and its MRS.** With metabolisms $m_1,m_2$ and $m_T=m_1+m_2$, the Sugarscape welfare
function is the Cobb–Douglas
$$
W(w_1,w_2)=w_1^{\,m_1/m_T}\,w_2^{\,m_2/m_T}.
$$
Differentiating,
$$
\text{MRS}=\frac{\partial W/\partial w_1}{\partial W/\partial w_2}
=\frac{(m_1/m_T)\,W/w_1}{(m_2/m_T)\,W/w_2}=\frac{m_1 w_2}{m_2 w_1}=\frac{w_2/m_2}{w_1/m_1}=\frac{\tau_2}{\tau_1}.
$$
Buying sugar (raising $w_1$, lowering $w_2$) lowers an agent's MRS; selling sugar raises it. That is why
repeated trade between a high-MRS buyer and a low-MRS seller pushes their MRSs together, and why the
no-crossing rule is the natural place to stop.

**Why the price lies between the MRSs, and why the geometric mean.** Agent $A$ (higher MRS) would give up to
$\text{MRS}_A$ spice for a unit of sugar; $B$ needs at least $\text{MRS}_B$. Any price in
$[\text{MRS}_B,\text{MRS}_A]$ benefits both at the margin. In logs, $\pi=\ln p$, the geometric mean
$p=\sqrt{\text{MRS}_A\text{MRS}_B}$ is the midpoint $\pi=\tfrac12(\ln\text{MRS}_A+\ln\text{MRS}_B)$, which
treats a price of 10 and a price of 1/10 as equally far from parity. This is why Epstein and Axtell report
statistics of $\ln p$.

**The Walrasian benchmark in the figure.** Write $\alpha_i=m_{1i}/m_{Ti}$ and let $p$ be the price of sugar
in spice. Agent $i$ with endowment $(e_{1i},e_{2i})$ has wealth $p\,e_{1i}+e_{2i}$ in spice units and, with
Cobb–Douglas preferences, spends a share $\alpha_i$ on sugar: $x_{1i}=\alpha_i(p\,e_{1i}+e_{2i})/p$.
Market clearing in sugar, $\sum_i x_{1i}=\sum_i e_{1i}$, gives
$$
p^{*}=\frac{\sum_i \alpha_i\,e_{2i}}{\sum_i (1-\alpha_i)\,e_{1i}} .
$$
At $p^*$ every agent's MRS equals $p^*$. That is the spike the figure's left panel measures departures
from. Clones share $\alpha_i$ and $e_i$, so all reach the same Walrasian welfare.

**Why horizontal inequality breaks the First Welfare Theorem's logic.** The theorem's proof uses one price
vector for everyone: each agent's chosen bundle is the best it can afford at that price, so any bundle
someone strictly prefers must cost more than they have, and summing these inequalities rules out a Pareto
improvement. With bilateral trade, agent $i$'s sequence of trades happens at prices $p_{i1},p_{i2},\dots$
that differ across agents. The single budget constraint is gone, and identical agents facing different price
sequences reach different bundles. Foley's revision uses the dispersion of transaction prices as a measure
of how far the allocation falls short of efficiency.

:::
