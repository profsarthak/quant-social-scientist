Complex Adaptive Systems

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source

Markets, immune systems, ecologies, and economies share a deep structure: many agents, each adapting to the others, with no one in charge. The whole learns even though no part intends to.

A market has no manager, yet it allocates resources. An immune system has no commander, yet it learns to recognise new pathogens. An ant colony has no architect, yet it builds. These are complex adaptive systems (CAS): large populations of agents that adapt — changing their behaviour in response to feedback from the environment and from each other — with no central control. Out of this local adaptation, coherent and intelligent-looking macro behaviour emerges that no single agent designed or could predict. The system as a whole adapts, even though adaptation lives only in the parts.

Why it matters

Most of the systems social scientists actually care about — economies, organisations, cultures, ecosystems — are complex adaptive systems, and they break the assumptions of the tidy models we reach for first. Their agents are heterogeneous, not identical; they learn and change strategy, so yesterday’s regularity dissolves; and the “fitness” of a strategy depends on what everyone else is doing, so the ground keeps moving (Miller and Page 2007). CAS thinking gives you the right default expectations — perpetual novelty, adaptation, emergent order — and warns you off the wrong ones (a fixed equilibrium reached by a representative rational agent).

What makes a system complex and adaptive

John Holland’s definition: a CAS is a large network of heterogeneous agents interacting without central control, each adapting its behaviour based on feedback to do better in its environment (Mitchell 2009; Miller and Page 2007). From that local adaptation, emergent macro behaviour arises — global patterns not present in, and not predictable from, any individual part (Miller and Page 2007). Holland highlighted recurring ingredients: aggregation (agents combine into higher-level structures), nonlinearity (the whole is not the sum), flows of resources and information through networks, and diversity (a continuously renewed variety of agent types). The defining move beyond a merely complex system is adaptation: the agents are not fixed automata but learners.

Fitness landscapes: a map for adaptation

The dominant metaphor for adaptation is the fitness landscape (Mitchell 2009). Picture every possible strategy laid out on a plain, with height = fitness (payoff, reproductive success, profit). Adaptation becomes hill-climbing: an agent tweaks its strategy and moves toward higher ground.

The shape of the landscape decides how hard this is:

  • A smooth landscape has a single peak — easy. Keep climbing and you arrive at the global best.
  • A rugged landscape — created by nonlinear interactions among the parts of a strategy — has many peaks and valleys (Mitchell 2009). Now hill-climbing is treacherous: you can climb diligently and still get stuck on a local optimum, a hill that is higher than everything nearby but far short of the true summit.

This is the exploration–exploitation trade-off in its purest form: exploit the good peak you’re on, or explore — risk going downhill — in search of a higher one (Mitchell 2009). Every adaptive agent, from a firm choosing a strategy to an algorithm fitting a model, lives on this knife-edge.

Genetic algorithms: evolution as a search strategy

Holland turned the metaphor into a method. A genetic algorithm (GA) searches a rugged landscape the way evolution does — with a population, not a lone climber, so many regions are explored at once (Mitchell 2009). The recipe:

  1. Population. Start with a set of random candidate solutions, each encoded as a string (a “genome”).
  2. Selection. Evaluate each candidate’s fitness; pick “parents” with probability rising in fitness (fitness-proportional selection) — the better solutions reproduce more.
  3. Crossover. Pair parents and recombine their genomes — swap segments — so good pieces from different solutions can combine.
  4. Mutation. Randomly flip a few bits, injecting the diversity that lets the search escape local optima.
  5. Repeat over many generations; the population marches uphill.

The figure runs a real genetic algorithm, by hand, on a deliberately rugged landscape.

Show the figure code 
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(3)

# rugged fitness landscape on [0,1]: one global + two local peaks (+ ripples)
def fitness_x(x):
    return (np.exp(-30 * (x - 0.70) ** 2)          # global peak
            + 0.65 * np.exp(-40 * (x - 0.30) ** 2)  # local peak
            + 0.50 * np.exp(-70 * (x - 0.48) ** 2)  # local peak
            + 0.03 * np.sin(40 * x) ** 2)

L = 16                      # bits per genome
P = 40                      # population size
G = 40                      # generations
pc, pm = 0.8, 0.02          # crossover, per-bit mutation rates
denom = 2 ** L - 1

def decode(pop):            # binary rows -> x in [0,1]
    weights = 2 ** np.arange(L)[::-1]
    return (pop @ weights) / denom

pop = rng.integers(0, 2, size=(P, L))
gen0_x = decode(pop)
best_hist, mean_hist = [], []

for g in range(G):
    fit = fitness_x(decode(pop))
    best_hist.append(fit.max()); mean_hist.append(fit.mean())
    # fitness-proportional (roulette) selection of parents
    probs = fit / fit.sum()
    idx = rng.choice(P, size=(P, 2), p=probs)
    new = np.empty_like(pop)
    for k, (a, b) in enumerate(idx):
        child = pop[a].copy()
        if rng.random() < pc:                       # single-point crossover
            cut = rng.integers(1, L)
            child[cut:] = pop[b, cut:]
        flip = rng.random(L) < pm                   # mutation
        child[flip] ^= 1
        new[k] = child
    # elitism: carry the best solution forward unchanged
    new[0] = pop[fit.argmax()]
    pop = new

genG_x = decode(pop)

fig, (axL, axR) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(9.4, 4.3))

grid = np.linspace(0, 1, 800)
axL.plot(grid, fitness_x(grid), color="#b7b6a8", lw=1.5, label="fitness landscape")
axL.scatter(gen0_x, fitness_x(gen0_x) + 0.02, color="#8a8a7e", s=18, zorder=3, label="generation 0")
axL.scatter(genG_x, fitness_x(genG_x) - 0.02, color="#161613", s=18, zorder=3, label="generation 40")
axL.axvline(0.70, color="#e8531d", ls="--", lw=0.8)
axL.text(0.70, 0.05, " global peak", color="#e8531d", fontsize=8)
axL.set_xlabel("strategy  x"); axL.set_ylabel("fitness")
axL.set_title("A population crossing the valleys")
axL.legend(fontsize=8, frameon=False, loc="upper left")

axR.plot(best_hist, color="#161613", lw=2, label="best fitness")
axR.plot(mean_hist, color="#8a8a7e", lw=2, label="mean fitness")
axR.set_xlabel("generation"); axR.set_ylabel("fitness")
axR.set_title("Adaptation over generations")
axR.legend(fontsize=8, frameon=False, loc="lower right")

fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()

Co-evolution: the landscape that fights back

The deepest twist is that in a real CAS, fitness is endogenous — how well your strategy does depends on the strategies of everyone else (Miller and Page 2007). Predators adapt to prey, which adapt right back; firms react to competitors who react in turn. So the landscape is not a fixed terrain you climb but a co-evolving surface that deforms as you and everyone else move — a Red Queen world where you must keep adapting just to hold your position. This is why CAS rarely settle into a static equilibrium and instead show perpetual novelty, arms races, and waves of innovation.

When the average agent misleads

Miller and Page open their book with a standing ovation. A textbook model gives each audience member a noisy signal of the performance’s quality, s_i = q + \epsilon_i. A person stands if the signal clears a threshold T, and everyone else rises once more than a share \alpha is already standing. That model predicts two outcomes only: the first group stays alone, or the whole house stands after two rounds. Real ovations come in waves and form spatial patterns. To get those, put people in seats and let them come with friends. Then signals are no longer independent, influence depends on who can see whom, and “identical” people behave differently because of where they sit. The people in the front have the most visible influence and the least information. Those at the back have the most information and the least influence. (“Think of the former as celebrities and the latter as academics.”) (Miller and Page 2007)

The general lesson is about heterogeneity. Sometimes differences cancel out. One bee in a swarm flies erratically, but the swarm’s centre moves predictably, so a single “representative bee” is a good model. Often, though, differences interact, and then the average agent is a poor guide to the whole. (Miller and Page 2007) Two beehive examples make the point.

  • Keeping the hive warm. A hive must stay within a narrow temperature band. Cold bees huddle and buzz; hot bees spread out and fan. Each bee’s huddling and fanning thresholds are genetically linked. In a genetically uniform hive, every bee crosses its threshold at once. They all huddle, the hive overheats, and they all scatter: “like a house with a primitive thermostat”. In a diverse hive, a few bees react first and more join only if the temperature keeps moving. Swings stay small. (Fischer, 2004, as reported by Miller and Page.) (Miller and Page 2007)
  • Defending the hive. A hundred bees each join an attack once R_i others have joined. R guard bees start it. If every bee has R_i = 50.5, all 100 attack when R>50 and none do otherwise. If the thresholds are R_i = i, running from 1 to 100, a single guard bee sets off the whole hive. The bee with threshold 1 joins, which triggers the bee with threshold 2, and so on. The average threshold is 50.5 in both hives, yet one is hard to rouse and the other is on a hair trigger. The model simplifies Granovetter’s (1978) and Lohmann’s (1993) models of riots. Miller and Page say its application to bees is “more speculative”. (Miller and Page 2007)

In one hive heterogeneity brings stability; in the other it brings instability. The difference is the sign of the feedback. When one bee acts to cool the hive, other bees become less likely to act: negative feedback. When one bee attacks, others become more likely to: positive feedback. (Miller and Page 2007) The same diversity steadies a negative-feedback system and destabilises a positive-feedback one. (The threshold cascade reappears on networks as complex contagion.)

Voting with your feet: noise as annealing

Miller and Page’s second opening model is a tiny version of Tiebout’s (1956) idea that citizens sort themselves among towns by moving. There are two towns, each with three citizens. Each town must choose red or green chile for its annual picnic, and each town has two green-lovers and one red-lover. Under majority rule both towns serve green. Nobody gains by moving, so this is an equilibrium, but the two red-lovers can never win because they are a minority everywhere. (Miller and Page 2007)

The way out is noise. Suppose one citizen moves at random. Whoever moves, one town is left solidly green and the other is split evenly. The split town may then switch to red. Once it does, everyone re-sorts: four green-lovers in one town, two red-lovers in the other, and everyone gets their favourite. This arrangement is also robust, because no single move changes either town’s majority. A gentler rule works too: let citizens move when moving leaves them no worse off. Biologists call such changes neutral mutations. They change nothing immediately but open up new possibilities. (Miller and Page 2007) The model shows a social system locked into an inferior equilibrium, and noise or a looser behavioural rule releasing it.

The computational version (Kollman, Miller and Page, 1997) has many towns and citizens and eleven binary issues. Each citizen has random weights on the issues. The towns choose platforms through different institutions, and citizens move to the town they like best. (Miller and Page 2007)

  • With one town, nobody can move, so all that matters is how well the institution matches its residents. Issue-by-issue democratic referenda do best, because they settle at once on the median voter’s position on each issue. Next come two parties in direct competition, then multiparty proportional representation, then more than two parties in direct competition.
  • With many towns, the ranking completely reverses, and referenda come last. Referenda freeze each town’s platform within a few rounds, so citizens stop moving before they have sorted. Party competition keeps platforms shifting early on, which sends citizens migrating, and only later settles. (Miller and Page 2007)

The institutions that work best share a property. They add noise when a town’s residents disagree, and remove it as the residents become alike. A mixed town gets platforms that jump around, which pushes people to move and can set off resorting across many towns. A sorted town settles near its residents’ median position. Miller and Page call this a “natural” annealing process. The name comes from metalworking: heat the metal to jostle its atoms, then cool it slowly so they settle into a better structure. Kirkpatrick, Gelatt and Vecchi (1983) turned the idea into simulated annealing, an optimisation method. (Miller and Page 2007) In Murphy’s account, simulated annealing accepts a worse move with a probability that falls as the “temperature” drops. Early on, the search can leave a local optimum. Later, it settles. (Murphy 2012)

This connects directly to the fitness landscape above. Noise is how a system explores a rugged landscape, and “cooling” is when it switches to exploiting what it has found. Miller and Page note that this runs against habit: noise is usually treated as something that knocks a system away from good equilibria, not as the way to reach them. (Miller and Page 2007) Their later chapters make the same point: allowing mistakes, “especially if they are not too costly or occur early in the search process”, helps systems escape poor outcomes. “Less perfection is often more.” (Miller and Page 2007)

Common pitfalls

WarningWhere this goes wrong
  • Assuming a single equilibrium. A CAS with co-evolving, adapting agents typically has no fixed resting point; modelling it as one representative agent at equilibrium throws away its essential behaviour. (Miller and Page 2007)
  • Forgetting local optima. Pure exploitation (greedy hill-climbing) gets stuck; without exploration — mutation, search, experimentation — a system fossilises on a mediocre peak. (Mitchell 2009)
  • Designing for control. There is no central controller to program; you shape a CAS through incentives, feedback, and the rules of interaction, not by issuing commands. (Miller and Page 2007)
  • Calibrating to the average agent. Two hives with the same mean threshold (50.5) behave in opposite ways: one needs more than 50 guard bees to start an attack, the other needs one. When differences interact through feedback, model the distribution, not its mean. (Miller and Page 2007)
  • Assuming heterogeneity always stabilises (or always destabilises). Diversity steadies a negative-feedback system (hive temperature) and puts a positive-feedback one on a hair trigger (hive defence). Ask which kind of feedback links the agents before predicting the effect. (Miller and Page 2007)
  • Treating every equilibrium as good, and all noise as harmful. In the two-town chile model, everyone staying put is an equilibrium in which the red-lovers can never win. A random move breaks the symmetry. Institutions that add noise to mixed towns and remove it from sorted ones did best when citizens could move. (Miller and Page 2007)
  • Ranking institutions in a world without exit. Referenda were the best institution with one town and the worst with many. Judging a mechanism without the sorting it causes can reverse the verdict. (Miller and Page 2007)
  • Reading GAs as optimisers with guarantees. A genetic algorithm is a robust heuristic search of a rugged space, not a method that provably returns the global optimum. (Mitchell 2009)

Appendix · the mathematics

Hill-climbing and gradients. On a smooth landscape f, local adaptation is gradient ascent x_{t+1}=x_t+\eta\,\nabla f(x_t), which converges to a local maximum (where \nabla f=0 and the Hessian is negative-definite). On a rugged landscape there are many such points, and which one you reach depends entirely on the start — hence the global-vs-local problem.

Why a population helps. A single climber samples one basin of attraction. A population of P climbers samples up to P basins in parallel; crossover then recombines partial solutions across basins — a move no local gradient step can make. This is the intuition behind Holland’s schema theorem: short, high-fitness building blocks (schemas) receive exponentially increasing trials over generations, so a GA implicitly searches many strategy-combinations at once (“implicit parallelism”).

Threshold cascades as a fixed point. Let N agents have thresholds R_1,\dots,R_N and let R initiators act first. If n_k agents have joined after round k, then n_{k+1}=\#\{i: R_i\le R+n_k\}, starting from n_0=0. The cascade stops at the first fixed point n^*=\#\{i:R_i\le R+n^*\}. For R_i\equiv 50.5 the count jumps from 0 to N, so n^*=0 for R\le50 and n^*=100 for R\ge51. For R_i=i, \#\{i:R_i\le m\}=\min(m,100), so n_{k+1}=\min(R+n_k,100) climbs to 100 from any R\ge1. The mean of the thresholds does not appear anywhere; the whole distribution does. (Miller and Page 2007)

Simulated annealing. To minimise an “energy” f(x), propose x' near the current x_k and accept it with probability \min(1,\alpha), where \alpha=\exp\{(f(x_k)-f(x'))/T\}. Downhill moves are always accepted. Uphill moves are accepted sometimes, and less often as the temperature T is lowered. As T\to 0 the chain concentrates on the minimum of the Boltzmann distribution p(x)\propto e^{-f(x)/T}. (Murphy 2012) The Tiebout institutions do this without a schedule: a mixed town has a high “temperature” and a sorted one a low “temperature”. (Miller and Page 2007)

NK landscapes. Kauffman’s NK model tunes ruggedness with one knob: N genes, each contributing to fitness depending on K others. K=0 gives a single smooth peak; raising K increases the epistatic interactions and the number of local optima, interpolating from Mount-Fuji-smooth to fully rugged. It is the standard formalisation of “how hard is this adaptive search.”

::: {.lead-kernel}
A market has no manager, yet it allocates resources. An immune system has no commander, yet it learns to recognise new pathogens. An ant colony has no architect, yet it builds. These are **complex adaptive systems (CAS)**: large populations of **agents** that *adapt* — changing their behaviour in response to feedback from the environment and from each other — with **no central control**. Out of this local adaptation, coherent and intelligent-looking **macro behaviour emerges** that no single agent designed or could predict. The system as a whole *adapts*, even though adaptation lives only in the parts.
:::

## Why it matters

Most of the systems social scientists actually care about — economies, organisations, cultures, ecosystems — are complex adaptive systems, and they break the assumptions of the tidy models we reach for first. Their agents are **heterogeneous**, not identical; they **learn and change strategy**, so yesterday's regularity dissolves; and the "fitness" of a strategy depends on what everyone *else* is doing, so the ground keeps moving [@millerpage_cas]. CAS thinking gives you the right default expectations — perpetual novelty, adaptation, emergent order — and warns you off the wrong ones (a fixed equilibrium reached by a representative rational agent).

## What makes a system complex *and* adaptive

John Holland's definition: a CAS is a large network of **heterogeneous agents** interacting **without central control**, each **adapting** its behaviour based on feedback to do better in its environment [@mitchell_complexity; @millerpage_cas]. From that local adaptation, **emergent macro behaviour** arises — global patterns not present in, and not predictable from, any individual part [@millerpage_cas]. Holland highlighted recurring ingredients: **aggregation** (agents combine into higher-level structures), **nonlinearity** (the whole is not the sum), **flows** of resources and information through networks, and **diversity** (a continuously renewed variety of agent types). The defining move beyond a merely *complex* system is **adaptation**: the agents are not fixed automata but learners.

## Fitness landscapes: a map for adaptation {#sec-fitness-landscapes}

The dominant metaphor for adaptation is the **fitness landscape** [@mitchell_complexity]. Picture every possible strategy laid out on a plain, with **height = fitness** (payoff, reproductive success, profit). Adaptation becomes **hill-climbing**: an agent tweaks its strategy and moves toward higher ground.

The shape of the landscape decides how hard this is:

- A **smooth** landscape has a single peak — easy. Keep climbing and you arrive at the global best.
- A **rugged** landscape — created by nonlinear interactions among the parts of a strategy — has **many peaks and valleys** [@mitchell_complexity]. Now hill-climbing is treacherous: you can climb diligently and still get **stuck on a local optimum**, a hill that is higher than everything nearby but far short of the true summit.

This is the **exploration–exploitation trade-off** in its purest form: **exploit** the good peak you're on, or **explore** — risk going downhill — in search of a higher one [@mitchell_complexity]. Every adaptive agent, from a firm choosing a strategy to an algorithm fitting a model, lives on this knife-edge.

## Genetic algorithms: evolution as a search strategy

Holland turned the metaphor into a method. A **genetic algorithm (GA)** searches a rugged landscape the way evolution does — with a **population**, not a lone climber, so many regions are explored at once [@mitchell_complexity]. The recipe:

1. **Population.** Start with a set of random candidate solutions, each encoded as a string (a "genome").
2. **Selection.** Evaluate each candidate's fitness; pick "parents" with probability rising in fitness (**fitness-proportional selection**) — the better solutions reproduce more.
3. **Crossover.** Pair parents and **recombine** their genomes — swap segments — so good pieces from different solutions can combine.
4. **Mutation.** Randomly flip a few bits, injecting the **diversity** that lets the search escape local optima.
5. **Repeat** over many generations; the population marches uphill.

The figure runs a real genetic algorithm, by hand, on a deliberately rugged landscape.

```{python}
#| label: fig-ga
#| fig-cap: "A genetic algorithm climbing a rugged fitness landscape, by hand. LEFT: the landscape (grey curve) has a tall GLOBAL peak near x≈0.70 plus two lower LOCAL peaks — exactly the trap that defeats a lone hill-climber. Generation 0 (olive points) is a random population scattered everywhere; after 40 generations of selection + crossover + mutation the population (ink points) has concentrated on the global peak (orange dashed line). RIGHT: best and mean fitness per generation. The population's best solution finds the global optimum within a handful of generations and the whole population follows — evolutionary search, not gradient following, is what lets it cross the valleys a hill-climber cannot."
#| code-fold: true
#| code-summary: "Show the figure code"

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(3)

# rugged fitness landscape on [0,1]: one global + two local peaks (+ ripples)
def fitness_x(x):
    return (np.exp(-30 * (x - 0.70) ** 2)          # global peak
            + 0.65 * np.exp(-40 * (x - 0.30) ** 2)  # local peak
            + 0.50 * np.exp(-70 * (x - 0.48) ** 2)  # local peak
            + 0.03 * np.sin(40 * x) ** 2)

L = 16                      # bits per genome
P = 40                      # population size
G = 40                      # generations
pc, pm = 0.8, 0.02          # crossover, per-bit mutation rates
denom = 2 ** L - 1

def decode(pop):            # binary rows -> x in [0,1]
    weights = 2 ** np.arange(L)[::-1]
    return (pop @ weights) / denom

pop = rng.integers(0, 2, size=(P, L))
gen0_x = decode(pop)
best_hist, mean_hist = [], []

for g in range(G):
    fit = fitness_x(decode(pop))
    best_hist.append(fit.max()); mean_hist.append(fit.mean())
    # fitness-proportional (roulette) selection of parents
    probs = fit / fit.sum()
    idx = rng.choice(P, size=(P, 2), p=probs)
    new = np.empty_like(pop)
    for k, (a, b) in enumerate(idx):
        child = pop[a].copy()
        if rng.random() < pc:                       # single-point crossover
            cut = rng.integers(1, L)
            child[cut:] = pop[b, cut:]
        flip = rng.random(L) < pm                   # mutation
        child[flip] ^= 1
        new[k] = child
    # elitism: carry the best solution forward unchanged
    new[0] = pop[fit.argmax()]
    pop = new

genG_x = decode(pop)

fig, (axL, axR) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(9.4, 4.3))

grid = np.linspace(0, 1, 800)
axL.plot(grid, fitness_x(grid), color="#b7b6a8", lw=1.5, label="fitness landscape")
axL.scatter(gen0_x, fitness_x(gen0_x) + 0.02, color="#8a8a7e", s=18, zorder=3, label="generation 0")
axL.scatter(genG_x, fitness_x(genG_x) - 0.02, color="#161613", s=18, zorder=3, label="generation 40")
axL.axvline(0.70, color="#e8531d", ls="--", lw=0.8)
axL.text(0.70, 0.05, " global peak", color="#e8531d", fontsize=8)
axL.set_xlabel("strategy  x"); axL.set_ylabel("fitness")
axL.set_title("A population crossing the valleys")
axL.legend(fontsize=8, frameon=False, loc="upper left")

axR.plot(best_hist, color="#161613", lw=2, label="best fitness")
axR.plot(mean_hist, color="#8a8a7e", lw=2, label="mean fitness")
axR.set_xlabel("generation"); axR.set_ylabel("fitness")
axR.set_title("Adaptation over generations")
axR.legend(fontsize=8, frameon=False, loc="lower right")

fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

## Co-evolution: the landscape that fights back

The deepest twist is that in a real CAS, **fitness is endogenous** — how well your strategy does depends on the strategies of everyone else [@millerpage_cas]. Predators adapt to prey, which adapt right back; firms react to competitors who react in turn. So the landscape is not a fixed terrain you climb but a **co-evolving surface that deforms as you and everyone else move** — a Red Queen world where you must keep adapting just to hold your position. This is why CAS rarely settle into a static equilibrium and instead show perpetual novelty, arms races, and waves of innovation.

## When the average agent misleads {#sec-average-misleads}

Miller and Page open their book with a standing ovation. A textbook model gives each audience member a
noisy signal of the performance's quality, $s_i = q + \epsilon_i$. A person stands if the signal clears
a threshold $T$, and everyone else rises once more than a share $\alpha$ is already standing. That model
predicts two outcomes only: the first group stays alone, or the whole house stands after two rounds. Real
ovations come in **waves** and form **spatial patterns**. To get those, put people in seats and let them
come with friends. Then signals are no longer independent, influence depends on who can see whom, and
"identical" people behave differently because of where they sit. The people in the front have the most
visible influence and the least information. Those at the back have the most information and the least
influence. ("Think of the former as celebrities and the latter as academics.") [@millerpage_cas]

The general lesson is about **heterogeneity**. Sometimes differences cancel out. One bee in a swarm flies
erratically, but the swarm's centre moves predictably, so a single "representative bee" is a good model.
Often, though, differences **interact**, and then the average agent is a poor guide to the whole.
[@millerpage_cas] Two beehive examples make the point.

- **Keeping the hive warm.** A hive must stay within a narrow temperature band. Cold bees huddle and buzz;
  hot bees spread out and fan. Each bee's huddling and fanning thresholds are genetically linked. In a
  genetically uniform hive, every bee crosses its threshold at once. They all huddle, the hive overheats,
  and they all scatter: "like a house with a primitive thermostat". In a diverse hive, a few bees react
  first and more join only if the temperature keeps moving. Swings stay small. (Fischer, 2004, as reported
  by Miller and Page.) [@millerpage_cas]
- **Defending the hive.** A hundred bees each join an attack once $R_i$ others have joined. $R$ guard
  bees start it. If every bee has $R_i = 50.5$, all 100 attack when $R>50$ and none do otherwise. If the
  thresholds are $R_i = i$, running from 1 to 100, **a single guard bee sets off the whole hive**. The bee
  with threshold 1 joins, which triggers the bee with threshold 2, and so on. The **average threshold is
  50.5 in both hives**, yet one is hard to rouse and the other is on a hair trigger. The model simplifies
  Granovetter's (1978) and Lohmann's (1993) models of riots. Miller and Page say its application to bees
  is "more speculative". [@millerpage_cas]

In one hive heterogeneity brings stability; in the other it brings instability. The difference is the sign
of the **feedback**. When one bee acts to cool the hive, other bees become *less* likely to act: negative
feedback. When one bee attacks, others become *more* likely to: positive feedback. [@millerpage_cas]
The same diversity steadies a negative-feedback system and destabilises a positive-feedback one. (The
threshold cascade reappears on networks as [complex contagion](spreading-on-networks.qmd).)

## Voting with your feet: noise as annealing {#sec-tiebout-annealing}

Miller and Page's second opening model is a tiny version of Tiebout's (1956) idea that citizens sort
themselves among towns by moving. There are two towns, each with three citizens. Each town must choose red
or green chile for its annual picnic, and each town has two green-lovers and one red-lover. Under majority
rule both towns serve green. Nobody gains by moving, so this is an **equilibrium**, but the two red-lovers
can never win because they are a minority everywhere. [@millerpage_cas]

The way out is **noise**. Suppose one citizen moves at random. Whoever moves, one town is left solidly
green and the other is split evenly. The split town may then switch to red. Once it does, everyone re-sorts:
four green-lovers in one town, two red-lovers in the other, and **everyone gets their favourite**. This
arrangement is also robust, because no single move changes either town's majority. A gentler rule works
too: let citizens move when moving leaves them *no worse off*. Biologists call such changes **neutral
mutations**. They change nothing immediately but open up new possibilities. [@millerpage_cas] The model
shows a social system **locked into an inferior equilibrium**, and noise or a looser behavioural rule
releasing it.

**The computational version** (Kollman, Miller and Page, 1997) has many towns and citizens and **eleven
binary issues**. Each citizen has random weights on the issues. The towns choose platforms through
different institutions, and citizens move to the town they like best. [@millerpage_cas]

- **With one town**, nobody can move, so all that matters is how well the institution matches its
  residents. Issue-by-issue **democratic referenda** do best, because they settle at once on the median
  voter's position on each issue. Next come two parties in direct competition, then multiparty proportional
  representation, then more than two parties in direct competition.
- **With many towns**, the ranking **completely reverses**, and referenda come *last*. Referenda freeze
  each town's platform within a few rounds, so citizens stop moving before they have sorted. Party
  competition keeps platforms shifting early on, which sends citizens migrating, and only later settles.
  [@millerpage_cas]

The institutions that work best share a property. They **add noise when a town's residents disagree, and
remove it as the residents become alike**. A mixed town gets platforms that jump around, which pushes
people to move and can set off resorting across many towns. A sorted town settles near its residents'
median position. Miller and Page call this a "natural" **annealing** process. The name comes from
metalworking: heat the metal to jostle its atoms, then cool it slowly so they settle into a better
structure. Kirkpatrick, Gelatt and Vecchi (1983) turned the idea into **simulated annealing**, an
optimisation method. [@millerpage_cas] In Murphy's account, simulated annealing accepts a worse move with
a probability that falls as the "temperature" drops. Early on, the search can leave a local optimum.
Later, it settles. [@murphy_ml]

This connects directly to the [fitness landscape](#sec-fitness-landscapes) above.
Noise is how a system explores a rugged landscape, and "cooling" is when it switches to exploiting what it
has found. Miller and Page note that this runs against habit: noise is usually treated as something that
knocks a system *away* from good equilibria, not as the way to reach them. [@millerpage_cas] Their later
chapters make the same point: allowing mistakes, "especially if they are not too costly or occur early in
the search process", helps systems escape poor outcomes. "Less perfection is often more." [@millerpage_cas]

## Common pitfalls

::: {.callout-warning title="Where this goes wrong"}
- **Assuming a single equilibrium.** A CAS with co-evolving, adapting agents typically has no fixed resting point; modelling it as one representative agent at equilibrium throws away its essential behaviour. [@millerpage_cas]
- **Forgetting local optima.** Pure exploitation (greedy hill-climbing) gets stuck; without exploration — mutation, search, experimentation — a system fossilises on a mediocre peak. [@mitchell_complexity]
- **Designing for control.** There is no central controller to program; you shape a CAS through incentives, feedback, and the rules of interaction, not by issuing commands. [@millerpage_cas]
- **Calibrating to the average agent.** Two hives with the same mean threshold (50.5) behave in opposite
  ways: one needs more than 50 guard bees to start an attack, the other needs one. When differences
  interact through feedback, model the distribution, not its mean. [@millerpage_cas]
- **Assuming heterogeneity always stabilises (or always destabilises).** Diversity steadies a
  negative-feedback system (hive temperature) and puts a positive-feedback one on a hair trigger (hive
  defence). Ask which kind of feedback links the agents before predicting the effect. [@millerpage_cas]
- **Treating every equilibrium as good, and all noise as harmful.** In the two-town chile model, everyone
  staying put is an equilibrium in which the red-lovers can never win. A random move breaks the
  symmetry. Institutions that add noise to mixed towns and remove it from sorted ones did best when
  citizens could move. [@millerpage_cas]
- **Ranking institutions in a world without exit.** Referenda were the best institution with one town and
  the worst with many. Judging a mechanism without the sorting it causes can reverse the verdict.
  [@millerpage_cas]
- **Reading GAs as optimisers with guarantees.** A genetic algorithm is a robust *heuristic* search of a rugged space, not a method that provably returns the global optimum. [@mitchell_complexity]
:::

## Appendix · the mathematics {.unnumbered}

::: {.math-appendix}

**Hill-climbing and gradients.** On a smooth landscape $f$, local adaptation is gradient ascent $x_{t+1}=x_t+\eta\,\nabla f(x_t)$, which converges to *a* local maximum (where $\nabla f=0$ and the Hessian is negative-definite). On a **rugged** landscape there are many such points, and which one you reach depends entirely on the start — hence the global-vs-local problem.

**Why a population helps.** A single climber samples one basin of attraction. A population of $P$ climbers samples up to $P$ basins in parallel; **crossover** then recombines partial solutions across basins — a move no local gradient step can make. This is the intuition behind Holland's **schema theorem**: short, high-fitness building blocks (schemas) receive exponentially increasing trials over generations, so a GA implicitly searches many strategy-combinations at once ("implicit parallelism").

**Threshold cascades as a fixed point.** Let $N$ agents have thresholds $R_1,\dots,R_N$ and let $R$
initiators act first. If $n_k$ agents have joined after round $k$, then
$n_{k+1}=\#\{i: R_i\le R+n_k\}$, starting from $n_0=0$. The cascade stops at the first fixed point
$n^*=\#\{i:R_i\le R+n^*\}$. For $R_i\equiv 50.5$ the count jumps from $0$ to $N$, so $n^*=0$ for $R\le50$
and $n^*=100$ for $R\ge51$. For $R_i=i$, $\#\{i:R_i\le m\}=\min(m,100)$, so $n_{k+1}=\min(R+n_k,100)$ climbs
to 100 from any $R\ge1$. The mean of the thresholds does not appear anywhere; the whole distribution does.
[@millerpage_cas]

**Simulated annealing.** To minimise an "energy" $f(x)$, propose $x'$ near the current $x_k$ and accept it
with probability $\min(1,\alpha)$, where $\alpha=\exp\{(f(x_k)-f(x'))/T\}$. Downhill moves are always
accepted. Uphill moves are accepted sometimes, and less often as the temperature $T$ is lowered. As
$T\to 0$ the chain concentrates on the minimum of the Boltzmann distribution $p(x)\propto e^{-f(x)/T}$.
[@murphy_ml] The Tiebout institutions do this without a schedule: a mixed town has a high "temperature"
and a sorted one a low "temperature". [@millerpage_cas]

**NK landscapes.** Kauffman's $NK$ model tunes ruggedness with one knob: $N$ genes, each contributing to fitness depending on $K$ others. $K=0$ gives a single smooth peak; raising $K$ increases the **epistatic** interactions and the number of local optima, interpolating from Mount-Fuji-smooth to fully rugged. It is the standard formalisation of "how hard is this adaptive search."

:::
