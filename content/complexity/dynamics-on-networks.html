Dynamics on Networks: Consensus, Synchrony & Co-evolution

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source

A network is not just a wiring diagram. Opinions, money, rhythms and diseases move along its links, and the links themselves are rewired by what moves along them. One matrix, the graph Laplacian, tells you how fast a network can agree.

Put something on a network and let neighbours pull each other together, and the wiring decides how fast the whole network agrees. A single number, the spectral gap of the graph Laplacian, sets that speed, and it is small whenever the network has a bottleneck. Let the ties also respond to what flows along them, cutting links to the sick or to people who disagree, and you get adaptive networks, where the network can end up split by the very process that should have brought it together.

Why it matters

Most of the network entries so far describe structure: degrees, paths, hubs, communities. But the questions social scientists actually ask are usually about processes running on that structure. Will a divided organisation converge on one culture after a merger? Will a rumour, a norm or a price signal spread evenly or stall at a boundary? Will traders, fireflies or neurons fall into step? And do people’s ties shift in response to what they learn, so that the network you measured last year no longer exists?

Sayama sorts the models into three classes (Sayama 2015):

Class What changes Examples
Dynamics on networks Node states; the wiring is fixed Opinion spread, epidemics, diffusion of culture or money, coupled oscillators
Dynamics of networks The wiring itself: nodes and links added, dropped or rewired Watts–Strogatz rewiring, Barabási–Albert growth, triadic closure
Adaptive networks Both, each reacting to the other on the same time scale People cutting ties to infected friends; homophily rewiring a network while influence moves opinions

The second class already has homes here: small worlds and scale-free growth. This entry covers the first and third, and the one piece of mathematics that unifies them.

Dynamics on a fixed network: three ways to agree

Majority rule and the voter model

The simplest opinion model gives everyone a binary state and lets each person adopt the majority view of their ego network (themselves plus their neighbours), all at once. Run it on a real friendship network and sometimes everyone converges, and sometimes the network freezes into two camps with a stable boundary between them (Sayama 2015).

The voter model from statistical physics updates one pair at a time: a “listener” copies a “speaker” it is linked to, until consensus. How the pair is drawn matters (Sayama 2015):

  • Pull (pick a listener, then one of its neighbours to speak) tends to pick high-degree nodes as speakers. This is the friendship paradox: a random neighbour is more popular than a random node. Popular people get more airtime, so consensus is faster.
  • Push (pick a speaker, then a neighbour to listen) gives everyone an equal chance to speak but makes hubs the frequent listeners, so homogenisation is much slower.
  • Edge-based (pick a link, then assign the roles at random) sits in between.

Sayama states these as predictions, to be checked by Monte Carlo runs of time-to-consensus (Sayama 2015). The lesson holds anyway: who gets to talk, not just who is connected, shapes the outcome.

Diffusion: neighbours averaging each other

Make the state continuous: an amount of “stuff” at each node, such as money in a city, a firm’s cultural norm, or a population in a habitat. Let it flow across each link in proportion to the difference across that link (Sayama 2015):

\frac{dc_i}{dt}=\alpha\sum_{j\in N_i}(c_j-c_i).

Each node gains if its neighbours have more and loses if they have less. Stacked into a vector this is \dot{\mathbf c}=-\alpha L\mathbf c, where L=D-A is the graph Laplacian: the degree matrix minus the adjacency matrix. Its recipe is the same as the Laplacian of a continuous space: measure the difference between each neighbour and yourself, and add the differences up. The only catch is a historical sign flip (\nabla^2\Leftrightarrow -L) (Sayama 2015).

Diffusion conserves the total, so in a connected network every node ends at the average of the starting values. The interesting question is how fast.

Coupled oscillators: falling into step

Now let each node have its own rhythm, with a preferred frequency \omega_i that differs slightly from node to node, and let it nudge its phase toward its neighbours’. Kuramoto’s model replaces the difference \theta_j-\theta_i with \sin(\theta_j-\theta_i) (Sayama 2015). Phases live on a circle: two marching soldiers who are far apart in angle (0 and 2\pi-0.1) are actually side by side on the track, and should pull toward each other there. With strong enough coupling the population self-organises into synchronised marching. The amount of synchrony is measured by the phase coherence r=\big|\tfrac1n\sum_j e^{i\theta_j}\big|: 1 for perfect lockstep, near 0 for phases scattered evenly around the circle. Sayama’s examples are fireflies, neurons and traders in financial markets. Whether synchrony is good or bad depends on the context, and the question was part of what motivated Watts and Strogatz’s small-world paper (Sayama 2015).

One number for the speed of agreement: the spectral gap

All three processes are, near consensus, versions of the same linear dynamics, so the eigenvalues of L govern them. Sayama lists the Laplacian’s key properties for an undirected network (Sayama 2015):

  1. It always has an eigenvalue 0, with the all-ones vector as its eigenvector. Uniform states don’t move, which is consensus.
  2. All other eigenvalues are \ge 0.
  3. The number of zero eigenvalues equals the number of connected components. Each disconnected piece settles on its own average.
  4. The smallest non-zero eigenvalue, \lambda_2, is the spectral gap, or algebraic connectivity. It is the decay rate of the slowest-dying disagreement pattern, so it sets how fast diffusion finishes.

A small \lambda_2 means some pattern of disagreement dies out very slowly. That happens whenever the network has a bottleneck: two dense groups joined by a few bridges, or a long thin ring where information must travel step by step. On Zachary’s karate-club friendship network \lambda_2\approx 0.4685 (Sayama 2015). The number means little alone and only becomes informative when compared with other topologies, which is what the figure does.

Show the figure code (Laplacian diffusion + adaptive homophily, by hand) 
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
n, m = 40, 80

def laplacian(E, n):
    A = np.zeros((n, n))
    for i, j in E:
        A[i, j] = A[j, i] = 1
    return np.diag(A.sum(1)) - A, A

def connected(E, n):
    return np.linalg.eigvalsh(laplacian(E, n)[0])[1] > 1e-9

def ring(n, k=4):                      # each node linked to its k nearest neighbours
    return [(i, (i + d) % n) for i in range(n) for d in range(1, k // 2 + 1)]

def random_graph(n, m):
    while True:
        E = set()
        while len(E) < m:
            i, j = rng.integers(n, size=2)
            if i != j:
                E.add((min(i, j), max(i, j)))
        if connected(E, n):
            return sorted(E)

def two_groups(n, m, bridges=2):        # two dense halves joined by a couple of bridges
    h = n // 2
    while True:
        E = set()
        for off in (0, h):
            inside = set()
            while len(inside) < (m - bridges) // 2:
                i, j = rng.integers(h, size=2)
                if i != j:
                    inside.add((off + min(i, j), off + max(i, j)))
            E |= inside
        while len(E) < m:
            E.add((int(rng.integers(h)), int(h + rng.integers(h))))
        if connected(E, n):
            return sorted(E)

nets = {"random": random_graph(n, m), "ring": ring(n), "two groups + 2 bridges": two_groups(n, m)}
style = {"random": dict(color="#161613"), "ring": dict(color="#b7b6a8"),
         "two groups + 2 bridges": dict(color="#e8531d")}

fig, (ax1, ax2) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(10.4, 4.3))

# ---- Left: diffusion dc/dt = -L c, disagreement vs time ----
dt, T = 0.01, 25
c0 = np.random.default_rng(2).random(n)   # shared random starting states
t_axis = np.arange(0, T + dt / 2, dt)
for name, E in nets.items():
    L, _ = laplacian(E, n)
    lam2 = np.linalg.eigvalsh(L)[1]
    c, sd = c0.copy(), []
    for _ in t_axis:
        sd.append(c.std())
        c = c - (L @ c) * dt
    sd = np.array(sd)
    ax1.plot(t_axis, sd, lw=2.2, label=f"{name}  (λ₂ = {lam2:.2f})", **style[name])
    late = t_axis >= 8                  # slope e^(-λ₂ t), pinned to where the run ends
    ax1.plot(t_axis[late], 3 * sd[-1] * np.exp(lam2 * (T - t_axis[late])), ls="--", lw=0.9,
             **style[name])
ax1.set_yscale("log")
ax1.set_ylim(1e-9, 0.6)
ax1.set_xlabel("time")
ax1.set_ylabel("disagreement (sd of node states)")
ax1.set_title("Same density, different speed of agreement")
ax1.legend(frameon=False, fontsize=8.5, loc="lower left")

# ---- Right: adaptive diffusion with homophily (two factions) ----
E = nets["two groups + 2 bridges"]
_, A = laplacian(E, n)
h = n // 2
cross = np.zeros_like(A, dtype=bool)
cross[:h, h:] = cross[h:, :h] = True
alpha, beta = 1.0, 3.0
t_axis = np.arange(0, 20 + dt / 2, dt)
for gamma, col in ((1.0, "#161613"), (3.0, "#e8531d")):
    c = np.r_[np.ones(h), np.zeros(h)]
    W = 0.5 * A
    gap, wx = [], []
    for _ in t_axis:
        gap.append(c[:h].mean() - c[h:].mean())
        wx.append(W[cross & (A > 0)].mean())
        diff = c[None, :] - c[:, None]                  # c_j - c_i
        dc = alpha * (W * diff).sum(1)
        dW = beta * W * (1 - W) * (1 - gamma * np.abs(diff)) * A
        c, W = c + dc * dt, W + dW * dt
    ax2.plot(t_axis, gap, color=col, lw=2.2, label=f"opinion gap, γ = {gamma:g}")
    ax2.plot(t_axis, wx, color=col, lw=1.3, ls=":", label=f"cross-group tie weight, γ = {gamma:g}")
ax2.set_ylim(-0.03, 1.05)
ax2.set_xlabel("time")
ax2.set_ylabel("gap between group means / mean tie weight")
ax2.set_title("Contagion vs homophily: assimilate or split")
ax2.legend(frameon=False, fontsize=8, loc="center right")

fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()

Synchronisability: when does lockstep survive?

The spectral gap also decides whether coupled oscillators can stay synchronised. Sayama linearises around the synchronised trajectory and gets a clean condition (Sayama 2015): every non-zero Laplacian eigenvalue must make coupling beat the nodes’ own tendency to drift apart. When a node’s output rises with its state, only the smallest one, \lambda_2, matters. His worked example has oscillators that each accelerate at rate \beta while coupled at strength \alpha. They synchronise if and only if \lambda_2 > \beta/\alpha. On the karate-club network (\lambda_2\approx0.4685), \alpha=2,\ \beta=1 gives \beta/\alpha=0.5, so synchrony fails: the nodes draw together at first, then scatter as they speed up. Lowering \beta to 0.9 (\beta/\alpha=0.45) makes synchrony stable. A small parameter change flips the global behaviour, and a few lines of linear algebra predicted it exactly (Sayama 2015).

Adaptive networks: when ties respond to what flows along them

In real social systems the network does not sit still while opinions or diseases move across it. Sayama argues that state–topology co-evolution is one of the central open problems in network research, especially as temporal network data pile up. He also flags that he is one of the field’s promoters, so “take what I say with a pinch of salt” (Sayama 2015). His examples include epidemics, where contacts change through quarantine and avoidance, and social communities, where opinions, wealth and membership change along with who talks to whom.

Adaptive epidemics. Add one rule to the SIS model of spreading on networks: a susceptible person who finds that a neighbour is infected severs that tie with probability p_s. (This is inspired by Gross and colleagues’ 2006 model, in which the tie is rewired to another susceptible person rather than cut.) With parameters that would otherwise cause a pandemic (p_i=0.5,\ p_r=0.2) and p_s=0.5, the disease first sweeps the network. Avoidance then thins the links until density falls below the critical level at which the disease can survive, and it dies out (Sayama 2015). Behaviour changed the network, and the new network changed the epidemic.

Contagion vs homophily. Sociologists have long argued over two forces: social contagion (people become like their neighbours) and homophily (people connect to those already like them). The adaptive diffusion model puts both in one system (Sayama 2015). Opinions diffuse along weighted ties, and each tie’s weight grows or shrinks with the opinion difference across it, at a rate set by an intolerance parameter \gamma. With \gamma=0, every tie strengthens to full weight. With large \gamma, only nearly identical neighbours stay connected. Sayama built it from his work with Junichi Yamanoi on corporate mergers: when do two merged firms’ cultures blend into one identity? He seeds it with the factions of Zachary’s karate club, which split over the price of lessons, the instructor Mr. Hi on one side and the club’s officers on the other. With \alpha=1,\ \beta=3,\ \gamma=3 the cross-faction ties wither, the within-faction ties strengthen, and the club splits, as the real club did. Some opinion diffusion still crosses the divide before the ties go (Sayama 2015). The right panel of the figure runs the same equations on a stylised two-group network. Tolerance keeps the bridges and the groups converge; intolerance cuts them and the gap freezes.

The social-science payoff is sobering. Fragmentation can be produced by the same dynamics that would otherwise produce consensus. A cross-sectional snapshot of a polarised network cannot tell you whether people sorted by opinion or were persuaded by their neighbours. This is the selection-vs-influence problem, now written as a dynamical system.

Common pitfalls

WarningWhere this goes wrong
  • Equating density with connectedness. The three networks in the figure have the same number of links, yet one agrees five times faster. A bottleneck, not a shortage of ties, is what slows diffusion, and \lambda_2 detects it where the mean degree cannot.
  • Reading \lambda_2 in isolation. The karate club’s 0.4685 means little on its own; spectral gaps are informative when you compare topologies of similar size and density (Sayama 2015).
  • Assuming the network is fixed. If ties respond to states (quarantine, unfriending, sorting), a static-network model will mispredict both the epidemic and the opinion distribution. Ask whether the wiring changes on the same time scale as the process.
  • Reading the end state as the mechanism. A split network is consistent with pure homophily, pure influence plus a bottleneck, or the co-evolution of both. The end state does not identify the process; you need repeated observations of both ties and states over time.
  • Ignoring the update rule. In the voter model, whether the listener or the speaker is drawn first changes the consensus time, through the friendship paradox (Sayama 2015). “Agents copy neighbours” is not a complete model specification.
  • Treating synchrony as automatically good. Synchronised traders or neurons can be a pathology; the model tells you when lockstep is stable, not whether you want it.

Appendix · the mathematics

Diffusion as a linear system. With adjacency matrix A and degree matrix D,

\frac{dc_i}{dt}=\alpha\sum_j A_{ij}(c_j-c_i)=\alpha\Big(\sum_j A_{ij}c_j-k_ic_i\Big) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \dot{\mathbf c}=-\alpha L\mathbf c,\qquad L=D-A.

The figure uses the Euler step \mathbf c(t+\Delta t)=(I-\alpha L\Delta t)\,\mathbf c(t).

Conservation. \mathbf 1^\top L=\mathbf 0^\top (each column of L sums to zero), so \frac{d}{dt}\mathbf 1^\top\mathbf c=-\alpha\,\mathbf 1^\top L\mathbf c=0. The total is constant, and in a connected network the consensus value is the starting mean.

Spectral solution. L is symmetric and positive semi-definite (L=M^\top M with M the signed incidence matrix), so it has orthonormal eigenvectors \mathbf v_k and eigenvalues 0=\lambda_1\le\lambda_2\le\dots\le\lambda_n. Expanding the initial state,

\mathbf c(t)=\sum_{k=1}^n a_k\,e^{-\alpha\lambda_k t}\,\mathbf v_k, \qquad a_k=\mathbf v_k^\top\mathbf c(0).

The k=1 term is the uniform mean. Every other mode decays, and the slowest is e^{-\alpha\lambda_2t}, so disagreement eventually shrinks at rate \alpha\lambda_2: the dashed lines in the figure. The multiplicity of the zero eigenvalue equals the number of connected components, since each component’s indicator vector is in the null space.

Synchronisability. For \dot x_i=R(x_i)-\alpha\sum_j L_{ij}H(x_j), perturb the synchronised trajectory: x_i=x_s(t)+\Delta x_i. Linearising, and using L\mathbf 1=\mathbf 0 to cancel the H(x_s) terms,

\frac{d\,\Delta\mathbf x}{dt}=\big(R'(x_s)I-\alpha H'(x_s)L\big)\Delta\mathbf x ,

whose eigenvalues are R'(x_s)-\alpha\lambda_kH'(x_s). The \lambda_1=0 mode is motion along the synchronised trajectory. Every other mode must decay, which requires \alpha\lambda_kH'(x_s(t))>R'(x_s(t)) for k=2,\dots,n at all t. All eigenvalues lie between \lambda_2 and \lambda_n, so it suffices to check those two. If H'\ge0 only \lambda_2 matters. For R(\theta)=\beta\theta, H(\theta)=\theta: \lambda_2>\beta/\alpha.

Adaptive diffusion (Sayama’s Eqs. 16.18–16.19), with weights w_{ij}\in[0,1]:

\frac{dc_i}{dt}=\alpha\sum_{j\in N_i}(c_j-c_i)\,w_{ij},\qquad \frac{dw_{ij}}{dt}=\beta\,w_{ij}(1-w_{ij})\big(1-\gamma\,|c_i-c_j|\big).

The logistic factor w(1-w) keeps weights in [0,1]. A tie grows when |c_i-c_j|<1/\gamma and decays otherwise. With opposing factions at 0 and 1, \gamma\le1 never cuts a tie, while \gamma>1 cuts every cross-faction tie until diffusion has narrowed the gap below 1/\gamma. The race between those two rates is the race between assimilation and fragmentation.

::: {.lead-kernel}
*Put something on a network and let neighbours pull each other together, and the wiring decides how fast
the whole network agrees.* A single number, the **spectral gap** of the graph Laplacian, sets that speed,
and it is small whenever the network has a bottleneck. Let the ties also respond to what flows along them,
cutting links to the sick or to people who disagree, and you get **adaptive networks**, where the network
can end up split by the very process that should have brought it together.
:::

## Why it matters

Most of the network entries so far describe *structure*: degrees, paths, hubs, communities. But the
questions social scientists actually ask are usually about **processes running on that structure**. Will a
divided organisation converge on one culture after a merger? Will a rumour, a norm or a price signal spread
evenly or stall at a boundary? Will traders, fireflies or neurons fall into step? And do people's ties shift
in response to what they learn, so that the network you measured last year no longer exists?

Sayama sorts the models into three classes [@sayama_complex]:

| Class | What changes | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Dynamics *on* networks** | Node *states*; the wiring is fixed | Opinion spread, epidemics, diffusion of culture or money, coupled oscillators |
| **Dynamics *of* networks** | The *wiring* itself: nodes and links added, dropped or rewired | Watts–Strogatz rewiring, Barabási–Albert growth, triadic closure |
| **Adaptive networks** | Both, each reacting to the other on the same time scale | People cutting ties to infected friends; homophily rewiring a network while influence moves opinions |

The second class already has homes here: [small worlds](small-world-networks.qmd) and
[scale-free growth](scale-free-networks.qmd). This entry covers the first and third, and the one piece of
mathematics that unifies them.

## Dynamics on a fixed network: three ways to agree

### Majority rule and the voter model

The simplest opinion model gives everyone a binary state and lets each person adopt the **majority view of
their ego network** (themselves plus their neighbours), all at once. Run it on a real friendship network and
sometimes everyone converges, and sometimes the network freezes into two camps with a stable boundary
between them [@sayama_complex].

The **voter model** from statistical physics updates one pair at a time: a "listener" copies a "speaker"
it is linked to, until consensus. How the pair is drawn matters [@sayama_complex]:

- **Pull** (pick a listener, then one of its neighbours to speak) tends to pick *high-degree* nodes as
  speakers. This is the [friendship paradox](degree-correlations-and-the-friendship-paradox.qmd): a random
  neighbour is more popular than a random node. Popular people get more airtime, so consensus is faster.
- **Push** (pick a speaker, then a neighbour to listen) gives everyone an equal chance to speak but makes
  hubs the frequent *listeners*, so homogenisation is much slower.
- **Edge-based** (pick a link, then assign the roles at random) sits in between.

Sayama states these as predictions, to be checked by Monte Carlo runs of time-to-consensus
[@sayama_complex]. The lesson holds anyway: *who gets to talk*, not just who is connected, shapes the
outcome.

### Diffusion: neighbours averaging each other

Make the state continuous: an amount of "stuff" at each node, such as money in a city, a firm's cultural
norm, or a population in a habitat. Let it flow across each link in proportion to the difference across
that link [@sayama_complex]:

$$
\frac{dc_i}{dt}=\alpha\sum_{j\in N_i}(c_j-c_i).
$$

Each node gains if its neighbours have more and loses if they have less. Stacked into a vector this is
$\dot{\mathbf c}=-\alpha L\mathbf c$, where $L=D-A$ is the **graph Laplacian**: the degree matrix minus the
adjacency matrix. Its recipe is the same as the Laplacian of a continuous space: *measure the difference
between each neighbour and yourself, and add the differences up*. The only catch is a historical sign flip
($\nabla^2\Leftrightarrow -L$) [@sayama_complex].

Diffusion **conserves the total**, so in a connected network every node ends at the *average* of the
starting values. The interesting question is **how fast**.

### Coupled oscillators: falling into step

Now let each node have its own rhythm, with a preferred frequency $\omega_i$ that differs slightly from
node to node, and let it nudge its phase toward its neighbours'. Kuramoto's model replaces the difference
$\theta_j-\theta_i$ with $\sin(\theta_j-\theta_i)$ [@sayama_complex]. Phases live on a circle: two marching
soldiers who are far apart in *angle* (0 and $2\pi-0.1$) are actually side by side on the track, and
should pull toward each other there. With strong enough coupling the population self-organises into
synchronised marching. The amount of synchrony is measured by the **phase coherence**
$r=\big|\tfrac1n\sum_j e^{i\theta_j}\big|$: 1 for perfect lockstep, near 0 for phases scattered evenly
around the circle. Sayama's examples are fireflies, neurons and **traders in financial markets**. Whether
synchrony is good or bad depends on the context, and the question was part of what motivated Watts and
Strogatz's small-world paper [@sayama_complex].

## One number for the speed of agreement: the spectral gap

All three processes are, near consensus, versions of the same linear dynamics, so the eigenvalues of $L$
govern them. Sayama lists the Laplacian's key properties for an undirected network [@sayama_complex]:

1. It always has an eigenvalue **0**, with the all-ones vector as its eigenvector. Uniform states don't
   move, which is consensus.
2. All other eigenvalues are $\ge 0$.
3. The **number of zero eigenvalues equals the number of connected components**. Each disconnected piece
   settles on its own average.
4. The smallest non-zero eigenvalue, $\lambda_2$, is the **spectral gap**, or **algebraic connectivity**.
   It is the decay rate of the slowest-dying disagreement pattern, so **it sets how fast diffusion
   finishes.**

A small $\lambda_2$ means some pattern of disagreement dies out very slowly. That happens whenever the
network has a **bottleneck**: two dense groups joined by a few bridges, or a long thin ring where
information must travel step by step. On Zachary's karate-club friendship network $\lambda_2\approx 0.4685$
[@sayama_complex]. The number means little alone and only becomes informative when compared with other
topologies, which is what the figure does.

```{python}
#| label: fig-network-dynamics
#| fig-cap: "Left: diffusion on three 40-node networks with the same number of links (80, mean degree 4), all started from the same random states. The vertical axis is the disagreement (standard deviation across nodes) on a log scale; thin dashed lines, offset slightly above each curve, have the slope e^(−λ₂t) predicted by that network's spectral gap, and each curve settles onto its line once the faster modes have died out. Disagreement in the random network (λ₂ ≈ 0.61) dies out about five times faster than in the ring and in the two dense groups joined by two bridges (both λ₂ ≈ 0.12, so their curves almost coincide), the ring because news travels step by step and the two groups because everything must squeeze through the bridges. Right: an adaptive version (Sayama's Eqs. 16.18–16.19) on the same two-group network. The groups start with opposite views (1 and 0) and all tie weights at 0.5. Opinions diffuse along weighted ties while each tie strengthens or weakens with the difference across it, at a rate set by intolerance γ. Tolerant agents (γ = 1, ink) keep their cross-group ties and converge. Picky agents (γ = 3, orange) cut the cross-group ties within a few time units, and the opinion gap freezes near its starting value."
#| code-fold: true
#| code-summary: "Show the figure code (Laplacian diffusion + adaptive homophily, by hand)"

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
n, m = 40, 80

def laplacian(E, n):
    A = np.zeros((n, n))
    for i, j in E:
        A[i, j] = A[j, i] = 1
    return np.diag(A.sum(1)) - A, A

def connected(E, n):
    return np.linalg.eigvalsh(laplacian(E, n)[0])[1] > 1e-9

def ring(n, k=4):                      # each node linked to its k nearest neighbours
    return [(i, (i + d) % n) for i in range(n) for d in range(1, k // 2 + 1)]

def random_graph(n, m):
    while True:
        E = set()
        while len(E) < m:
            i, j = rng.integers(n, size=2)
            if i != j:
                E.add((min(i, j), max(i, j)))
        if connected(E, n):
            return sorted(E)

def two_groups(n, m, bridges=2):        # two dense halves joined by a couple of bridges
    h = n // 2
    while True:
        E = set()
        for off in (0, h):
            inside = set()
            while len(inside) < (m - bridges) // 2:
                i, j = rng.integers(h, size=2)
                if i != j:
                    inside.add((off + min(i, j), off + max(i, j)))
            E |= inside
        while len(E) < m:
            E.add((int(rng.integers(h)), int(h + rng.integers(h))))
        if connected(E, n):
            return sorted(E)

nets = {"random": random_graph(n, m), "ring": ring(n), "two groups + 2 bridges": two_groups(n, m)}
style = {"random": dict(color="#161613"), "ring": dict(color="#b7b6a8"),
         "two groups + 2 bridges": dict(color="#e8531d")}

fig, (ax1, ax2) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(10.4, 4.3))

# ---- Left: diffusion dc/dt = -L c, disagreement vs time ----
dt, T = 0.01, 25
c0 = np.random.default_rng(2).random(n)   # shared random starting states
t_axis = np.arange(0, T + dt / 2, dt)
for name, E in nets.items():
    L, _ = laplacian(E, n)
    lam2 = np.linalg.eigvalsh(L)[1]
    c, sd = c0.copy(), []
    for _ in t_axis:
        sd.append(c.std())
        c = c - (L @ c) * dt
    sd = np.array(sd)
    ax1.plot(t_axis, sd, lw=2.2, label=f"{name}  (λ₂ = {lam2:.2f})", **style[name])
    late = t_axis >= 8                  # slope e^(-λ₂ t), pinned to where the run ends
    ax1.plot(t_axis[late], 3 * sd[-1] * np.exp(lam2 * (T - t_axis[late])), ls="--", lw=0.9,
             **style[name])
ax1.set_yscale("log")
ax1.set_ylim(1e-9, 0.6)
ax1.set_xlabel("time")
ax1.set_ylabel("disagreement (sd of node states)")
ax1.set_title("Same density, different speed of agreement")
ax1.legend(frameon=False, fontsize=8.5, loc="lower left")

# ---- Right: adaptive diffusion with homophily (two factions) ----
E = nets["two groups + 2 bridges"]
_, A = laplacian(E, n)
h = n // 2
cross = np.zeros_like(A, dtype=bool)
cross[:h, h:] = cross[h:, :h] = True
alpha, beta = 1.0, 3.0
t_axis = np.arange(0, 20 + dt / 2, dt)
for gamma, col in ((1.0, "#161613"), (3.0, "#e8531d")):
    c = np.r_[np.ones(h), np.zeros(h)]
    W = 0.5 * A
    gap, wx = [], []
    for _ in t_axis:
        gap.append(c[:h].mean() - c[h:].mean())
        wx.append(W[cross & (A > 0)].mean())
        diff = c[None, :] - c[:, None]                  # c_j - c_i
        dc = alpha * (W * diff).sum(1)
        dW = beta * W * (1 - W) * (1 - gamma * np.abs(diff)) * A
        c, W = c + dc * dt, W + dW * dt
    ax2.plot(t_axis, gap, color=col, lw=2.2, label=f"opinion gap, γ = {gamma:g}")
    ax2.plot(t_axis, wx, color=col, lw=1.3, ls=":", label=f"cross-group tie weight, γ = {gamma:g}")
ax2.set_ylim(-0.03, 1.05)
ax2.set_xlabel("time")
ax2.set_ylabel("gap between group means / mean tie weight")
ax2.set_title("Contagion vs homophily: assimilate or split")
ax2.legend(frameon=False, fontsize=8, loc="center right")

fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

### Synchronisability: when does lockstep survive?

The spectral gap also decides whether coupled oscillators can **stay** synchronised. Sayama linearises
around the synchronised trajectory and gets a clean condition [@sayama_complex]: every non-zero Laplacian
eigenvalue must make *coupling beat the nodes' own tendency to drift apart*. When a node's output rises
with its state, only the smallest one, $\lambda_2$, matters. His worked example has oscillators that each
accelerate at rate $\beta$ while coupled at strength $\alpha$. They synchronise if and only if
$\lambda_2 > \beta/\alpha$. On the karate-club network ($\lambda_2\approx0.4685$), $\alpha=2,\ \beta=1$
gives $\beta/\alpha=0.5$, so synchrony fails: the nodes draw together at first, then scatter as they speed
up. Lowering $\beta$ to $0.9$ ($\beta/\alpha=0.45$) makes synchrony stable. A small parameter change flips
the global behaviour, and a few lines of linear algebra predicted it exactly [@sayama_complex].

## Adaptive networks: when ties respond to what flows along them

In real social systems the network does not sit still while opinions or diseases move across it. Sayama
argues that state–topology **co-evolution** is one of the central open problems in network research,
especially as temporal network data pile up. He also flags that he is one of the field's promoters, so
"take what I say with a pinch of salt" [@sayama_complex]. His examples include epidemics, where contacts
change through quarantine and avoidance, and social communities, where opinions, wealth and membership
change along with who talks to whom.

**Adaptive epidemics.** Add one rule to the SIS model of [spreading on networks](spreading-on-networks.qmd):
a susceptible person who finds that a neighbour is infected **severs that tie** with probability $p_s$.
(This is inspired by Gross and colleagues' 2006 model, in which the tie is *rewired* to another susceptible
person rather than cut.) With parameters that would otherwise cause a pandemic ($p_i=0.5,\ p_r=0.2$) and
$p_s=0.5$, the disease first sweeps the network. Avoidance then thins the links until density falls below
the critical level at which the disease can survive, and it dies out [@sayama_complex]. Behaviour changed
the network, and the new network changed the epidemic.

**Contagion vs homophily.** Sociologists have long argued over two forces: **social contagion** (people
become like their neighbours) and **homophily** (people connect to those already like them). The adaptive
diffusion model puts both in one system [@sayama_complex]. Opinions diffuse along weighted ties, and each
tie's weight grows or shrinks with the opinion difference across it, at a rate set by an **intolerance**
parameter $\gamma$. With $\gamma=0$, every tie strengthens to full weight. With large $\gamma$, only nearly
identical neighbours stay connected. Sayama built it from his work with Junichi Yamanoi on **corporate
mergers**: when do two merged firms' cultures blend into one identity? He seeds it with the factions of
Zachary's karate club, which split over the price of lessons, the instructor Mr. Hi on one side and the
club's officers on the other. With $\alpha=1,\ \beta=3,\ \gamma=3$ the cross-faction ties wither, the
within-faction ties strengthen, and the club splits, as the real club did. Some opinion diffusion still
crosses the divide before the ties go [@sayama_complex]. The right panel of the figure runs the same
equations on a stylised two-group network. Tolerance keeps the bridges and the groups converge; intolerance
cuts them and the gap freezes.

The social-science payoff is sobering. **Fragmentation can be produced by the same dynamics that would
otherwise produce consensus.** A cross-sectional snapshot of a polarised network cannot tell you whether
people sorted by opinion or were persuaded by their neighbours. This is the
[selection-vs-influence](community-detection.qmd) problem, now written as a dynamical system.

## Common pitfalls

::: {.callout-warning title="Where this goes wrong"}
- **Equating density with connectedness.** The three networks in the figure have the *same* number of
  links, yet one agrees five times faster. A bottleneck, not a shortage of ties, is what slows diffusion,
  and $\lambda_2$ detects it where the mean degree cannot.
- **Reading $\lambda_2$ in isolation.** The karate club's 0.4685 means little on its own; spectral gaps
  are informative when you compare topologies of similar size and density [@sayama_complex].
- **Assuming the network is fixed.** If ties respond to states (quarantine, unfriending, sorting), a
  static-network model will mispredict both the epidemic and the opinion distribution. Ask whether the
  wiring changes on the same time scale as the process.
- **Reading the end state as the mechanism.** A split network is consistent with pure homophily, pure
  influence plus a bottleneck, or the co-evolution of both. The end state does not identify the process;
  you need repeated observations of both ties and states over time.
- **Ignoring the update rule.** In the voter model, whether the listener or the speaker is drawn first
  changes the consensus time, through the friendship paradox [@sayama_complex]. "Agents copy neighbours" is
  not a complete model specification.
- **Treating synchrony as automatically good.** Synchronised traders or neurons can be a pathology; the
  model tells you *when* lockstep is stable, not whether you want it.
:::

## Appendix · the mathematics {.unnumbered}

::: {.math-appendix}

**Diffusion as a linear system.** With adjacency matrix $A$ and degree matrix $D$,

$$
\frac{dc_i}{dt}=\alpha\sum_j A_{ij}(c_j-c_i)=\alpha\Big(\sum_j A_{ij}c_j-k_ic_i\Big)
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \dot{\mathbf c}=-\alpha L\mathbf c,\qquad L=D-A.
$$

The figure uses the Euler step $\mathbf c(t+\Delta t)=(I-\alpha L\Delta t)\,\mathbf c(t)$.

**Conservation.** $\mathbf 1^\top L=\mathbf 0^\top$ (each column of $L$ sums to zero), so
$\frac{d}{dt}\mathbf 1^\top\mathbf c=-\alpha\,\mathbf 1^\top L\mathbf c=0$. The total is constant, and in a
connected network the consensus value is the starting mean.

**Spectral solution.** $L$ is symmetric and positive semi-definite ($L=M^\top M$ with $M$ the signed
incidence matrix), so it has orthonormal eigenvectors $\mathbf v_k$ and eigenvalues
$0=\lambda_1\le\lambda_2\le\dots\le\lambda_n$. Expanding the initial state,

$$
\mathbf c(t)=\sum_{k=1}^n a_k\,e^{-\alpha\lambda_k t}\,\mathbf v_k,
\qquad a_k=\mathbf v_k^\top\mathbf c(0).
$$

The $k=1$ term is the uniform mean. Every other mode decays, and the slowest is $e^{-\alpha\lambda_2t}$, so
disagreement eventually shrinks at rate $\alpha\lambda_2$: the dashed lines in the figure. The multiplicity
of the zero eigenvalue equals the number of connected components, since each component's indicator vector
is in the null space.

**Synchronisability.** For $\dot x_i=R(x_i)-\alpha\sum_j L_{ij}H(x_j)$, perturb the synchronised
trajectory: $x_i=x_s(t)+\Delta x_i$. Linearising, and using $L\mathbf 1=\mathbf 0$ to cancel the
$H(x_s)$ terms,

$$
\frac{d\,\Delta\mathbf x}{dt}=\big(R'(x_s)I-\alpha H'(x_s)L\big)\Delta\mathbf x ,
$$

whose eigenvalues are $R'(x_s)-\alpha\lambda_kH'(x_s)$. The $\lambda_1=0$ mode is motion *along* the
synchronised trajectory. Every other mode must decay, which requires
$\alpha\lambda_kH'(x_s(t))>R'(x_s(t))$ for $k=2,\dots,n$ at all $t$. All eigenvalues lie between
$\lambda_2$ and $\lambda_n$, so it suffices to check those two. If $H'\ge0$ only $\lambda_2$ matters. For
$R(\theta)=\beta\theta$, $H(\theta)=\theta$: $\lambda_2>\beta/\alpha$.

**Adaptive diffusion** (Sayama's Eqs. 16.18–16.19), with weights $w_{ij}\in[0,1]$:

$$
\frac{dc_i}{dt}=\alpha\sum_{j\in N_i}(c_j-c_i)\,w_{ij},\qquad
\frac{dw_{ij}}{dt}=\beta\,w_{ij}(1-w_{ij})\big(1-\gamma\,|c_i-c_j|\big).
$$

The logistic factor $w(1-w)$ keeps weights in $[0,1]$. A tie grows when $|c_i-c_j|<1/\gamma$ and decays
otherwise. With opposing factions at 0 and 1, $\gamma\le1$ never cuts a tie, while $\gamma>1$ cuts every
cross-faction tie until diffusion has narrowed the gap below $1/\gamma$. The race between those two rates
is the race between assimilation and fragmentation.

:::
