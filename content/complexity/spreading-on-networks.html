Spreading on Networks: Contagion & Diffusion

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source

A disease, a rumour, a behaviour — none spreads in a well-mixed soup. It travels along the edges of a contact network, and the network’s shape decides whether it fizzles or sweeps the world.

Classical epidemiology assumes everyone mixes with everyone. Real contagion — viral, informational, behavioural — moves node by node along the edges of a contact network, so structure is destiny. The hubs are superspreaders that abolish the usual safety threshold; weak ties carry novelty between communities; and behaviours spread by a different rule than germs. This entry — the capstone of the Network Analysis sub-track — is where everything before it pays off.

Why it matters

Epidemics, viral marketing, the diffusion of innovations, the cascade of a protest or a bank run — all are spreading processes on networks, and the network determines who to vaccinate, who to seed, and why some good ideas never catch on. It also connects straight back to causal inference: peer effects and contagion are why SUTVA (no interference between units) so often fails in social data.

Epidemic models, run on a graph

The compartmental models, but evolving node by node with transmission along edges at probability \beta (Barabási 2016; Menczer et al. 2020):

  • SI — susceptible → infected, and infected forever. The simplest baseline.
  • SIS — no immunity (a cold): infected nodes recover back to susceptible and can be reinfected.
  • SIR — permanent immunity or removal: susceptible → infectious → removed (recovered/dead, no longer transmitting).

Hubs and the missing threshold

Network topology sets the speed and reach (Barabási 2016):

  • Hubs are superspreaders. A high-degree node is infected early (many exposures) and, once infected, rebroadcasts to enormous numbers of others; many shortest paths run through hubs, shrinking the distance the contagion must travel (Barabási 2016).
  • No epidemic threshold on scale-free networks (Pastor-Satorras & Vespignani). In a random network a disease must be “contagious enough” to persist; on a scale-free network the hubs let a disease linger and spread no matter how low its infectiousness (Barabási 2016). The usual safety margin simply vanishes.

Where the thresholds come from: the mean-field approximation

The thresholds above come from one analytical trick, and the trick has a known blind spot. A network model has one state per node, which is far too many variables to analyse with pen and paper. The mean-field approximation replaces all of them with a single number, the average state (for example, the fraction infected). It then asks how one typical node responds to that average, and how the average changes as a result. Every node is treated as if its neighbours were a random draw from the whole population. That hypothetical, well-mixed world is the “mean field”. Averaging the system like this is the same as ignoring who is next to whom (Sayama 2015).

A warm-up where it fails. Sayama first applies it to the majority-rule cellular automaton: on a grid, each cell copies the majority of itself and its eight neighbours (the same rule studied on networks in dynamics on networks). If p is the share of 1s, the chance that a cell is 1 next step is a binomial sum over its neighbours, and it collapses to a single polynomial in p. The polynomial has three equilibria. At 0 and 1 everyone agrees, and both are stable. At 1/2 the two choices are tied, and that equilibrium is unstable. So the approximation predicts that any initial majority snowballs into unanimity: 55% becomes 62%, then 78%, 97% and, by the fourth step, essentially 100% (our iteration of Sayama’s map). Real grid simulations usually don’t do this. Clusters of the minority state survive, because a cell inside a cluster sees mostly its own kind, whatever the global average is (Sayama 2015). The approximation lost exactly the thing that mattered, which was local structure.

Where it works: disease on a random network. For the SIS model (infected nodes recover back to susceptible), let q be the fraction infected, p_i the chance of infection per infected neighbour per step, and p_r the recovery probability. On a random network a susceptible node escapes infection only if none of its contacts passes the disease on. When transmission per contact is rare, the fraction infected next step is approximately q_{t+1}\approx q\,\big[(1-q)\,\langle k\rangle p_i+1-p_r\big], where \langle k\rangle is the average degree (Sayama 2015). This map has two equilibria, q=0 (the disease dies out) and q=1-p_r/(\langle k\rangle p_i) (it becomes endemic). Exactly one is stable at a time, and they swap at p_i=\frac{p_r}{\langle k\rangle}. > In plain words: a disease survives on a random network only if each infected person passes it on > faster than they recover. It has to be “contagious enough”, and more contacts lower the bar > (Sayama 2015).

With ten contacts each and a recovery chance of one half, the bar is p_i=.05. The right panel of Figure 1 runs the model on a 1,000-node random network. Below .05 the disease dies out every time, and above it it settles at an endemic level. The simulation matches the full (un-approximated) mean-field map to within half a percentage point. The simple formula 1-p_r/(\langle k\rangle p_i) gets the threshold right but overstates prevalence further up (.75 against .58 at p_i=.2). That is because it treats each contact’s risk as small, and well above the threshold it is not (our runs).

Why does a random network suit the approximation? Every edge joins two nodes picked from anywhere, so each link is a global bridge that mixes states across the whole system, much as the mean field assumes. The approximation should break down when ties are locally clustered, as in a lattice or a Watts–Strogatz small world (Sayama 2015). The figure’s second network checks this. It is a ring where everyone again has ten contacts, but the contacts are their nearest neighbours. Far above the threshold the two networks look alike. Near the threshold, which is exactly where the policy question lives, the ring needs about half as much contagiousness again before the disease takes hold (p_i\approx.075 rather than .05). An infected node’s contacts are mostly people who are already infected, so its new infections are wasted (our runs).

Hubs: one mean field per degree. On a scale-free network a single average hides the fact that hubs and loners face very different risks. The fix is to keep a separate infection rate q(k) for each degree k (Sayama 2015). There is one twist. The chance that a neighbour is infected cannot use the ordinary degree distribution, because following an edge lands on high-degree nodes more often, with probability proportional to kP(k). This is the friendship paradox doing real work (Sayama 2015). The equilibrium then has hubs infected far more often than loners. On a Barabási–Albert network, the endemic state survives however small p_i is, which is Pastor-Satorras and Vespignani’s no-threshold result reached by Sayama’s route (Sayama 2015). The same formula also shows what “survives” means. With m=2 and p_r=.5, the infected share of neighbours is about 22% at p_i=.1, 3.4% at p_i=.05, and 0.005% at p_i=.02: it shrinks exponentially (our arithmetic). The disease never quite vanishes, but at low contagiousness it lingers at trace levels, sheltered in the hubs.

Sayama’s verdict is the useful one. The mean-field approximation is a “zeroth-order” first look. It works for systems that are fully connected or randomly mixed. Treat it as preliminary, and don’t draw a final conclusion from it, when interactions are local or uneven or the states form patterns. It is also a reference point: if your model behaves just as the mean field predicts, the model is probably simpler than it looks (Sayama 2015).

Simple vs complex contagion

Crucially, information and behaviour spread by different rules (Menczer et al. 2020):

  • Simple contagion — germs and simple information (a rumour) transmit on a single contact (Menczer et al. 2020).
  • Complex contagion — behaviours, norms, and technology adoption need multiple reinforcing contacts: a node adopts only when a threshold fraction q of its neighbours already has (Granovetter threshold models, the Watts global-cascade model) (Menczer et al. 2020).

This flips the value of network structure (Menczer et al. 2020): the long-range shortcuts that make a small world great for simple contagion are ineffective for complex contagion, which needs social reinforcement and so spreads better through densely clustered ties where neighbours share friends (Menczer et al. 2020). Tight communities can block a cascade — why some innovations never diffuse.

The strength of weak ties

Granovetter’s classic insight (Menczer et al. 2020): strong ties (close friends) are redundant — they sit inside the same dense cluster, all knowing each other. Weak ties (acquaintances) act as local bridges between communities, and so are the main conduits of novel information — job leads, news from distant parts of the network (Menczer et al. 2020). The counterintuitive moral: for reach and novelty, your acquaintances matter more than your friends.

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(5)
N, m = 1500, 2
# grow a Barabási–Albert network
deg = np.zeros(N, int); repeated = []; nbrs = [set() for _ in range(N)]
for i in range(m+1):
    for j in range(i+1, m+1):
        nbrs[i].add(j); nbrs[j].add(i); deg[i]+=1; deg[j]+=1; repeated += [i, j]
for new in range(m+1, N):
    tg = set()
    while len(tg) < m: tg.add(repeated[rng.integers(len(repeated))])
    for t in tg:
        nbrs[new].add(t); nbrs[t].add(new); deg[new]+=1; deg[t]+=1; repeated += [new, t]
adj = [np.fromiter(s, int) for s in nbrs]

def sir(immune, beta=0.2, gamma=0.12, T=60):
    state = np.zeros(N, int)                  # 0=S 1=I 2=R
    state[list(immune)] = 2
    ever = np.zeros(N, bool)
    seed = rng.choice(np.where(state == 0)[0]); state[seed] = 1; ever[seed] = True
    curve = []
    for _ in range(T):
        inf = np.where(state == 1)[0]
        ns = state.copy()
        for u in inf:
            for v in adj[u]:
                if state[v] == 0 and rng.random() < beta: ns[v] = 1; ever[v] = True
            if rng.random() < gamma: ns[u] = 2
        state = ns; curve.append(ever.mean())
    return np.array(curve)

nv = int(0.08*N)
rand_immune = set(rng.choice(N, nv, replace=False))
hub_immune = set(np.argsort(deg)[-nv:])
none = np.mean([sir(set()) for _ in range(20)], axis=0)        # 20 runs each, averaged
randc = np.mean([sir(rand_immune) for _ in range(20)], axis=0)
hubc = np.mean([sir(hub_immune) for _ in range(20)], axis=0)

# ---- Right: synchronous SIS vs the mean-field approximation (Sayama §18.5) ----
rs = np.random.default_rng(3)
n, kbar, pr = 1000, 10, 0.5
pe = kbar / (n - 1)
U = np.triu(rs.random((n, n)) < pe, 1); A_er = (U | U.T).astype(np.float32)   # random network
A_ring = np.zeros((n, n), np.float32)                                          # ring: 5 neighbours each side
for d in range(1, kbar // 2 + 1):
    i = np.arange(n); A_ring[i, (i + d) % n] = 1; A_ring[(i + d) % n, i] = 1

def sis(A, pi, T=400, burn=250):
    x = (rs.random(n) < 0.2).astype(np.float32); out = []
    for t in range(T):
        caught = rs.random(n) < 1 - (1 - pi) ** (A @ x)      # escape every infected neighbour, or not
        stays = rs.random(n) >= pr
        x = np.where(x == 1, stays, caught).astype(np.float32)
        if x.sum() == 0: return 0.0
        if t >= burn: out.append(x.mean())
    return float(np.mean(out))

def mf_exact(pi):                                             # iterate Sayama's eq. (18.19)
    q = 0.5
    for _ in range(3000): q = (1 - q) * (1 - (1 - pe * q * pi) ** (n - 1)) + q * (1 - pr)
    return q

pis = np.array([.03, .04, .045, .05, .055, .06, .065, .07, .075, .08, .09, .10, .12, .15, .20, .25])
sim_er = [np.mean([sis(A_er, p) for _ in range(2)]) for p in pis]
sim_ring = [np.mean([sis(A_ring, p) for _ in range(2)]) for p in pis]
grid = np.linspace(.02, .25, 200)
lin = np.clip(1 - pr / (kbar * grid), 0, None)

fig, (ax, ax2) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(10.4, 4.3))
ax.plot(none, color="#161613", lw=2.0, label="no immunisation")
ax.plot(randc, color="#8a8a7e", lw=2.0, label="random 8% immunised")
ax.plot(hubc, color="#e8531d", lw=2.4, label="top-8% hubs immunised")
ax.set_xlabel("time"); ax.set_ylabel("cumulative fraction infected")
ax.set_title("Vaccinate the hubs: same budget")
ax.legend(fontsize=8.5, frameon=False, loc="center right"); ax.set_ylim(0, 1)

ax2.plot(grid, lin, color="#b7b6a8", ls="--", lw=1.6,
         label=r"linearised: $1-p_r/(\langle k\rangle p_i)$")
ax2.plot(grid, [mf_exact(p) for p in grid], color="#5e5e54", lw=1.8, label="full mean-field map")
ax2.plot(pis, sim_er, "o", color="#161613", ms=5, label="simulated, random network")
ax2.plot(pis, sim_ring, "s", color="#8a8a7e", ms=5, label="simulated, ring (clustered)")
ax2.axvline(pr / kbar, color="#161613", ls=":", lw=1)
ax2.text(pr / kbar + .004, .86, "threshold\n" + r"$p_r/\langle k\rangle$", fontsize=8.5)
ax2.set_xlabel(r"infection probability per contact, $p_i$"); ax2.set_ylabel("endemic fraction infected")
ax2.set_title("Mean field: right on random wiring, off on clusters")
ax2.legend(fontsize=8, frameon=False, loc="lower right"); ax2.set_ylim(0, 1)
fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()

Practical implications

The structure points straight at policy (Barabási 2016; Menczer et al. 2020):

  • Targeted immunisation. Vaccinating a small fraction of hubs fragments the network and stops a pandemic far more effectively than random vaccination (Barabási 2016) — the figure’s lesson.
  • Seeding diffusion. In marketing, target influentials with access to easily-influenced others to seed a cascade; but for complex contagion, seed clustered communities for reinforcement (Menczer et al. 2020).
  • Why innovations stall. Densely closed communities can block cascades — a diagnosis for failed diffusion (Menczer et al. 2020).

Common pitfalls

WarningWhere this goes wrong
  • Assuming well-mixing. Network structure changes the threshold, the speed, and who matters; the mean-field SIR can badly mislead. (Barabási 2016)
  • Trusting a mean-field result on a clustered network. The approximation assumes every contact is a random draw from the population. With local, clustered ties it misplaces the threshold, which is the number that matters most. In the figure, the ring needs about 1.5 times the contagiousness. Use it as a zeroth-order first look and check against a simulation of the real wiring. (Sayama 2015)
  • Reading “no threshold” as “large outbreak”. On a scale-free network the endemic state survives at any infectiousness, but its size falls exponentially as infectiousness falls. The result says the disease persists in the hubs, not that it is widespread. (Sayama 2015)
  • Trusting a linearised formula far from where it was linearised. The tidy prevalence formula 1-p_r/(\langle k\rangle p_i) assumes each contact’s risk is small, so it overstates prevalence for very contagious diseases. Sayama’s upper stability bound, above which the synchronous model is said to oscillate, comes from the same linearised map. In our runs above that bound, both the full map and a direct simulation settled at a steady level. (Sayama 2015)
  • Treating behaviour like a virus. Adoption is complex contagion (needs reinforcement); long-range shortcuts that spread rumours fast may fail to spread behaviour. (Menczer et al. 2020)
  • Random vaccination on a scale-free network. It wastes doses on low-degree nodes and misses the hubs doing the spreading. (Barabási 2016)
  • Ignoring interference in causal studies. Contagion violates SUTVA — one unit’s treatment spills to its neighbours. (Menczer et al. 2020)

Appendix · the mathematics

SIR on a network. Each infected node u infects each susceptible neighbour independently with probability \beta per step and recovers with probability \gamma — exactly the loop in the code. The mean-field epidemic threshold is \lambda_c=\beta/\gamma \sim \langle k\rangle/\langle k^2\rangle: an outbreak grows only if \lambda>\lambda_c. On scale-free networks \langle k^2\rangle\to\infty, so \lambda_c\to0 — no threshold. Targeted immunisation works by removing the high-k nodes that make \langle k^2\rangle explode, restoring a finite threshold.

The mean-field SIS map (Sayama 2015). With synchronous updating on a random network with connection probability p_e, a susceptible node avoids infection from each of the other n-1 nodes with probability 1-p_eqp_i, so q_{t+1}=(1-q)\big[1-(1-p_eqp_i)^{n-1}\big]+q(1-p_r) \approx q\big[(1-q)s+1-p_r\big]\equiv f(q),\qquad s=(n-1)p_ep_i=\langle k\rangle p_i, using (1-x)^{n-1}\approx1-(n-1)x for small x. Setting f(q)=q gives q=0 or q=1-p_r/s. The slopes are f'(0)=1-p_r+s and f'(1-p_r/s)=1+p_r-s. An equilibrium of a map is stable when |f'|<1, so for -1\le p_r-s\le1 extinction is stable exactly when s<p_r, and the endemic state when s>p_r: the threshold is p_i=p_r/\langle k\rangle. The figure’s dark line iterates the first (exact) expression; the pale dashed line is 1-p_r/s.

Degree-based mean field (Sayama 2015). Let q(k) be the infected share among degree-k nodes. A neighbour has degree k' with probability k'P(k')/\langle k\rangle, so the chance that a neighbour is infected is q_n=\sum_{k'}k'P(k')q(k')/\langle k\rangle. Linearising as before, q_{eq}(k)=\frac{kq_np_i}{kq_np_i+p_r}, which rises with k: hubs are infected most. Substituting back gives a self-consistency equation for q_n. For P(k)\approx1 at k=\langle k\rangle it returns the random-network threshold, together with an upper bound p_i<(p_r+2)/\langle k\rangle beyond which the linearised endemic state loses stability. For a Barabási–Albert network, P(k)=2m^2k^{-3} with k\ge m, and approximating the sum by an integral gives q_n\approx\frac{p_r}{\big(e^{p_r/(mp_i)}-1\big)\,mp_i}>0\quad\text{for every }p_i>0, with the slope at the endemic state tending to 1-p_r\in(0,1) as p_i\to0. The endemic state stays stable, so there is no threshold. The exponential in the denominator is why the endemic level becomes tiny at small p_i (with m=2, p_r=.5: q_n\approx.224, .034 and 4.7\times10^{-5} at p_i=.1, .05 and .02).

Complex-contagion threshold. In Watts’ model a node with fraction \phi of adopting neighbours adopts iff \phi\ge q. A global cascade requires a connected “vulnerable” cluster (nodes with low enough threshold relative to degree); too few or too many connections both suppress cascades, so there is a cascade window in (q,\langle k\rangle) — unlike simple contagion, more connectivity is not always more spread.

Why weak ties carry novelty. A strong tie sits in a triangle (its endpoints share neighbours), so its information is redundant. A weak tie that is a local bridge is the only short path between two clusters, so it is the unique conduit for whatever one cluster knows and the other doesn’t.

  • Connects to: Erdős–Rényi percolation (the \langle k\rangle=1 threshold is the epidemic threshold), branching processes (why a mean of one new case per case is the knife-edge between fizzling out and taking off), causal interference / SUTVA, and the friendship paradox, which supplies the neighbour-degree weights in the degree-based mean field.

Classical epidemiology assumes everyone mixes with everyone. Real contagion — viral, informational,
behavioural — moves **node by node along the edges** of a contact network, so **structure is destiny**. The
[hubs](scale-free-networks.qmd) are superspreaders that abolish the usual safety threshold; **weak ties**
carry novelty between communities; and *behaviours* spread by a different rule than *germs*. This entry —
the capstone of the Network Analysis sub-track — is where everything before it pays off.

## Why it matters

Epidemics, viral marketing, the diffusion of innovations, the cascade of a protest or a bank run — all are
spreading processes on networks, and the network determines who to vaccinate, who to seed, and why some good
ideas never catch on. It also connects straight back to causal inference: [peer effects and
contagion](../05-causal-inference/peer-effects-and-spillovers.qmd) are why
[SUTVA](../05-causal-inference/potential-outcomes.qmd) (no interference between units) so often fails in
social data.

## Epidemic models, run on a graph

The compartmental models, but evolving **node by node** with transmission **along edges** at probability
$\beta$ [@barabasi_network; @menczer_network]:

- **SI** — susceptible → infected, and infected **forever**. The simplest baseline.
- **SIS** — no immunity (a cold): infected nodes recover **back to susceptible** and can be reinfected.
- **SIR** — permanent immunity or removal: susceptible → **infectious** → **removed** (recovered/dead, no
  longer transmitting).

## Hubs and the missing threshold

Network topology sets the speed and reach [@barabasi_network]:

- **Hubs are superspreaders.** A high-degree node is **infected early** (many exposures) and, once infected,
  **rebroadcasts to enormous numbers** of others; many shortest paths run through hubs, shrinking the
  distance the contagion must travel [@barabasi_network].
- **No epidemic threshold on scale-free networks** (Pastor-Satorras & Vespignani). In a random network a
  disease must be "contagious enough" to persist; on a **scale-free** network the **hubs let a disease linger
  and spread *no matter how low* its infectiousness** [@barabasi_network]. The usual safety margin simply
  vanishes.

## Where the thresholds come from: the mean-field approximation {#sec-mean-field}

The thresholds above come from one analytical trick, and the trick has a known blind spot. A network
model has one state per node, which is far too many variables to analyse with pen and paper. The
**mean-field approximation** replaces all of them with a single number, the *average* state (for example,
the fraction infected). It then asks how one typical node responds to that average, and how the average
changes as a result. Every node is treated as if its neighbours were a random draw from the whole
population. That hypothetical, well-mixed world is the "mean field". Averaging the system like this is the
same as **ignoring who is next to whom** [@sayama_complex].

**A warm-up where it fails.** Sayama first applies it to the majority-rule cellular automaton: on a grid,
each cell copies the majority of itself and its eight neighbours (the same rule studied on networks in
[dynamics on networks](dynamics-on-networks.qmd#sec-majority-rule)). If $p$ is the share of 1s, the chance
that a cell is 1 next step is a binomial sum over its neighbours, and it collapses to a single polynomial
in $p$. The polynomial has three equilibria. At 0 and 1 everyone agrees, and both are stable. At $1/2$ the
two choices are tied, and that equilibrium is unstable. So the approximation predicts that any initial
majority snowballs into unanimity: 55% becomes 62%, then 78%, 97% and, by the fourth step, essentially 100% (our
iteration of Sayama's map). Real grid simulations usually don't do this. **Clusters of the minority state
survive**, because a cell inside a cluster sees mostly its own kind, whatever the global average is
[@sayama_complex]. The approximation lost exactly the thing that mattered, which was local structure.

**Where it works: disease on a random network.** For the SIS model (infected nodes recover back to
susceptible), let $q$ be the fraction infected, $p_i$ the chance of infection per infected neighbour per
step, and $p_r$ the recovery probability. On a random network a susceptible node escapes infection only if
none of its contacts passes the disease on. When transmission per contact is rare, the fraction infected
next step is approximately
$$
q_{t+1}\approx q\,\big[(1-q)\,\langle k\rangle p_i+1-p_r\big],
$$
where $\langle k\rangle$ is the average degree [@sayama_complex]. This map has two equilibria, $q=0$
(the disease dies out) and $q=1-p_r/(\langle k\rangle p_i)$ (it becomes endemic). Exactly one is stable at a
time, and they swap at
$$
p_i=\frac{p_r}{\langle k\rangle}.
$$
> **In plain words:** a disease survives on a random network only if each infected person passes it on
> faster than they recover. It has to be "contagious enough", and more contacts lower the bar
> [@sayama_complex].

With ten contacts each and a recovery chance of one half, the bar is $p_i=.05$. The right panel of
@fig-immunization runs the model on a 1,000-node random network. Below .05 the disease dies out every time,
and above it it settles at an endemic level. The simulation matches the full (un-approximated) mean-field
map to within half a percentage point. The simple formula $1-p_r/(\langle k\rangle p_i)$ gets the threshold
right but **overstates prevalence** further up (.75 against .58 at $p_i=.2$). That is because it treats each
contact's risk as small, and well above the threshold it is not (our runs).

Why does a random network suit the approximation? Every edge joins two nodes picked from anywhere, so
**each link is a global bridge** that mixes states across the whole system, much as the mean field assumes.
The approximation should break down when ties are **locally clustered**, as in a lattice or a Watts–Strogatz
small world [@sayama_complex]. The figure's second network checks this. It is a ring where everyone again has
ten contacts, but the contacts are their nearest neighbours. Far above the threshold the two networks look
alike. Near the threshold, which is exactly where the policy question lives, the ring needs about half as
much contagiousness again before the disease takes hold ($p_i\approx.075$ rather than .05). An infected
node's contacts are mostly people who are already infected, so its new infections are wasted (our runs).

**Hubs: one mean field per degree.** On a scale-free network a single average hides the fact that hubs
and loners face very different risks. The fix is to keep a separate infection rate $q(k)$ for each degree
$k$ [@sayama_complex]. There is one twist. The chance that a *neighbour* is infected cannot use the ordinary
degree distribution, because following an edge lands on high-degree nodes more often, with probability
proportional to $kP(k)$. This is the [friendship paradox](degree-correlations-and-the-friendship-paradox.qmd)
doing real work [@sayama_complex]. The equilibrium then has hubs infected far more often than loners. On a
Barabási–Albert network, the endemic state survives however small $p_i$ is, which is Pastor-Satorras and
Vespignani's **no-threshold** result reached by Sayama's route [@sayama_complex]. The same formula also
shows what "survives" means. With $m=2$ and $p_r=.5$, the infected share of neighbours is about 22% at
$p_i=.1$, 3.4% at $p_i=.05$, and 0.005% at $p_i=.02$: it shrinks exponentially (our arithmetic). The disease
never quite vanishes, but at low contagiousness it lingers at trace levels, sheltered in the hubs.

Sayama's verdict is the useful one. The mean-field approximation is a **"zeroth-order" first look**. It
works for systems that are fully connected or randomly mixed. Treat it as preliminary, and don't draw a
final conclusion from it, when interactions are local or uneven or the states form patterns. It is also a
**reference point**: if your model behaves just as the mean field predicts, the model is probably simpler
than it looks [@sayama_complex].

## Simple vs complex contagion

Crucially, **information and behaviour spread by different rules** [@menczer_network]:

- **Simple contagion** — germs and simple information (a rumour) transmit on a **single contact**
  [@menczer_network].
- **Complex contagion** — behaviours, norms, and technology **adoption** need **multiple reinforcing
  contacts**: a node adopts only when a **threshold fraction $q$ of its neighbours** already has (Granovetter
  **threshold models**, the Watts **global-cascade** model) [@menczer_network].

This flips the value of network structure [@menczer_network]: the long-range **shortcuts** that make a
[small world](small-world-networks.qmd) great for *simple* contagion are **ineffective for complex**
contagion, which needs **social reinforcement** and so spreads better through **densely clustered ties**
where neighbours share friends [@menczer_network]. Tight communities can *block* a cascade — why some
innovations never diffuse.

## The strength of weak ties

Granovetter's classic insight [@menczer_network]: **strong ties** (close friends) are **redundant** — they
sit inside the same dense cluster, all knowing each other. **Weak ties** (acquaintances) act as **local
bridges between communities**, and so are the **main conduits of novel information** — job leads, news from
distant parts of the network [@menczer_network]. The counterintuitive moral: for *reach and novelty*, your
acquaintances matter more than your friends.

```{python}
#| label: fig-immunization
#| fig-cap: "Left: why you vaccinate the hubs, simulated. An SIR epidemic runs on a scale-free network (grown by preferential attachment). Averaged over 20 runs, with no immunisation (ink) the outbreak reaches nearly 80% of the network. Immunising 8% of nodes at random (olive) helps only a little (about 69%): a random node is almost always low-degree, so the hubs survive and keep spreading. Immunising the same 8%, chosen as the highest-degree hubs (orange), collapses the epidemic. Same vaccine budget, opposite outcome. Right: checking the mean-field approximation. Synchronous SIS (recovery chance .5) on two 1,000-node networks where everyone has about ten contacts, run to equilibrium at each infection probability. On a random network (ink circles) the simulation tracks the full mean-field map (dark line) and takes off at the predicted threshold p_r/⟨k⟩ = .05 (dotted). The linearised formula 1 − p_r/(⟨k⟩p_i) (pale dashed) gets the threshold right but overstates prevalence above it. On a ring of nearest neighbours (olive squares) the same contacts are clustered, and the disease needs about 1.5 times the contagiousness to persist."
#| code-fold: true
#| code-summary: "Show the figure code (BA network + SIR, then SIS vs mean field, by hand)"

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(5)
N, m = 1500, 2
# grow a Barabási–Albert network
deg = np.zeros(N, int); repeated = []; nbrs = [set() for _ in range(N)]
for i in range(m+1):
    for j in range(i+1, m+1):
        nbrs[i].add(j); nbrs[j].add(i); deg[i]+=1; deg[j]+=1; repeated += [i, j]
for new in range(m+1, N):
    tg = set()
    while len(tg) < m: tg.add(repeated[rng.integers(len(repeated))])
    for t in tg:
        nbrs[new].add(t); nbrs[t].add(new); deg[new]+=1; deg[t]+=1; repeated += [new, t]
adj = [np.fromiter(s, int) for s in nbrs]

def sir(immune, beta=0.2, gamma=0.12, T=60):
    state = np.zeros(N, int)                  # 0=S 1=I 2=R
    state[list(immune)] = 2
    ever = np.zeros(N, bool)
    seed = rng.choice(np.where(state == 0)[0]); state[seed] = 1; ever[seed] = True
    curve = []
    for _ in range(T):
        inf = np.where(state == 1)[0]
        ns = state.copy()
        for u in inf:
            for v in adj[u]:
                if state[v] == 0 and rng.random() < beta: ns[v] = 1; ever[v] = True
            if rng.random() < gamma: ns[u] = 2
        state = ns; curve.append(ever.mean())
    return np.array(curve)

nv = int(0.08*N)
rand_immune = set(rng.choice(N, nv, replace=False))
hub_immune = set(np.argsort(deg)[-nv:])
none = np.mean([sir(set()) for _ in range(20)], axis=0)        # 20 runs each, averaged
randc = np.mean([sir(rand_immune) for _ in range(20)], axis=0)
hubc = np.mean([sir(hub_immune) for _ in range(20)], axis=0)

# ---- Right: synchronous SIS vs the mean-field approximation (Sayama §18.5) ----
rs = np.random.default_rng(3)
n, kbar, pr = 1000, 10, 0.5
pe = kbar / (n - 1)
U = np.triu(rs.random((n, n)) < pe, 1); A_er = (U | U.T).astype(np.float32)   # random network
A_ring = np.zeros((n, n), np.float32)                                          # ring: 5 neighbours each side
for d in range(1, kbar // 2 + 1):
    i = np.arange(n); A_ring[i, (i + d) % n] = 1; A_ring[(i + d) % n, i] = 1

def sis(A, pi, T=400, burn=250):
    x = (rs.random(n) < 0.2).astype(np.float32); out = []
    for t in range(T):
        caught = rs.random(n) < 1 - (1 - pi) ** (A @ x)      # escape every infected neighbour, or not
        stays = rs.random(n) >= pr
        x = np.where(x == 1, stays, caught).astype(np.float32)
        if x.sum() == 0: return 0.0
        if t >= burn: out.append(x.mean())
    return float(np.mean(out))

def mf_exact(pi):                                             # iterate Sayama's eq. (18.19)
    q = 0.5
    for _ in range(3000): q = (1 - q) * (1 - (1 - pe * q * pi) ** (n - 1)) + q * (1 - pr)
    return q

pis = np.array([.03, .04, .045, .05, .055, .06, .065, .07, .075, .08, .09, .10, .12, .15, .20, .25])
sim_er = [np.mean([sis(A_er, p) for _ in range(2)]) for p in pis]
sim_ring = [np.mean([sis(A_ring, p) for _ in range(2)]) for p in pis]
grid = np.linspace(.02, .25, 200)
lin = np.clip(1 - pr / (kbar * grid), 0, None)

fig, (ax, ax2) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(10.4, 4.3))
ax.plot(none, color="#161613", lw=2.0, label="no immunisation")
ax.plot(randc, color="#8a8a7e", lw=2.0, label="random 8% immunised")
ax.plot(hubc, color="#e8531d", lw=2.4, label="top-8% hubs immunised")
ax.set_xlabel("time"); ax.set_ylabel("cumulative fraction infected")
ax.set_title("Vaccinate the hubs: same budget")
ax.legend(fontsize=8.5, frameon=False, loc="center right"); ax.set_ylim(0, 1)

ax2.plot(grid, lin, color="#b7b6a8", ls="--", lw=1.6,
         label=r"linearised: $1-p_r/(\langle k\rangle p_i)$")
ax2.plot(grid, [mf_exact(p) for p in grid], color="#5e5e54", lw=1.8, label="full mean-field map")
ax2.plot(pis, sim_er, "o", color="#161613", ms=5, label="simulated, random network")
ax2.plot(pis, sim_ring, "s", color="#8a8a7e", ms=5, label="simulated, ring (clustered)")
ax2.axvline(pr / kbar, color="#161613", ls=":", lw=1)
ax2.text(pr / kbar + .004, .86, "threshold\n" + r"$p_r/\langle k\rangle$", fontsize=8.5)
ax2.set_xlabel(r"infection probability per contact, $p_i$"); ax2.set_ylabel("endemic fraction infected")
ax2.set_title("Mean field: right on random wiring, off on clusters")
ax2.legend(fontsize=8, frameon=False, loc="lower right"); ax2.set_ylim(0, 1)
fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

## Practical implications

The structure points straight at policy [@barabasi_network; @menczer_network]:

- **Targeted immunisation.** Vaccinating a small fraction of **hubs fragments the network** and stops a
  pandemic far more effectively than random vaccination [@barabasi_network] — the figure's lesson.
- **Seeding diffusion.** In marketing, target **influentials** with access to easily-influenced others to
  **seed a cascade**; but for *complex* contagion, seed **clustered** communities for reinforcement
  [@menczer_network].
- **Why innovations stall.** Densely closed communities can **block** cascades — a diagnosis for failed
  diffusion [@menczer_network].

## Common pitfalls

::: {.callout-warning title="Where this goes wrong"}
- **Assuming well-mixing.** Network structure changes the threshold, the speed, and who matters; the
  mean-field SIR can badly mislead. [@barabasi_network]
- **Trusting a mean-field result on a clustered network.** The approximation assumes every contact is a
  random draw from the population. With local, clustered ties it misplaces the threshold, which is the
  number that matters most. In the figure, the ring needs about 1.5 times the contagiousness. Use it as a
  zeroth-order first look and check against a simulation of the real wiring. [@sayama_complex]
- **Reading "no threshold" as "large outbreak".** On a scale-free network the endemic state survives at
  any infectiousness, but its size falls exponentially as infectiousness falls. The result says the disease
  persists in the hubs, not that it is widespread. [@sayama_complex]
- **Trusting a linearised formula far from where it was linearised.** The tidy prevalence formula
  $1-p_r/(\langle k\rangle p_i)$ assumes each contact's risk is small, so it overstates prevalence for very
  contagious diseases. Sayama's upper stability bound, above which the synchronous model is said to
  oscillate, comes from the same linearised map. In our runs above that bound, both the full map and a
  direct simulation settled at a steady level. [@sayama_complex]
- **Treating behaviour like a virus.** Adoption is *complex* contagion (needs reinforcement); long-range
  shortcuts that spread rumours fast may fail to spread behaviour. [@menczer_network]
- **Random vaccination on a scale-free network.** It wastes doses on low-degree nodes and misses the hubs
  doing the spreading. [@barabasi_network]
- **Ignoring interference in causal studies.** Contagion violates [SUTVA](../05-causal-inference/potential-outcomes.qmd) —
  one unit's treatment spills to its neighbours. [@menczer_network]
:::

## Appendix · the mathematics {.unnumbered}

::: {.math-appendix}

**SIR on a network.** Each infected node $u$ infects each susceptible neighbour independently with
probability $\beta$ per step and recovers with probability $\gamma$ — exactly the loop in the code. The
mean-field epidemic threshold is $\lambda_c=\beta/\gamma \sim \langle k\rangle/\langle k^2\rangle$: an
outbreak grows only if $\lambda>\lambda_c$. On scale-free networks $\langle k^2\rangle\to\infty$, so
$\lambda_c\to0$ — **no threshold**. Targeted immunisation works by *removing the high-$k$ nodes that make
$\langle k^2\rangle$ explode*, restoring a finite threshold.

**The mean-field SIS map** [@sayama_complex]. With synchronous updating on a random network with connection
probability $p_e$, a susceptible node avoids infection from each of the other $n-1$ nodes with probability
$1-p_eqp_i$, so
$$
q_{t+1}=(1-q)\big[1-(1-p_eqp_i)^{n-1}\big]+q(1-p_r)
\approx q\big[(1-q)s+1-p_r\big]\equiv f(q),\qquad s=(n-1)p_ep_i=\langle k\rangle p_i,
$$
using $(1-x)^{n-1}\approx1-(n-1)x$ for small $x$. Setting $f(q)=q$ gives $q=0$ or $q=1-p_r/s$. The slopes
are $f'(0)=1-p_r+s$ and $f'(1-p_r/s)=1+p_r-s$. An equilibrium of a map is stable when $|f'|<1$, so for
$-1\le p_r-s\le1$ extinction is stable exactly when $s<p_r$, and the endemic state when $s>p_r$: the
threshold is $p_i=p_r/\langle k\rangle$. The figure's dark line iterates the first (exact) expression; the
pale dashed line is $1-p_r/s$.

**Degree-based mean field** [@sayama_complex]. Let $q(k)$ be the infected share among degree-$k$ nodes. A
neighbour has degree $k'$ with probability $k'P(k')/\langle k\rangle$, so the chance that a neighbour is
infected is $q_n=\sum_{k'}k'P(k')q(k')/\langle k\rangle$. Linearising as before,
$$
q_{eq}(k)=\frac{kq_np_i}{kq_np_i+p_r},
$$
which rises with $k$: hubs are infected most. Substituting back gives a self-consistency equation for $q_n$.
For $P(k)\approx1$ at $k=\langle k\rangle$ it returns the random-network threshold, together with an upper
bound $p_i<(p_r+2)/\langle k\rangle$ beyond which the linearised endemic state loses stability. For a
Barabási–Albert network, $P(k)=2m^2k^{-3}$ with $k\ge m$, and approximating the sum by an integral gives
$$
q_n\approx\frac{p_r}{\big(e^{p_r/(mp_i)}-1\big)\,mp_i}>0\quad\text{for every }p_i>0,
$$
with the slope at the endemic state tending to $1-p_r\in(0,1)$ as $p_i\to0$. The endemic state stays
stable, so there is no threshold. The exponential in the denominator is why the endemic level becomes tiny
at small $p_i$ (with $m=2$, $p_r=.5$: $q_n\approx.224$, .034 and $4.7\times10^{-5}$ at $p_i=.1$, .05 and .02).

**Complex-contagion threshold.** In Watts' model a node with fraction $\phi$ of adopting neighbours adopts iff
$\phi\ge q$. A global cascade requires a connected **"vulnerable" cluster** (nodes with low enough threshold
relative to degree); too few or too many connections both *suppress* cascades, so there is a cascade *window*
in $(q,\langle k\rangle)$ — unlike simple contagion, more connectivity is not always more spread.

**Why weak ties carry novelty.** A strong tie sits in a triangle (its endpoints share neighbours), so its
information is redundant. A weak tie that is a **local bridge** is the *only* short path between two clusters,
so it is the unique conduit for whatever one cluster knows and the other doesn't.
