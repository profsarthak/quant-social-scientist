Information Cascades: Rational Herding

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source

Why fully rational people abandon their own evidence and copy the crowd — and how a whole population can lock onto the wrong answer from almost no information at all.

When you can see what other people do but not what they know, it can be perfectly rational to ignore your own private information and imitate them. Once enough people do this, an information cascade forms: everyone copies everyone, no new information enters, and the crowd can settle — confidently, unanimously, and wrongly — on a choice backed by only a handful of early signals.

Why it matters

Fashions and fads, best-seller lists that feed on themselves, bank runs, viral products, a hiring committee that swings unanimously behind the first name spoken, the 45% of passers-by who stop to stare at the sky once enough others do — all look like mindless conformity, and all can instead be rational imitation under uncertainty (Easley and Kleinberg 2010). The distinction matters for a social scientist because it changes the diagnosis and the cure. If a run of identical choices reflects independent private information, it is strong evidence. If it reflects a cascade, it is almost no evidence at all — a long column of people each copying the two people in front — and the usual comfort that “surely all those people can’t be wrong” is misplaced. Cascades are the mechanism that turns the wisdom of crowds into the madness of crowds.

Following the crowd can be rational

You arrive in an unfamiliar town intending to eat at restaurant A, chosen from your own online research. You get there and A is empty while B next door is packed. If the other diners did research too, their crowd is telling you something your single review did not — and it can be rational to join B against your own information (Easley and Kleinberg 2010). This is not social pressure to conform; it is inference. You are reading the earlier diners’ actions as evidence about their private signals.

Two ingredients make this happen, and they recur in every example:

  • Decisions are made sequentially, and later people can observe the choices of earlier people.
  • Later people cannot see the private information behind those choices — only the choices themselves.

That gap — seeing what others do but not what they know — is the entire engine. Easley & Kleinberg carefully separate this informational effect from a second, different reason to imitate: direct-benefit / network effects, where you copy others because their choice directly raises your payoff (a fax machine is worthless if no one else owns one). Both produce herding; only the first is about learning from the crowd.

The urn experiment: watch a cascade start

Anderson & Holt’s classroom experiment strips the idea to its bones (Easley and Kleinberg 2010). An urn is either majority-red (2 red, 1 blue) or majority-blue (2 blue, 1 red), each with prior probability ½. Students file up one at a time, privately draw a marble, replace it, and then publicly announce a guess of the urn’s majority — but not the colour they drew. Everyone hears the guesses; no one sees the draws.

  • Student 1 simply guesses the colour drawn — so the guess perfectly reveals the private draw.
  • Student 2, if she draws the opposite colour, is now effectively holding one red and one blue; she is indifferent, and by convention follows her own draw. Either way, her guess again reveals her draw.
  • Student 3 is where it turns. Suppose the first two guessed blue, blue, and student 3 draws red. He now reasons as though he has seen three draws — blue, blue, red — a 2-to-1 majority for blue. So he should guess blue, discarding his own red draw. And crucially, the class hears only “blue” — his guess now carries no information about what he saw.
  • Student 4 onward knows student 3’s guess was uninformative, so she is in exactly his position. Whatever she draws, the two genuine blue signals outweigh it. She guesses blue too. The cascade has begun, and every remaining student guesses blue regardless of their marble.

Three lessons fall out of this toy, and the general model below confirms all three:

  • Cascades can be wrong. If the urn is really majority-red, there is a \tfrac13\cdot\tfrac13=\tfrac19 chance the first two students both draw blue — and then everyone guesses blue, forever, all wrong. Adding more students does not help: they all herd too.
  • Cascades rest on almost no information. Once a cascade starts, private signals stop entering the public record. A crowd of 1000 may be acting on the two marbles the first two students happened to draw.
  • Cascades are fragile. Because they rest on so little, they are easily broken. If students 50 and 51 quietly show their red marbles to the room, the next student now has four real signals (two blue, two red) and follows her own draw again — a 49-guess run of “blue” overturned by two fresh facts.

The general model: a random walk with a two-step cliff

Easley & Kleinberg abstract the urn into a model that fits adoption, voting, restaurant choice, or crime (Easley and Kleinberg 2010). The world is in one of two states — the option is a good idea (G) or a bad idea (B) — with prior \Pr[G]=p. Each person privately gets a high signal (H, favouring accept) or a low signal (L), with quality q>\tfrac12: \Pr[H\mid G]=\Pr[L\mid B]=q. Payoffs are set so that with no signal you are exactly indifferent between accepting and rejecting.

Two facts drive everything. First, one signal tips a rational person: a high signal raises the posterior \Pr[G\mid H] = \tfrac{pq}{pq+(1-p)(1-q)} > p, so you accept; a low signal, you reject (derivation in the appendix). Second, with a bag of signals — a high and b low — the posterior beats the prior exactly when a>b: rational choice is a majority vote over the signals you can infer.

Now run it sequentially. Because everyone before a cascade is following their own signal, you can infer their signals from their actions and add them to your own. Track the running difference

D = (\#\text{accepts}) - (\#\text{rejects}).

  • While |D|\le 1, your single private signal can still swing the majority, so you follow your own signal — and reveal it.
  • The instant |D| reaches 2, no single signal can overturn the lead. You follow the crowd and ignore your signal — and because everyone knows you did, your action reveals nothing. Everyone after you is in the identical position. The cascade locks, and by self-reinforcement it never unlocks.

So D is a random walk that starts at 0 and drifts until it first escapes the ribbon [-1,+1]; the moment it hits \pm 2 the population freezes onto that choice. And escape is essentially certain: three matching signals in a row always trigger a cascade, and in a long enough sequence that is bound to happen — the probability a cascade has formed converges to 1 as the crowd grows (Easley and Kleinberg 2010). What is not guaranteed is that it forms on the right side.

The wisdom of crowds runs backwards

Here is the punchline a quantitative researcher should carry away, and it directly contradicts the popular “wisdom of crowds” story (Easley and Kleinberg 2010). Surowiecki’s jar-of-jelly-beans result — that averaging many independent guesses is uncannily accurate — depends entirely on the guesses being independent. Each person’s private signal enters the pool. Pool enough of them and the law of large numbers / Condorcet logic drives the aggregate to the truth.

Let people observe each other and decide in sequence, and the same crowd stops learning. A cascade locks in after a few signals; the thousandth person contributes a copy, not a signal. So the collective accuracy plateaus well below certainty and stops improving with size — more people cannot rescue a herd. The figure makes the contrast concrete.

Show the figure code (both aggregation rules, by hand) 
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(16)
q = 0.70                       # signal quality; true state = Good, so P(high signal) = q
sizes = np.arange(1, 61)
RUNS = 6000

def independent_correct(n):
    # each person acts on own signal alone; "collective verdict" = majority action
    highs = rng.random((RUNS, n)) < q          # high signal favours the (correct) Accept
    maj_accept = highs.sum(axis=1) > (n / 2)    # strict majority accepts
    ties = highs.sum(axis=1) == (n / 2)         # break ties by a fair coin
    maj_accept = np.where(ties, rng.random(RUNS) < 0.5, maj_accept)
    return maj_accept.mean()

def herding_correct(n):
    # sequential: follow own signal while |D|<=1, else follow the crowd (cascade locks)
    sig = rng.random((RUNS, n)) < q
    correct = np.zeros(RUNS, bool)
    for r in range(RUNS):
        D = 0
        act = True
        for i in range(n):
            if abs(D) >= 2:
                act = D > 0                      # cascade: copy the lead, ignore signal
            else:
                act = bool(sig[r, i])            # informative: follow own signal
            D += 1 if act else -1
        correct[r] = act                         # the herd's standing verdict = last action
    return correct.mean()

ind = np.array([independent_correct(n) for n in sizes])
herd = np.array([herding_correct(n) for n in sizes])

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7.2, 4.3))
ax.axhline(1.0, color="#b7b6a8", lw=0.8, ls=":")
ax.plot(sizes, ind, color="#161613", lw=2.3, label="independent — pool private signals")
ax.plot(sizes, herd, color="#e8531d", lw=2.4, label="sequential herding — observe & copy")
ax.annotate("cascade wastes\nthe crowd's information",
            xy=(52, herd[51]), xytext=(30, 0.72), fontsize=8.5, color="#e8531d",
            arrowprops=dict(arrowstyle="->", color="#e8531d", lw=1.1))
ax.set_xlabel("number of decision-makers")
ax.set_ylabel("P(crowd's verdict is correct)")
ax.set_title("More people rescue independent guesses, but not a herd")
ax.set_ylim(0.5, 1.02)
ax.legend(fontsize=8.5, frameon=False, loc="center right")
fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()

Informational effects vs network effects

The imitation in a cascade is informational — you copy others because their actions carry news. Easley & Kleinberg contrast this sharply with direct-benefit or network effects, where you copy others because their choice directly changes your payoff (Easley and Kleinberg 2010). A fax machine, an operating system, a social platform: each is more valuable the more others use it — a positive externality. The two mechanisms look identical from outside (everyone ends up on the same option) but behave differently in one respect that matters for policy:

  • Cascades are fragile — a little fresh public information overturns even a long run, because the run was built on so little.
  • Network effects are sticky — once a standard tips, its advantage is self-perpetuating and hard to reverse, even for a technically superior rival, because the benefit is real, not inferential.

Telling them apart in the field is exactly the diagnostic question: is the crowd giving you information, or giving you compatibility? Spreading on networks takes the structural version of this story — how the shape of the contact network decides whether a behaviour cascades — where this entry takes the informational version on a sequence of decision-makers.

Common pitfalls

WarningWhere this goes wrong
  • “They can’t all be wrong.” A unanimous crowd can rest on two early signals. Length of a run is not evidence of its strength once a cascade is possible. (Easley and Kleinberg 2010)
  • Going around the table. Polling a committee in sequence manufactures cascades: a few early voices for A can rationally swing the rest, even against their own read. Collect independent judgements first, then discuss — and let the best-informed people speak last, not first. (Easley and Kleinberg 2010)
  • Mistaking a cascade for wisdom-of-crowds. Averaging works only for independent signals; the moment people see each other’s choices, the aggregate can stop tracking the truth. (Easley and Kleinberg 2010)
  • Confusing herding with conformity. Cascades need no social pressure — pure rational inference produces them. The cure (inject public information) differs from the cure for conformity (protect dissent). (Easley and Kleinberg 2010)
  • Assuming more data fixes it. A larger crowd does not break a cascade; only new information that enters the public record does (revealed payoffs, an audit, a forced independent report). (Easley and Kleinberg 2010)

From the sources

NoteWho says what
  • Easley & Kleinberg, Networks, Crowds, and Markets (Ch. 16–17) — the whole spine of this entry: the restaurant and sky-gazing examples; the Anderson–Holt urn experiment (following Banerjee and Bikhchandani–Hirshleifer–Welch); Bayes’ rule as the decision engine; the general accept/reject–signal model and its “D escapes \pm1 \Rightarrow cascade” rule; the three lessons (cascades can be wrong, rest on little information, and are fragile); the explicit contrast with Surowiecki’s Wisdom of Crowds; and the separation of informational effects (Ch. 16) from direct-benefit / network effects (Ch. 17).

Appendix · the mathematics

One signal moves you off the prior. With prior \Pr[G]=p and signal quality \Pr[H\mid G]=q>\tfrac12, Bayes’ rule gives the posterior after a high signal:

\Pr[G\mid H] \;=\; \frac{\Pr[G]\,\Pr[H\mid G]}{\Pr[H]} \;=\; \frac{pq}{pq + (1-p)(1-q)}.

Since pq+(1-p)(1-q) < pq+(1-p)q = q, the denominator is below q, so \Pr[G\mid H] > p: a high signal always raises your belief that the option is good, and (given the indifference calibration of payoffs) tips you to accept. A low signal is symmetric and tips you to reject.

Many signals = a majority vote. For a sequence S with a high and b low signals, independence gives \Pr[S\mid G]=q^{a}(1-q)^{b} and \Pr[S\mid B]=(1-q)^{a}q^{b}, so

\Pr[G\mid S] \;=\; \frac{p\,q^{a}(1-q)^{b}}{p\,q^{a}(1-q)^{b} + (1-p)(1-q)^{a}q^{b}}.

Compare to p by asking whether replacing the second denominator term with (1-p)q^{a}(1-q)^{b} (which would collapse the whole expression to exactly p) makes the denominator larger or smaller. Because q>\tfrac12, more q-factors than (1-q)-factors — i.e. a>b — makes that term smaller than the true one, so the true posterior exceeds p. Hence \Pr[G\mid S]>p \iff a>b: accept iff high signals outnumber low.

The cascade condition. Because each pre-cascade actor follows (and thus reveals) their own signal, an observer can reconstruct the running signal tally, summarised by D=\#\text{accepts}-\#\text{rejects}. Your own single signal shifts the inferred tally by \pm1, so it can only change the majority verdict when |D|\le 1. Once |D|\ge 2, your signal cannot overturn the lead, so you rationally copy the majority; your action then reveals nothing, every later actor faces the same D, and the cascade is self-reinforcing — D only moves further from 0. The process is a random walk on D with a reflecting-then-absorbing behaviour at \pm 2.

Cascades are almost certain (but can be wrong). Split the population into blocks of three. Any block of three identical signals forces a cascade, and a block is all-identical with probability q^{3}+(1-q)^{3}>0. The probability that the first N people contain no such block is at most \bigl(1-q^{3}-(1-q)^{3}\bigr)^{N/3}\to 0, so \Pr[\text{a cascade has formed}]\to 1. Yet the cascade can lock onto the wrong state: with the option truly good, two early low signals (D\to-2) start a rejection cascade. That wrong-cascade probability is bounded away from 0 and does not shrink as the crowd grows — the plateau in the figure. This is the precise sense in which sequential observation destroys the large-sample convergence that independent aggregation enjoys.

Connections

  • Builds on: Bayes’ rule and the prior→posterior update — the decision engine every actor runs — and the law of large numbers, whose convergence the cascade defeats.
  • Contrasts with: spreading on networks — the structural cousin, where network shape (not sequential inference) decides whether a behaviour cascades; and complex contagion / threshold models there, which herd on payoffs rather than beliefs.
  • Leads to: agent-based models, which simulate populations of such Bayesian imitators, and the field-realities reading of metrics as feedback loops — best-seller lists and rankings that cascade on themselves.

  He now reasons as though he has seen *three* draws — blue, blue, red — a 2-to-1 majority for blue. So he
  should **guess blue, discarding his own red draw.** And crucially, the class hears only "blue" — his guess
  now carries *no* information about what he saw.
- **Student 4 onward** knows student 3's guess was uninformative, so she is in exactly his position. Whatever
  she draws, the two genuine blue signals outweigh it. She guesses blue too. **The cascade has begun**, and
  every remaining student guesses blue regardless of their marble.

Three lessons fall out of this toy, and the general model below confirms all three:

- **Cascades can be wrong.** If the urn is really majority-red, there is a $\tfrac13\cdot\tfrac13=\tfrac19$
  chance the first two students both draw blue — and then *everyone* guesses blue, forever, all wrong. Adding
  more students does not help: they all herd too.
- **Cascades rest on almost no information.** Once a cascade starts, private signals stop entering the public
  record. A crowd of 1000 may be acting on the two marbles the first two students happened to draw.
- **Cascades are fragile.** Because they rest on so little, they are easily broken. If students 50 and 51
  quietly *show* their red marbles to the room, the next student now has four real signals (two blue, two
  red) and follows her own draw again — a 49-guess run of "blue" overturned by two fresh facts.

## The general model: a random walk with a two-step cliff

Easley & Kleinberg abstract the urn into a model that fits adoption, voting, restaurant choice, or crime
[@easley_kleinberg]. The world is in one of two states — the option is a **good idea** ($G$) or a **bad
idea** ($B$) — with prior $\Pr[G]=p$. Each person privately gets a **high** signal ($H$, favouring accept)
or a **low** signal ($L$), with quality $q>\tfrac12$: $\Pr[H\mid G]=\Pr[L\mid B]=q$. Payoffs are set so that
with no signal you are exactly indifferent between accepting and rejecting.

Two facts drive everything. First, one signal tips a rational person: a high signal raises the posterior
$\Pr[G\mid H] = \tfrac{pq}{pq+(1-p)(1-q)} > p$, so you accept; a low signal, you reject
([derivation in the appendix](#appendix-the-mathematics)). Second, with a bag of signals — $a$ high and $b$
low — the posterior beats the prior exactly when $a>b$: **rational choice is a majority vote over the signals
you can infer.**

Now run it sequentially. Because everyone before a cascade is following their own signal, you can *infer*
their signals from their actions and add them to your own. Track the running difference

$$D = (\#\text{accepts}) - (\#\text{rejects}).$$

- While $|D|\le 1$, your single private signal can still swing the majority, so you **follow your own
  signal** — and reveal it.
- The instant $|D|$ reaches **2**, no single signal can overturn the lead. You **follow the crowd and
  ignore your signal** — and because everyone knows you did, your action reveals nothing. Everyone after you
  is in the identical position. **The cascade locks, and by self-reinforcement it never unlocks.**

So $D$ is a random walk that starts at 0 and drifts until it first escapes the ribbon $[-1,+1]$; the moment
it hits $\pm 2$ the population freezes onto that choice. And escape is essentially certain: three matching
signals in a row always trigger a cascade, and in a long enough sequence that is bound to happen — the
probability a cascade has formed **converges to 1** as the crowd grows [@easley_kleinberg]. What is *not*
guaranteed is that it forms on the *right* side.

## The wisdom of crowds runs backwards

Here is the punchline a quantitative researcher should carry away, and it directly contradicts the popular
"wisdom of crowds" story [@easley_kleinberg]. Surowiecki's jar-of-jelly-beans result — that averaging many
independent guesses is uncannily accurate — depends entirely on the guesses being **independent**. Each
person's private signal enters the pool. Pool enough of them and the [law of large
numbers](../01-foundations/law-of-large-numbers.qmd) / Condorcet logic drives the aggregate to the truth.

Let people **observe each other and decide in sequence**, and the same crowd stops learning. A cascade locks
in after a few signals; the thousandth person contributes a copy, not a signal. So the collective accuracy
**plateaus well below certainty and stops improving with size** — more people cannot rescue a herd. The
figure makes the contrast concrete.

```{python}
#| label: fig-cascade-vs-independent
#| fig-cap: "Two ways to aggregate a crowd's private information, both with signal quality q=0.70 and the option truly Good. INDEPENDENT (ink): each person acts on their own signal alone, without seeing others; the fraction of the crowd that ends up on the correct choice climbs toward certainty as the crowd grows — the wisdom of crowds (Condorcet). SEQUENTIAL HERDING (orange): each person sees the earlier actions and rationally updates; a cascade locks in after a handful of signals and the collective accuracy PLATEAUS far below 1 — adding people cannot fix a herd. The gap between the curves is the private information the cascade throws away. Monte Carlo, 6000 runs per crowd size."
#| code-fold: true
#| code-summary: "Show the figure code (both aggregation rules, by hand)"

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(16)
q = 0.70                       # signal quality; true state = Good, so P(high signal) = q
sizes = np.arange(1, 61)
RUNS = 6000

def independent_correct(n):
    # each person acts on own signal alone; "collective verdict" = majority action
    highs = rng.random((RUNS, n)) < q          # high signal favours the (correct) Accept
    maj_accept = highs.sum(axis=1) > (n / 2)    # strict majority accepts
    ties = highs.sum(axis=1) == (n / 2)         # break ties by a fair coin
    maj_accept = np.where(ties, rng.random(RUNS) < 0.5, maj_accept)
    return maj_accept.mean()

def herding_correct(n):
    # sequential: follow own signal while |D|<=1, else follow the crowd (cascade locks)
    sig = rng.random((RUNS, n)) < q
    correct = np.zeros(RUNS, bool)
    for r in range(RUNS):
        D = 0
        act = True
        for i in range(n):
            if abs(D) >= 2:
                act = D > 0                      # cascade: copy the lead, ignore signal
            else:
                act = bool(sig[r, i])            # informative: follow own signal
            D += 1 if act else -1
        correct[r] = act                         # the herd's standing verdict = last action
    return correct.mean()

ind = np.array([independent_correct(n) for n in sizes])
herd = np.array([herding_correct(n) for n in sizes])

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7.2, 4.3))
ax.axhline(1.0, color="#b7b6a8", lw=0.8, ls=":")
ax.plot(sizes, ind, color="#161613", lw=2.3, label="independent — pool private signals")
ax.plot(sizes, herd, color="#e8531d", lw=2.4, label="sequential herding — observe & copy")
ax.annotate("cascade wastes\nthe crowd's information",
            xy=(52, herd[51]), xytext=(30, 0.72), fontsize=8.5, color="#e8531d",
            arrowprops=dict(arrowstyle="->", color="#e8531d", lw=1.1))
ax.set_xlabel("number of decision-makers")
ax.set_ylabel("P(crowd's verdict is correct)")
ax.set_title("More people rescue independent guesses, but not a herd")
ax.set_ylim(0.5, 1.02)
ax.legend(fontsize=8.5, frameon=False, loc="center right")
fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

## Informational effects vs network effects

The imitation in a cascade is **informational** — you copy others because their actions carry news. Easley &
Kleinberg contrast this sharply with **direct-benefit** or **network effects**, where you copy others because
their choice *directly* changes your payoff [@easley_kleinberg]. A fax machine, an operating system, a social
platform: each is more valuable the more others use it — a **positive externality**. The two mechanisms look
identical from outside (everyone ends up on the same option) but behave differently in one respect that
matters for policy:

- **Cascades are fragile** — a little fresh public information overturns even a long run, because the run was
  built on so little.
- **Network effects are sticky** — once a standard tips, its advantage is self-perpetuating and *hard to
  reverse*, even for a technically superior rival, because the benefit is real, not inferential.

Telling them apart in the field is exactly the diagnostic question: is the crowd giving you information, or
giving you compatibility? [Spreading on networks](spreading-on-networks.qmd) takes the *structural* version
of this story — how the shape of the contact network decides whether a behaviour cascades — where this entry
takes the *informational* version on a sequence of decision-makers.

## Common pitfalls

::: {.callout-warning title="Where this goes wrong"}
- **"They can't all be wrong."** A unanimous crowd can rest on two early signals. Length of a run is *not*
  evidence of its strength once a cascade is possible. [@easley_kleinberg]
- **Going around the table.** Polling a committee in sequence manufactures cascades: a few early voices for A
  can rationally swing the rest, even against their own read. Collect independent judgements *first*, then
  discuss — and let the best-informed people speak last, not first. [@easley_kleinberg]
- **Mistaking a cascade for wisdom-of-crowds.** Averaging works only for **independent** signals; the moment
  people see each other's choices, the aggregate can stop tracking the truth. [@easley_kleinberg]
- **Confusing herding with conformity.** Cascades need no social pressure — pure rational inference produces
  them. The cure (inject public information) differs from the cure for conformity (protect dissent). [@easley_kleinberg]
- **Assuming more data fixes it.** A larger crowd does *not* break a cascade; only *new information* that
  enters the public record does (revealed payoffs, an audit, a forced independent report). [@easley_kleinberg]
:::

## Appendix · the mathematics {.unnumbered}

::: {.math-appendix}

**One signal moves you off the prior.** With prior $\Pr[G]=p$ and signal quality $\Pr[H\mid G]=q>\tfrac12$,
Bayes' rule gives the posterior after a high signal:

$$
\Pr[G\mid H] \;=\; \frac{\Pr[G]\,\Pr[H\mid G]}{\Pr[H]}
\;=\; \frac{pq}{pq + (1-p)(1-q)}.
$$

Since $pq+(1-p)(1-q) < pq+(1-p)q = q$, the denominator is below $q$, so $\Pr[G\mid H] > p$: a high signal
always raises your belief that the option is good, and (given the indifference calibration of payoffs) tips
you to **accept**. A low signal is symmetric and tips you to **reject**.

**Many signals = a majority vote.** For a sequence $S$ with $a$ high and $b$ low signals, independence gives
$\Pr[S\mid G]=q^{a}(1-q)^{b}$ and $\Pr[S\mid B]=(1-q)^{a}q^{b}$, so

$$
\Pr[G\mid S] \;=\; \frac{p\,q^{a}(1-q)^{b}}{p\,q^{a}(1-q)^{b} + (1-p)(1-q)^{a}q^{b}}.
$$

Compare to $p$ by asking whether replacing the second denominator term with $(1-p)q^{a}(1-q)^{b}$ (which
would collapse the whole expression to exactly $p$) makes the denominator larger or smaller. Because
$q>\tfrac12$, more $q$-factors than $(1-q)$-factors — i.e. $a>b$ — makes that term *smaller* than the true
one, so the true posterior exceeds $p$. Hence $\Pr[G\mid S]>p \iff a>b$: **accept iff high signals
outnumber low.**

**The cascade condition.** Because each pre-cascade actor follows (and thus reveals) their own signal, an
observer can reconstruct the running signal tally, summarised by $D=\#\text{accepts}-\#\text{rejects}$. Your
own single signal shifts the inferred tally by $\pm1$, so it can only change the majority verdict when
$|D|\le 1$. Once $|D|\ge 2$, your signal cannot overturn the lead, so you rationally copy the majority; your
action then reveals nothing, every later actor faces the same $D$, and the cascade is **self-reinforcing** —
$D$ only moves further from 0. The process is a random walk on $D$ with a reflecting-then-absorbing behaviour
at $\pm 2$.

**Cascades are almost certain (but can be wrong).** Split the population into blocks of three. Any block of
three identical signals forces a cascade, and a block is all-identical with probability $q^{3}+(1-q)^{3}>0$.
The probability that the first $N$ people contain *no* such block is at most
$\bigl(1-q^{3}-(1-q)^{3}\bigr)^{N/3}\to 0$, so $\Pr[\text{a cascade has formed}]\to 1$. Yet the cascade can
lock onto the wrong state: with the option truly good, two early low signals ($D\to-2$) start a *rejection*
cascade. That wrong-cascade probability is bounded away from 0 and **does not shrink as the crowd grows** —
the plateau in the figure. This is the precise sense in which sequential observation destroys the
[large-sample](../01-foundations/law-of-large-numbers.qmd) convergence that independent aggregation enjoys.

:::
