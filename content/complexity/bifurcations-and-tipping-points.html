Bifurcations & Tipping Points

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source

Turn a knob smoothly and, at a precise value, the system’s whole repertoire of resting states changes — equilibria are born, die, or swap stability. That is where sudden jumps, thresholds, and irreversible collapses come from.

A smooth cause can have a sudden effect. Let a parameter drift slowly — a forest growing, a price creeping, a policy tightening — and the system’s equilibria drift with it, until two of them collide and vanish. The state that was sitting there has nowhere left to sit, and it jumps. Worse, turning the knob back does not undo the jump: the way in is not the way out. That lack of reversibility is hysteresis, and it is the mathematical skeleton of every “tipping point”.

Why it matters

Social scientists constantly reason as if effects were smooth and reversible: a bit more of X gives a bit more of Y, and removing the extra X removes the extra Y. Regression coefficients, elasticities and marginal effects all bake in that assumption. Many of the systems we study — markets, forests, norms, neighbourhoods, epidemics — are instead nonlinear dynamical systems, and for those the interesting question is not “how big is the slope?” but “how many stable states are there, and when does that number change?”

Strogatz’s answer is the theory of bifurcations: qualitative changes in a system’s dynamics as a control parameter is varied — fixed points created or destroyed, or their stability flipped (Strogatz 2015). A bifurcation is where a gentle, continuous push produces a discontinuous outcome. It explains three things that linear thinking cannot: thresholds (below this point, nothing; above it, everything), sudden collapse with no proportionate trigger, and path dependence (two places facing the same conditions today can sit in completely different states because of where they came from).

This entry is the one-dimensional, fixed-point companion to nonlinear dynamics & chaos: that entry follows the logistic map through period-doubling into chaos; this one stays with smooth flows and asks the simpler, more frequently useful question of how equilibria themselves appear and disappear.

Equilibria that move with a parameter

Start with a one-variable system \dot x = f(x) — the population, the share adopting a behaviour, the deflection of a beam. Its fixed points x^\* are where f(x^\*)=0; a fixed point is stable when nearby states flow back to it, which on a line means f'(x^\*)<0 (Strogatz 2015). On a line the dynamics are almost boring: every trajectory either settles at a fixed point or runs off to infinity. What makes one-dimensional systems interesting, Strogatz argues, is dependence on parameters (Strogatz 2015).

His opening picture is a beam under a weight. A light load and the vertical beam stays upright; load it past some critical weight and the upright position becomes unstable and the beam buckles (Strogatz 2015). The weight is the control parameter; the deflection is the state. Nothing about the weight changes discontinuously — you add it a gram at a time — yet the beam’s behaviour does.

The tool for seeing this is the bifurcation diagram: put the parameter r on the horizontal axis and plot every fixed point x^\* above it, solid where stable, dashed where unstable (Strogatz 2015). As you read left to right, branches appear, merge and split. Every place where the count or the stability of branches changes is a bifurcation point.

The three basic ways a system can change its mind

Strogatz catalogues the elementary bifurcations of fixed points on a line (Strogatz 2015). Each has a simplest “normal form” that every example near that bifurcation looks like locally.

Saddle-node (fold): equilibria collide and annihilate. In \dot x = r + x^2, for r<0 there are two fixed points, one stable and one unstable. As r rises toward 0 they slide together, merge into a single half-stable point at r=0, and for r>0 there are no fixed points at all (Strogatz 2015). This is “the basic mechanism by which fixed points are created and destroyed.” Read backwards it is a “blue-sky bifurcation”: a pair of equilibria appears out of nowhere (Strogatz 2015). The social reading is stark: a stable status quo can simply cease to exist as conditions drift, and the system must go somewhere else.

Transcritical: equilibria exchange stability. Two fixed points pass through each other and swap roles — the one that was stable becomes unstable and vice versa (Strogatz 2015). Strogatz’s example is the laser threshold: below a critical pump strength the zero-photon state is stable and the device “acts like a lamp”; above it, that state loses stability and a stable state of laser action appears (Strogatz 2015). Think of any process with an “extinct/inactive” state that is always available but only stable below a threshold.

Pitchfork: one state splits into two symmetric ones. In the supercritical case, \dot x = rx - x^3, the origin is the only fixed point for r<0; at r=0 it goes unstable and a symmetric pair of new stable states branches off smoothly (Strogatz 2015). The system must “choose a side”, but it does so gently: the new states are born at small amplitude. The subcritical case is the dangerous twin, to which we turn next.

Hysteresis: the way in is not the way out

Flip the sign of the cubic term — \dot x = rx + x^3 - x^5 — and the pitchfork turns into something treacherous (Strogatz 2015). Now, over a range of r below zero, two different stable states coexist: the origin, and a pair of large-amplitude states. The origin is stable to small nudges but not to large ones — locally stable, not globally (Strogatz 2015).

Follow the system as you slowly raise r. It sits at the origin until r=0, where the origin loses stability; the slightest nudge sends it jumping out to the large-amplitude branch. Now lower r back below zero. The system stays on the large branch — even though the origin is stable again. You have to lower r further still, past the point where the large branch itself disappears in a saddle-node, before the state drops back (Strogatz 2015). Strogatz names this “lack of reversibility as a parameter is varied” hysteresis (Strogatz 2015).

Engineers have a blunt vocabulary for the two pitchforks: the supercritical one is soft or safe (new states are born small), the subcritical one hard or dangerous (a jump from zero to large amplitude) (Strogatz 2015). In the language of physics they correspond to continuous versus discontinuous phase transitions (Strogatz 2015). The practical lesson travels well beyond physics: knowing that a system is stable today tells you nothing about whether it is safe. What matters is whether the stable state you are in is the only one, and how close it is to a fold.

Worked example: the spruce budworm outbreak

Strogatz’s showcase is ecological, but its structure is exactly the shape of the social-science questions above. The spruce budworm is a pest of balsam fir in eastern Canada; when an outbreak occurs the budworms can defoliate and kill most of the firs in a forest in about four years (Strogatz 2015). Ludwig and colleagues modelled it with three ingredients (Strogatz 2015):

  • the budworm population grows logistically toward a carrying capacity set by the foliage;
  • birds eat budworms, but predation is a switch: almost none when budworms are scarce (the birds feed elsewhere), turning on sharply above a critical density and then saturating (the birds are eating as fast as they can);
  • the two processes run on separated time scales: budworms can multiply fivefold in a year, while trees take 7–10 years to replace their foliage and live 100–150 years. So for the budworm dynamics the forest can be treated as fixed — and then allowed to drift very slowly.

After rescaling to two dimensionless parameters, a growth-rate r and a carrying capacity k, the model has (for large enough k) three positive equilibria: a stable low refuge level, a stable high outbreak level, and an unstable level between them that acts as a threshold: start above it and an outbreak follows (Strogatz 2015). As the forest matures, the trees’ branch area grows and pushes r upward, carrying the system through the bistable region toward the region where only the outbreak level exists. Ludwig et al. estimate a young forest at roughly k\approx300, r<\tfrac12 (bistable, budworms kept down by birds who can easily search the few branches) and a mature forest at r\approx1, “dangerously in the outbreak region” (Strogatz 2015).

Two features make this a tipping point rather than just a threshold. First, the outbreak is triggered by a saddle-node: the refuge equilibrium collides with the threshold and vanishes, so the population jumps even though nothing dramatic happened to the forest that year. Second, hysteresis: even if conditions are restored to what they were before the outbreak, the population will not drop back to the refuge level (Strogatz 2015). In the real system recovery comes only through the slow forest cycle — the firs die, birch takes over, and the firs return over roughly 50–100 years (Strogatz 2015).

Show the figure code (fixed points + a slow parameter sweep, by hand) 
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

k = 10.0                                   # dimensionless carrying capacity, held fixed
def f(x, r):                               # Ludwig et al. budworm model, dimensionless form
    return r * x * (1 - x / k) - x**2 / (1 + x**2)

# Every equilibrium: solve r(1 - x/k) = x/(1 + x^2) for r, sweeping x
xs = np.linspace(0.05, 9.5, 4000)
rs = xs / ((1 + xs**2) * (1 - xs / k))
eps = 1e-5
stable = (f(xs + eps, rs) - f(xs - eps, rs)) / (2 * eps) < 0   # f'(x*) < 0 means stable

# the two folds (saddle-node points) are where the curve turns back: dr/dx = 0
drdx = np.gradient(rs, xs)
folds = np.where(np.diff(np.sign(drdx)) != 0)[0]

# slowly push r up, then bring it back down, integrating the fast population dynamics
r_min, r_max, T, dt = 0.30, 0.70, 3000.0, 0.05
r_up = np.linspace(r_min, r_max, int(T / dt))
r_dn = r_up[::-1]
def sweep(rpath, x0):
    x, out = x0, np.empty(len(rpath))
    for i, r in enumerate(rpath):
        x += dt * f(x, r)
        out[i] = x
    return out
x_up = sweep(r_up, 0.36)
x_dn = sweep(r_dn, x_up[-1])

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7.2, 4.5))
lo, hi = rs[folds].min(), rs[folds].max()
ax.axvspan(lo, hi, color="#dfe2d0", alpha=0.5, lw=0)
ax.text((lo + hi) / 2, 9.05, "bistable: two stable states coexist",
        ha="center", fontsize=8.5, color="#5e5e54")
# equilibrium branches: solid = stable, dashed = unstable (the threshold)
start = 0
for i in range(1, len(xs) + 1):
    if i == len(xs) or stable[i] != stable[start]:
        st = stable[start]
        ax.plot(rs[start:i], xs[start:i], color="#161613",
                lw=2.2 if st else 1.2, ls="-" if st else "--")
        start = i
# the system's actual path under the slow sweep
ax.plot(r_up, x_up, color="#e8531d", lw=1.5)
ax.plot(r_dn, x_dn, color="#e8531d", lw=1.5)
j_up, j_dn = np.argmax(np.diff(x_up)), np.argmin(np.diff(x_dn))
ax.annotate("", xy=(r_up[j_up], 6.6), xytext=(r_up[j_up], 1.5),
            arrowprops=dict(arrowstyle="-|>", color="#e8531d", lw=1.4))
ax.annotate("", xy=(r_dn[j_dn], 0.9), xytext=(r_dn[j_dn], 5.3),
            arrowprops=dict(arrowstyle="-|>", color="#e8531d", lw=1.4))
for fi in folds:
    ax.plot(rs[fi], xs[fi], "o", ms=6, color="#161613", mfc="#f4f3ec", mew=1.4)
ax.text(0.585, 1.05, "outbreak: refuge\nbranch vanishes", fontsize=8.5, color="#e8531d")
ax.text(0.31, 6.2, "recovery only once r falls\npast the OTHER fold", fontsize=8.5, color="#e8531d")
ax.text(0.47, 3.0, "unstable threshold", fontsize=8.5, color="#5e5e54", rotation=-28)
ax.text(0.615, 7.3, "outbreak level", fontsize=8.5, color="#161613")
ax.text(0.305, 0.75, "refuge level", fontsize=8.5, color="#161613")
ax.set_xlim(r_min, r_max); ax.set_ylim(0, 9.5)
ax.set_xlabel("slowly drifting parameter  r  (forest growth raises it)")
ax.set_ylabel("budworm population  x  (dimensionless)")
ax.set_title("Hysteresis: the way in is not the way out")
fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()

Notice that the orange jumps happen slightly after the folds. That lag is not numerical sloppiness; it is the next idea.

Warning signs: slowing down near a fold

How fast a system recovers from a small knock is set by the slope of f at its equilibrium: a perturbation decays roughly like e^{f'(x^\*)\,t} (Strogatz 2015). At a saddle-node the stable and unstable points merge and the linearisation vanishes, f'(x^\*)=0 (Strogatz 2015). So as a system drifts toward a fold, its restoring force weakens and it takes longer and longer to recover from shocks. Strogatz notes the physicists’ name for this, critical slowing down — at the transition, the system relaxes to equilibrium much more slowly than usual (Strogatz 2015). It is the same fingerprint the self-organised criticality entry describes as an early-warning signal.

Even after the fold, the vanished equilibria leave a trace. Just past a saddle-node there is a “saddle-node remnant or ghost” that creates a bottleneck: trajectories crawl through the region where the fixed points used to be before racing away (Strogatz 2015). The time spent there scales like 1/\sqrt{r - r_c}, the “square-root scaling law” (Strogatz 2015). That is why the figure’s jumps lag the folds, and why a system that has already tipped can look deceptively calm for a while.

Two knobs: the cusp

Real systems rarely have perfect symmetry. Add a small imperfection h to the pitchfork, \dot x = h + rx - x^3, and the clean split breaks into two disconnected pieces (Strogatz 2015). Now there are two control parameters, and the right picture is a stability diagram in the (r,h) plane: two curves of saddle-node bifurcations meeting tangentially at a cusp point, with three equilibria inside the wedge and one outside (Strogatz 2015). Stack the equilibria above that plane and you get the folded cusp catastrophe surface; the “catastrophe” is the moment the state is carried over the edge of the upper sheet and drops discontinuously to the lower one (Strogatz 2015). The budworm’s (k,r) stability diagram, with its refuge, bistable and outbreak regions, has exactly this shape (Strogatz 2015).

Strogatz flags that catastrophe theory provoked “a violent controversy” in the late 1970s (Strogatz 2015) — partly over its enthusiastic export to the social sciences. The lesson for us is not to avoid the geometry but to earn it: a cusp is a claim about specific, modellable feedbacks, not a metaphor to be draped over any sudden change.

Common pitfalls

WarningWhere this goes wrong
  • Calling every rapid change a “tipping point”. A tipping point in the precise sense is a fold: a stable state that ceases to exist as a parameter drifts. A steep but smooth response (an S-shaped curve with a single equilibrium) is fast but reversible. Ask whether there are two stable states before using the word.
  • Assuming reversibility. Under hysteresis, undoing the cause does not undo the effect — the budworm population stays at the outbreak level even when conditions are restored (Strogatz 2015). Policy that “reverses the shock” may need to overshoot the original conditions, or wait out a slow variable.
  • Reading local stability as safety. A state can be stable to small disturbances but not to large ones (Strogatz 2015); a big enough shock can kick a system across the threshold even far from any fold.
  • Ignoring time scales. The budworm analysis works because the forest moves orders of magnitude more slowly than the insects (Strogatz 2015). When the slow and fast variables are not well separated, the bifurcation diagram is a guide, not a forecast.
  • Fitting a linear slope across a bifurcation. Pooling observations from both sides of a fold, or from both branches of a bistable region, averages two different regimes into a coefficient that describes neither. Path dependence means x depends on history, not just on today’s r.
  • Over-selling catastrophe geometry. The cusp is a real mathematical object, but its social-science applications drew sharp criticism (Strogatz 2015). Derive it from mechanisms, don’t assert it.

Appendix · the mathematics

Linear stability on a line. For \dot x=f(x) with f(x^\*)=0, write x=x^\*+\eta. Taylor-expanding, \dot\eta \approx f'(x^\*)\,\eta, so \eta(t)\approx\eta_0 e^{f'(x^\*)t}: the fixed point is stable if f'(x^\*)<0, unstable if f'(x^\*)>0, and the characteristic recovery time is 1/|f'(x^\*)|. When f'(x^\*)\to0 that time diverges, which is critical slowing down.

The saddle-node normal form. For \dot x=r-x^2: fixed points x^\*=\pm\sqrt r exist only for r\ge0, and f'(x^\*)=-2x^\*, so +\sqrt r is stable and -\sqrt r unstable. At r=0 both merge and f'=0. Near any generic fold the dynamics reduce to this form by Taylor expansion — e.g. \dot x = r-x-e^{-x} near (x,r)=(0,1) gives \dot x\approx(r-1)-\tfrac12x^2.

The bottleneck time. Just past the fold, \dot x=r+x^2 with small r>0. The time to pass through is

T_{\text{bottleneck}}\approx\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dx}{r+x^2}=\frac{\pi}{\sqrt r},

so passage time diverges like (r-r_c)^{-1/2} as the parameter approaches the fold from the far side.

Tangency gives the bifurcation. A saddle-node occurs where a fixed point is also a double root: f(x^\*;r)=0 and \partial f/\partial x\,(x^\*;r)=0. For the imperfect pitchfork \dot x=h+rx-x^3, the cubic rx-x^3 peaks at x=\sqrt{r/3} with height \tfrac{2r}{3}\sqrt{r/3}, so folds occur at

h=\pm h_c(r),\qquad h_c(r)=\frac{2r}{3}\sqrt{\frac r3},

two curves meeting tangentially at the cusp (r,h)=(0,0).

The budworm bifurcation curves. In dimensionless form, \dot x = rx\!\left(1-\tfrac{x}{k}\right)-\dfrac{x^2}{1+x^2}. Nonzero equilibria satisfy r\!\left(1-\tfrac{x}{k}\right)=\dfrac{x}{1+x^2}, a straight line meeting a fixed curve. Requiring tangency (equal values and equal slopes) and eliminating r/k gives the curves parametrically in x>1:

r(x)=\frac{2x^3}{(1+x^2)^2},\qquad k(x)=\frac{2x^3}{x^2-1}.

For k=10 (the figure) the two folds sit at r\approx0.384 and r\approx0.560; between them the refuge and outbreak levels coexist. The minimum of k(x), at x=\sqrt3, is k=3\sqrt3\approx5.2: below that carrying capacity the line can only cross the curve once and there is no bistability at all — the cusp point.

::: {.lead-kernel}
*A smooth cause can have a sudden effect.* Let a parameter drift slowly — a forest growing, a price creeping,
a policy tightening — and the system's equilibria drift with it, until two of them **collide and vanish**.
The state that was sitting there has nowhere left to sit, and it **jumps**. Worse, turning the knob back
does not undo the jump: the way in is not the way out. That lack of reversibility is **hysteresis**, and it
is the mathematical skeleton of every "tipping point".
:::

## Why it matters

Social scientists constantly reason as if effects were smooth and reversible: a bit more of $X$ gives a bit
more of $Y$, and removing the extra $X$ removes the extra $Y$. Regression coefficients, elasticities and
marginal effects all bake in that assumption. Many of the systems we study — markets, forests, norms,
neighbourhoods, epidemics — are instead **nonlinear dynamical systems**, and for those the interesting
question is not "how big is the slope?" but "**how many stable states are there, and when does that number
change?**"

Strogatz's answer is the theory of **bifurcations**: qualitative changes in a system's dynamics as a control
parameter is varied — fixed points created or destroyed, or their stability flipped
[@strogatz_nonlinear]. A bifurcation is where a gentle, continuous push produces a discontinuous outcome. It
explains three things that linear thinking cannot: **thresholds** (below this point, nothing; above it,
everything), **sudden collapse** with no proportionate trigger, and **path dependence** (two places facing
the *same* conditions today can sit in completely different states because of where they came from).

This entry is the one-dimensional, fixed-point companion to [nonlinear dynamics &
chaos](nonlinear-dynamics-and-chaos.qmd): that entry follows the logistic *map* through period-doubling into
chaos; this one stays with smooth flows and asks the simpler, more frequently useful question of how
equilibria themselves appear and disappear.

## Equilibria that move with a parameter

Start with a one-variable system $\dot x = f(x)$ — the population, the share adopting a behaviour, the
deflection of a beam. Its **fixed points** $x^\*$ are where $f(x^\*)=0$; a fixed point is **stable** when
nearby states flow back to it, which on a line means $f'(x^\*)<0$ [@strogatz_nonlinear]. On a line the
dynamics are almost boring: every trajectory either settles at a fixed point or runs off to infinity. What
makes one-dimensional systems interesting, Strogatz argues, is **dependence on parameters**
[@strogatz_nonlinear].

His opening picture is a **beam under a weight**. A light load and the vertical beam stays upright; load it
past some critical weight and the upright position becomes unstable and the beam **buckles**
[@strogatz_nonlinear]. The weight is the **control parameter**; the deflection is the state. Nothing about
the weight changes discontinuously — you add it a gram at a time — yet the beam's behaviour does.

The tool for seeing this is the **bifurcation diagram**: put the parameter $r$ on the horizontal axis and
plot every fixed point $x^\*$ above it, solid where stable, dashed where unstable [@strogatz_nonlinear]. As
you read left to right, branches appear, merge and split. Every place where the *count* or the *stability*
of branches changes is a bifurcation point.

## The three basic ways a system can change its mind

Strogatz catalogues the elementary bifurcations of fixed points on a line [@strogatz_nonlinear]. Each has a
simplest "normal form" that every example near that bifurcation looks like locally.

**Saddle-node (fold): equilibria collide and annihilate.** In $\dot x = r + x^2$, for $r<0$ there are two
fixed points, one stable and one unstable. As $r$ rises toward 0 they slide together, merge into a single
half-stable point at $r=0$, and for $r>0$ there are **no fixed points at all** [@strogatz_nonlinear]. This is
"the basic mechanism by which fixed points are created and destroyed." Read backwards it is a
"blue-sky bifurcation": a pair of equilibria appears out of nowhere [@strogatz_nonlinear]. The social
reading is stark: a stable status quo can simply **cease to exist** as conditions drift, and the system
must go somewhere else.

**Transcritical: equilibria exchange stability.** Two fixed points pass through each other and swap roles —
the one that was stable becomes unstable and vice versa [@strogatz_nonlinear]. Strogatz's example is the
**laser threshold**: below a critical pump strength the zero-photon state is stable and the device "acts
like a lamp"; above it, that state loses stability and a stable state of laser action appears
[@strogatz_nonlinear]. Think of any process with an
"extinct/inactive" state that is always available but only *stable* below a threshold.

**Pitchfork: one state splits into two symmetric ones.** In the **supercritical** case, $\dot x = rx - x^3$,
the origin is the only fixed point for $r<0$; at $r=0$ it goes unstable and a symmetric pair of new stable
states branches off smoothly [@strogatz_nonlinear]. The system must "choose a side", but it does so gently:
the new states are born at small amplitude. The **subcritical** case is the dangerous twin, to which we turn
next.

## Hysteresis: the way in is not the way out

Flip the sign of the cubic term — $\dot x = rx + x^3 - x^5$ — and the pitchfork turns into something
treacherous [@strogatz_nonlinear]. Now, over a range of $r$ below zero, **two different stable states
coexist**: the origin, and a pair of large-amplitude states. The origin is stable to small nudges but not to
large ones — *locally* stable, not *globally* [@strogatz_nonlinear].

Follow the system as you slowly raise $r$. It sits at the origin until $r=0$, where the origin loses
stability; the slightest nudge sends it **jumping** out to the large-amplitude branch. Now lower $r$ back
below zero. The system **stays** on the large branch — even though the origin is stable again. You have to
lower $r$ further still, past the point where the large branch itself disappears in a saddle-node, before
the state drops back [@strogatz_nonlinear]. Strogatz names this "lack of reversibility as a parameter is
varied" **hysteresis** [@strogatz_nonlinear].

Engineers have a blunt vocabulary for the two pitchforks: the supercritical one is **soft** or **safe**
(new states are born small), the subcritical one **hard** or **dangerous** (a jump from zero to large
amplitude) [@strogatz_nonlinear]. In the language of physics they correspond to continuous versus
discontinuous phase transitions [@strogatz_nonlinear]. The practical lesson travels well beyond physics:
**knowing that a system is stable today tells you nothing about whether it is safe**. What matters is
whether the stable state you are in is the only one, and how close it is to a fold.

## Worked example: the spruce budworm outbreak

Strogatz's showcase is ecological, but its structure is exactly the shape of the social-science questions
above. The spruce budworm is a pest of balsam fir in eastern Canada; when an outbreak occurs the budworms
can defoliate and kill most of the firs in a forest in about four years [@strogatz_nonlinear]. Ludwig and
colleagues modelled it with three ingredients [@strogatz_nonlinear]:

- the budworm population grows **logistically** toward a carrying capacity set by the foliage;
- birds eat budworms, but predation is a **switch**: almost none when budworms are scarce (the birds feed
  elsewhere), turning on sharply above a critical density and then **saturating** (the birds are eating as
  fast as they can);
- the two processes run on **separated time scales**: budworms can multiply fivefold in a year, while trees
  take 7–10 years to replace their foliage and live 100–150 years. So for the budworm dynamics the forest
  can be treated as fixed — and then allowed to drift very slowly.

After rescaling to two dimensionless parameters, a growth-rate $r$ and a carrying capacity $k$, the model
has (for large enough $k$) **three** positive equilibria: a stable low **refuge** level, a stable high
**outbreak** level, and an unstable level between them that acts as a **threshold**: start above it and an
outbreak follows [@strogatz_nonlinear]. As the forest matures, the trees' branch area grows and pushes $r$
upward, carrying the system through the **bistable** region toward the region where only the outbreak level
exists. Ludwig et al. estimate a young forest at roughly $k\approx300$, $r<\tfrac12$ (bistable, budworms kept
down by birds who can easily search the few branches) and a mature forest at $r\approx1$, "dangerously in
the outbreak region" [@strogatz_nonlinear].

Two features make this a *tipping point* rather than just a threshold. First, the outbreak is **triggered by
a saddle-node**: the refuge equilibrium collides with the threshold and vanishes, so the population jumps
even though nothing dramatic happened to the forest that year. Second, **hysteresis**: even if conditions are
restored to what they were before the outbreak, the population will not drop back to the refuge level
[@strogatz_nonlinear]. In the real system recovery comes only through the slow forest cycle — the firs die,
birch takes over, and the firs return over roughly 50–100 years [@strogatz_nonlinear].

```{python}
#| label: fig-hysteresis
#| fig-cap: "The budworm model's bifurcation diagram, computed by hand (carrying capacity k = 10 held fixed). Ink: every equilibrium population as a function of the slowly drifting parameter r — solid where stable (the low refuge branch and the high outbreak branch), dashed where unstable (the threshold between them). The two open circles are saddle-node folds; between them (shaded) the system is bistable. Orange: what the population actually does when r is pushed slowly up from 0.30 to 0.70 and then back down, integrating the fast budworm dynamics at every step. On the way up it hugs the refuge branch until that branch vanishes at the right-hand fold and then jumps to the outbreak level; on the way down it stays at the outbreak level across the whole bistable band and only collapses back at the left-hand fold. Same parameter values, two different states: hysteresis."
#| code-fold: true
#| code-summary: "Show the figure code (fixed points + a slow parameter sweep, by hand)"

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

k = 10.0                                   # dimensionless carrying capacity, held fixed
def f(x, r):                               # Ludwig et al. budworm model, dimensionless form
    return r * x * (1 - x / k) - x**2 / (1 + x**2)

# Every equilibrium: solve r(1 - x/k) = x/(1 + x^2) for r, sweeping x
xs = np.linspace(0.05, 9.5, 4000)
rs = xs / ((1 + xs**2) * (1 - xs / k))
eps = 1e-5
stable = (f(xs + eps, rs) - f(xs - eps, rs)) / (2 * eps) < 0   # f'(x*) < 0 means stable

# the two folds (saddle-node points) are where the curve turns back: dr/dx = 0
drdx = np.gradient(rs, xs)
folds = np.where(np.diff(np.sign(drdx)) != 0)[0]

# slowly push r up, then bring it back down, integrating the fast population dynamics
r_min, r_max, T, dt = 0.30, 0.70, 3000.0, 0.05
r_up = np.linspace(r_min, r_max, int(T / dt))
r_dn = r_up[::-1]
def sweep(rpath, x0):
    x, out = x0, np.empty(len(rpath))
    for i, r in enumerate(rpath):
        x += dt * f(x, r)
        out[i] = x
    return out
x_up = sweep(r_up, 0.36)
x_dn = sweep(r_dn, x_up[-1])

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7.2, 4.5))
lo, hi = rs[folds].min(), rs[folds].max()
ax.axvspan(lo, hi, color="#dfe2d0", alpha=0.5, lw=0)
ax.text((lo + hi) / 2, 9.05, "bistable: two stable states coexist",
        ha="center", fontsize=8.5, color="#5e5e54")
# equilibrium branches: solid = stable, dashed = unstable (the threshold)
start = 0
for i in range(1, len(xs) + 1):
    if i == len(xs) or stable[i] != stable[start]:
        st = stable[start]
        ax.plot(rs[start:i], xs[start:i], color="#161613",
                lw=2.2 if st else 1.2, ls="-" if st else "--")
        start = i
# the system's actual path under the slow sweep
ax.plot(r_up, x_up, color="#e8531d", lw=1.5)
ax.plot(r_dn, x_dn, color="#e8531d", lw=1.5)
j_up, j_dn = np.argmax(np.diff(x_up)), np.argmin(np.diff(x_dn))
ax.annotate("", xy=(r_up[j_up], 6.6), xytext=(r_up[j_up], 1.5),
            arrowprops=dict(arrowstyle="-|>", color="#e8531d", lw=1.4))
ax.annotate("", xy=(r_dn[j_dn], 0.9), xytext=(r_dn[j_dn], 5.3),
            arrowprops=dict(arrowstyle="-|>", color="#e8531d", lw=1.4))
for fi in folds:
    ax.plot(rs[fi], xs[fi], "o", ms=6, color="#161613", mfc="#f4f3ec", mew=1.4)
ax.text(0.585, 1.05, "outbreak: refuge\nbranch vanishes", fontsize=8.5, color="#e8531d")
ax.text(0.31, 6.2, "recovery only once r falls\npast the OTHER fold", fontsize=8.5, color="#e8531d")
ax.text(0.47, 3.0, "unstable threshold", fontsize=8.5, color="#5e5e54", rotation=-28)
ax.text(0.615, 7.3, "outbreak level", fontsize=8.5, color="#161613")
ax.text(0.305, 0.75, "refuge level", fontsize=8.5, color="#161613")
ax.set_xlim(r_min, r_max); ax.set_ylim(0, 9.5)
ax.set_xlabel("slowly drifting parameter  r  (forest growth raises it)")
ax.set_ylabel("budworm population  x  (dimensionless)")
ax.set_title("Hysteresis: the way in is not the way out")
fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

Notice that the orange jumps happen slightly *after* the folds. That lag is not numerical sloppiness; it is
the next idea.

## Warning signs: slowing down near a fold

How fast a system recovers from a small knock is set by the slope of $f$ at its equilibrium: a perturbation
decays roughly like $e^{f'(x^\*)\,t}$ [@strogatz_nonlinear]. At a saddle-node the stable and unstable points
merge and the linearisation **vanishes**, $f'(x^\*)=0$ [@strogatz_nonlinear]. So as a system drifts toward a
fold, its restoring force weakens and it takes **longer and longer to recover** from shocks. Strogatz notes
the physicists' name for this, **critical slowing down** — at the transition, the system relaxes to
equilibrium much more slowly than usual [@strogatz_nonlinear]. It is the same fingerprint the
[self-organised criticality](self-organised-criticality.qmd) entry describes as an early-warning signal.

Even *after* the fold, the vanished equilibria leave a trace. Just past a saddle-node there is a "saddle-node
remnant or **ghost**" that creates a **bottleneck**: trajectories crawl through the region where the fixed
points used to be before racing away [@strogatz_nonlinear]. The time spent there scales like
$1/\sqrt{r - r_c}$, the "square-root scaling law" [@strogatz_nonlinear]. That is why the figure's jumps lag
the folds, and why a system that has *already* tipped can look deceptively calm for a while.

## Two knobs: the cusp

Real systems rarely have perfect symmetry. Add a small **imperfection** $h$ to the pitchfork,
$\dot x = h + rx - x^3$, and the clean split breaks into two disconnected pieces [@strogatz_nonlinear]. Now
there are two control parameters, and the right picture is a **stability diagram** in the $(r,h)$ plane:
two curves of saddle-node bifurcations meeting tangentially at a **cusp point**, with three equilibria
inside the wedge and one outside [@strogatz_nonlinear]. Stack the equilibria above that plane and you get
the folded **cusp catastrophe** surface; the "catastrophe" is the moment the state is carried over the edge
of the upper sheet and drops discontinuously to the lower one [@strogatz_nonlinear]. The budworm's $(k,r)$
stability diagram, with its *refuge*, *bistable* and *outbreak* regions, has exactly this shape
[@strogatz_nonlinear].

Strogatz flags that catastrophe theory provoked "a violent controversy" in the late 1970s
[@strogatz_nonlinear] — partly over its enthusiastic export to the social sciences. The lesson for us is not
to avoid the geometry but to earn it: a cusp is a claim about specific, modellable feedbacks, not a
metaphor to be draped over any sudden change.

## Common pitfalls

::: {.callout-warning title="Where this goes wrong"}
- **Calling every rapid change a "tipping point".** A tipping point in the precise sense is a **fold**: a
  stable state that ceases to exist as a parameter drifts. A steep but smooth response (an S-shaped curve
  with a single equilibrium) is fast but reversible. Ask whether there are two stable states before using
  the word.
- **Assuming reversibility.** Under hysteresis, undoing the cause does not undo the effect — the budworm
  population stays at the outbreak level even when conditions are restored [@strogatz_nonlinear]. Policy
  that "reverses the shock" may need to overshoot the original conditions, or wait out a slow variable.
- **Reading local stability as safety.** A state can be stable to small disturbances but not to large ones
  [@strogatz_nonlinear]; a big enough shock can kick a system across the threshold even far from any fold.
- **Ignoring time scales.** The budworm analysis works because the forest moves orders of magnitude more
  slowly than the insects [@strogatz_nonlinear]. When the slow and fast variables are not well separated,
  the bifurcation diagram is a guide, not a forecast.
- **Fitting a linear slope across a bifurcation.** Pooling observations from both sides of a fold, or from
  both branches of a bistable region, averages two different regimes into a coefficient that describes
  neither. Path dependence means $x$ depends on history, not just on today's $r$.
- **Over-selling catastrophe geometry.** The cusp is a real mathematical object, but its social-science
  applications drew sharp criticism [@strogatz_nonlinear]. Derive it from mechanisms, don't assert it.
:::

## Appendix · the mathematics {.unnumbered}

::: {.math-appendix}

**Linear stability on a line.** For $\dot x=f(x)$ with $f(x^\*)=0$, write $x=x^\*+\eta$. Taylor-expanding,
$\dot\eta \approx f'(x^\*)\,\eta$, so $\eta(t)\approx\eta_0 e^{f'(x^\*)t}$: the fixed point is stable if
$f'(x^\*)<0$, unstable if $f'(x^\*)>0$, and the **characteristic recovery time** is $1/|f'(x^\*)|$. When
$f'(x^\*)\to0$ that time diverges, which is critical slowing down.

**The saddle-node normal form.** For $\dot x=r-x^2$: fixed points $x^\*=\pm\sqrt r$ exist only for $r\ge0$,
and $f'(x^\*)=-2x^\*$, so $+\sqrt r$ is stable and $-\sqrt r$ unstable. At $r=0$ both merge and $f'=0$.
Near any generic fold the dynamics reduce to this form by Taylor expansion — e.g. $\dot x = r-x-e^{-x}$ near
$(x,r)=(0,1)$ gives $\dot x\approx(r-1)-\tfrac12x^2$.

**The bottleneck time.** Just past the fold, $\dot x=r+x^2$ with small $r>0$. The time to pass through is

$$
T_{\text{bottleneck}}\approx\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dx}{r+x^2}=\frac{\pi}{\sqrt r},
$$

so passage time diverges like $(r-r_c)^{-1/2}$ as the parameter approaches the fold from the far side.

**Tangency gives the bifurcation.** A saddle-node occurs where a fixed point is also a double root:
$f(x^\*;r)=0$ and $\partial f/\partial x\,(x^\*;r)=0$. For the imperfect pitchfork $\dot x=h+rx-x^3$, the cubic
$rx-x^3$ peaks at $x=\sqrt{r/3}$ with height $\tfrac{2r}{3}\sqrt{r/3}$, so folds occur at

$$
h=\pm h_c(r),\qquad h_c(r)=\frac{2r}{3}\sqrt{\frac r3},
$$

two curves meeting tangentially at the cusp $(r,h)=(0,0)$.

**The budworm bifurcation curves.** In dimensionless form,
$\dot x = rx\!\left(1-\tfrac{x}{k}\right)-\dfrac{x^2}{1+x^2}$. Nonzero equilibria satisfy
$r\!\left(1-\tfrac{x}{k}\right)=\dfrac{x}{1+x^2}$, a straight line meeting a fixed curve. Requiring
tangency (equal values *and* equal slopes) and eliminating $r/k$ gives the curves parametrically in $x>1$:

$$
r(x)=\frac{2x^3}{(1+x^2)^2},\qquad k(x)=\frac{2x^3}{x^2-1}.
$$

For $k=10$ (the figure) the two folds sit at $r\approx0.384$ and $r\approx0.560$; between them the refuge and
outbreak levels coexist. The minimum of $k(x)$, at $x=\sqrt3$, is $k=3\sqrt3\approx5.2$: below that carrying
capacity the line can only cross the curve once and there is no bistability at all — the cusp point.

:::
