Statistical Models of Networks

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source

From describing a network to modelling it: block models, exponential random graphs and latent spaces, the tools that let you ask why two people are connected and how sure you can be.

Most of the network entries describe a network: its degrees, its centres, its communities. A statistical network model goes one step further and writes down a probability for every possible tie, so you can estimate parameters, compare explanations and say how sure you are. Three families do most of the work. Stochastic block models say the chance of a tie depends on which hidden groups the two people belong to. Exponential random graph models say it depends on the patterns the tie would complete, such as triangles and shared attributes. Latent space models place everyone in an unseen social space where closeness means connection.

Why it matters

Suppose you have the friendship network of a college year and a survey of each student. Two questions follow at once. Do students who smoke befriend each other more than chance would predict? And is the network made of a few tight groups, a core and a periphery, or something else? Centrality and community detection give descriptive answers. They do not give a standard error, they cannot separate “smokers like smokers” from “friends of friends become friends”, and community detection only looks for one shape of group.

Jacobucci, Grimm and Zhang make the case for network data in social science on exactly this ground. Ordinary dyadic studies usually record only the pairs who are related, for instance friends, so they can say little about who does not become friends. A full network records both the ties and the non-ties within a bounded group, which is what lets a model ask why some pairs connect and others do not (Jacobucci et al. 2023). Murphy names the two goals that models of networks serve: to discover structure, such as clusters or communities, and to predict links, such as who will become friends with whom (Murphy 2012).

The running example: a WeChat network

Jacobucci and colleagues use one data set throughout their network chapter (Jacobucci et al. 2023). In May 2017 a Notre Dame lab gave each of the 181 students in a Chinese college’s Art and Design major a roster of their classmates and asked who they were friends with on WeChat. A WeChat friendship has to be accepted by both people, so the network is undirected. Of the 181, 165 students took part, and the survey also recorded gender, age, GPA, BMI, smoking, drinking, and scales for happiness, loneliness and depression.

Step one: use the network inside an ordinary model

The simplest approach extracts a number from the network for each person and puts it into a familiar model as a predictor, outcome, moderator or mediator (Jacobucci et al. 2023). The authors compared male and female students’ PageRank, logged because it is right-skewed: the means were −5.21 and −5.18, a Welch t of −0.43 on 142 degrees of freedom, p=0.67. They then tested whether the number of WeChat friends (degree) mediated the effects of GPA and BMI on happiness and loneliness. It did not: every indirect effect was small and nonsignificant (GPA → happiness, for example, −0.083, SE 0.089) (Jacobucci et al. 2023).

This is useful, and it uses the network only indirectly (Jacobucci et al. 2023). Everything about who is tied to whom is squeezed into one number per person. The models below treat the ties themselves as the outcome.

The baseline: every tie a coin flip

The Erdős–Rényi model is the simplest statistical model of a network: every pair is tied independently with the same probability p (Jacobucci et al. 2023). Its maximum-likelihood estimate is the obvious one, the number of ties divided by the number of pairs, which is just the network’s density. For WeChat that is 1{,}722/13{,}530 = 12.7\% (165 students make 165\times164/2 = 13{,}530 pairs) (Jacobucci et al. 2023).

As a model of a social network it is a straw man, and Jacobucci and colleagues say why. It has little clustering. Its degrees follow a Poisson distribution, where real networks often have heavy tails. And it cannot represent transitivity, the tendency of two people who share a friend to be friends themselves, because it treats every tie as independent of every other (Jacobucci et al. 2023). Each model below relaxes the independence in a different way.

Stochastic block models: ties depend on groups

A stochastic block model (SBM) divides the nodes into K groups, or blocks, and lets the tie probability depend only on the two blocks involved (Jacobucci et al. 2023). Inside block k, every pair is tied with probability p_{kk}. Between blocks k and l, with probability p_{kl}. Each block is an Erdős–Rényi graph, and the whole model is summarised by a K\times K matrix of probabilities. Jacobucci and colleagues credit the model to Holland, Laskey and Leinhardt (1983) (Jacobucci et al. 2023); Murphy’s citation for the version with hidden, estimated blocks is Nowicki and Snijders (2001) (Murphy 2012).

When the blocks are known, fitting is just counting. Splitting the WeChat network by gender gives a within-male tie probability of 0.18, within-female 0.15, and between the sexes only 0.084 (Jacobucci et al. 2023). That is homophily in its plainest form: a male–female pair is about half as likely to be friends as a same-sex pair.

When the blocks are unknown, finding them is a version of community detection (Jacobucci et al. 2023). Murphy writes the model generatively. Each node draws a hidden block q_i from probabilities \pi, each pair of blocks gets a tie probability \eta_{ab} (from a beta prior), and each tie is a Bernoulli draw with the probability for its two blocks (Murphy 2012). This looks like a mixture model, but it cannot be fitted by exact EM: once you condition on the ties, every node’s hidden block depends on every other’s. So it is fitted by variational EM or collapsed Gibbs sampling (Murphy 2012; Jacobucci et al. 2023).

How many blocks? Jacobucci and colleagues use the integrated completed likelihood (ICL), a criterion where larger is better, and fitted 1 to 15 blocks (Jacobucci et al. 2023). Ten blocks gave the highest ICL, but four of those blocks held one, two, four and five students. Seven blocks scored nearly as well and still left a block of four. They settled on six blocks of 37, 9, 33, 28, 31 and 27 students. That is a judgement, and a sensible one: a criterion picks a number, and the analyst decides whether the groups it implies are meaningful (Jacobucci et al. 2023). The six-block fit is readable. Block 1’s members are friends with each other with probability 0.312 and with block 2’s with probability 0.495, but rarely with blocks 3 to 6 (0.018 to 0.066). Block 2 is a small, very dense group of nine (0.604 inside) that is also well tied to every other block except block 3 (0.22 to 0.50), a bridging group. Block 3 is inward-looking: 0.403 inside and at most 0.082 to anyone else (Jacobucci et al. 2023).

A block is not a community

Murphy’s example shows what separates an SBM from community detection (Murphy 2012). Nine nodes look like a tangle until they are sorted into three blocks, and then it becomes clear that most ties run from the first block to the second, from the second to the third, and from the third back to the first. The third block’s members have no ties to each other at all. What they share is a role: they all link to the same kinds of other nodes. Murphy shows the same model generating rings, dominance hierarchies and other patterns, and notes that some social networks are layered into strata while others consist of cliques (Murphy 2012). A modularity-based method looks only for groups denser inside than out, so it cannot find a group defined by whom its members connect to.

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from matplotlib.colors import ListedColormap
from matplotlib.patches import Rectangle
from itertools import permutations

# --- plant a core, a periphery and a separate community -----------------------
rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
sizes = [14, 26, 20]
K, N = 3, sum(sizes)
z_true = np.repeat(np.arange(K), sizes)
P = np.array([[0.60, 0.30, 0.03],
              [0.30, 0.03, 0.03],
              [0.03, 0.03, 0.50]])
A = np.zeros((N, N), int)
for i in range(N):
    for j in range(i + 1, N):
        if rng.random() < P[z_true[i], z_true[j]]:
            A[i, j] = A[j, i] = 1

# --- SBM log-likelihood with the block probabilities profiled out -------------
def sbm_loglik(z):
    ll = 0.0
    for a in range(K):
        for b in range(a, K):
            ia, ib = z == a, z == b
            if a == b:
                pairs = ia.sum() * (ia.sum() - 1) / 2
                ties = A[np.ix_(ia, ia)].sum() / 2
            else:
                pairs = ia.sum() * ib.sum()
                ties = A[np.ix_(ia, ib)].sum()
            if pairs > 0 and 0 < ties < pairs:
                p = ties / pairs
                ll += ties * np.log(p) + (pairs - ties) * np.log(1 - p)
    return ll

def fit_sbm(restarts=20, seed=0):
    r = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    best_ll, best_z = -np.inf, None
    for _ in range(restarts):
        z = r.integers(0, K, N)
        cur, improved = sbm_loglik(z), True
        while improved:                      # move one node at a time, keep the best block
            improved = False
            for i in r.permutation(N):
                old = z[i]
                best_c, best = old, cur
                for c in range(K):
                    if c != old:
                        z[i] = c
                        ll = sbm_loglik(z)
                        if ll > best + 1e-9:
                            best_c, best = c, ll
                z[i] = best_c
                if best_c != old:
                    cur, improved = best, True
        if cur > best_ll:
            best_ll, best_z = cur, z.copy()
    return best_z

z_sbm = fit_sbm()
# match fitted block labels to the planted ones (labels are arbitrary)
perm = max(permutations(range(K)), key=lambda p: (np.array(p)[z_sbm] == z_true).sum())
z_sbm = np.array(perm)[z_sbm]
sbm_wrong = int((z_sbm != z_true).sum())

# --- modularity maximisation by local moves (as in the community entry) -------
k = A.sum(1); m = A.sum() / 2
adj = [np.where(A[i])[0] for i in range(N)]
comm, improved = np.arange(N), True
while improved:
    improved = False
    for i in rng.permutation(N):
        def gain(c):
            k_in = sum(1 for nb in adj[i] if comm[nb] == c)
            tot = k[comm == c].sum() - (k[i] if comm[i] == c else 0)
            return k_in - k[i] * tot / (2 * m)
        here = gain(comm[i])
        best_c, best_g = comm[i], here
        for c in set(comm[adj[i]]):
            g = gain(c)
            if g > best_g + 1e-9:
                best_c, best_g = c, g
        if best_c != comm[i]:
            comm[i], improved = best_c, True
_, comm = np.unique(comm, return_inverse=True)
n_mod = comm.max() + 1
core_like = np.bincount(comm[z_true == 0]).argmax()          # the module holding the core
periph_merged = int((comm[z_true == 1] == core_like).sum())

# --- draw ----------------------------------------------------------------------
cmap = ListedColormap(["#edece3", "#161613"])
order_rand = rng.permutation(N)
order_sbm = np.lexsort((rng.random(N), z_sbm))
order_mod = np.lexsort((z_true, comm))

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 3, figsize=(9.6, 3.7))
panels = [(order_rand, "random order\n(what you are handed)"),
          (order_sbm, f"sorted by fitted block model\n({sbm_wrong} of {N} nodes misplaced)"),
          (order_mod, f"sorted by modularity\n({n_mod} modules; {periph_merged} of 26 "
                      "periphery\nnodes merged with the core)")]
for ax, (order, title) in zip(axes, panels):
    ax.imshow(A[np.ix_(order, order)], cmap=cmap, vmin=0, vmax=1, interpolation="nearest")
    ax.set_title(title, fontsize=9)
    ax.set_xticks([]); ax.set_yticks([])
# block boundaries and the periphery box on the SBM panel
cuts = np.cumsum(np.bincount(z_sbm, minlength=K))[:-1] - 0.5
for c in cuts:
    axes[1].axhline(c, color="#8a8a7e", lw=0.8); axes[1].axvline(c, color="#8a8a7e", lw=0.8)
lo = (z_sbm == 0).sum() - 0.5; w = (z_sbm == 1).sum()
axes[1].add_patch(Rectangle((lo, lo), w, w, fill=False, ec="#e8531d", lw=2.2))
cuts_m = np.cumsum(np.bincount(comm))[:-1] - 0.5
for c in cuts_m:
    axes[2].axhline(c, color="#8a8a7e", lw=0.8); axes[2].axvline(c, color="#8a8a7e", lw=0.8)
fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()

Two things in the figure generalise. The periphery’s own corner of the matrix is nearly empty, yet the block model finds it, because its members all tie to the core and almost nowhere else. Modularity, which rewards dense insides, has no reason to keep them together, so most of them join the core’s module and the rest scatter. Neither method is wrong. They answer different questions: which groups are tight? against which nodes play the same part?

Softer blocks and messier data

Murphy surveys several extensions (Murphy 2012):

  • Mixed membership. In the mixed membership SBM (Airoldi et al. 2008), each node has a profile of block memberships rather than one block, and may act from a different block depending on whom it is dealing with. The classic test case is Sampson’s 1968 study of who liked whom among 18 monks, collected by hand over months. With three blocks the model recovers the groups Sampson named, the “young Turks”, the “outcasts” and the “loyal opposition”, and places some monks, notably monk 15, between two groups, the ones Sampson called the “waverers” (Murphy 2012). Murphy separates two kinds of uncertainty here. A membership profile says to what degree someone belongs to each group (ontological uncertainty). A posterior probability says how sure we are which single group they are in (epistemological uncertainty) (Murphy 2012).
  • Missing ties. A zero in the matrix may mean two people chose not to connect, or that they never had the chance, or that nobody recorded it. “Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence,” as Murphy puts it, and he notes that missing data is a prevalent problem in social network analysis. One fix is to let a fraction \rho of the zeros come from a background process, so the model only has to explain the rest (Murphy 2012). This is a network version of the missing-data problem.
  • Features and text. Node features can enter the tie probability, as in a logistic regression (Murphy 2012). The relational topic model (Chang & Blei 2010) models a citation network and the papers’ text together: whether two papers are linked depends on the topics they actually use. It predicts missing links better than fitting topics first and then regressing links on them, for the same reason partial least squares can beat principal components followed by regression: the hidden space is chosen to predict the outcome (Murphy 2012).
  • No fixed number of blocks, several kinds of node. The infinite relational model (Kemp et al. 2006) replaces the fixed K with a Dirichlet process and extends blocks to any number of object types and relations (Murphy 2012). One application is political. A 1965 data set records 14 countries, 54 kinds of interaction between them (from “sends tourists to” and “economic aid” to “accusations”) and 90 country features. The model sorted the countries into five groups that follow geopolitical lines (the US with the UK, the communist bloc), the interactions into 18 clusters (for instance negative behaviour together with accusations), and the features into five clusters, the first containing “noncommunist”, one of the most important aspects of this Cold War data (Murphy 2012). The matrix factorisation entry takes the same idea to people-by-item data.

Exponential random graph models: ties depend on patterns

The exponential random graph model (ERGM) is, in Jacobucci and colleagues’ words, probably the most widely used model for networks (Jacobucci et al. 2023). Instead of modelling ties one at a time it puts a probability on the whole network. You choose a set of network statistics, such as the number of ties, the number of triangles, or how many ties join two smokers. The probability of a network is then proportional to \exp(\beta^\top s), where s holds those statistics and \beta their weights (Jacobucci et al. 2023). A positive weight on triangles makes networks with many triangles more probable. The Erdős–Rényi model is the special case with only the tie count (Jacobucci et al. 2023).

The model becomes much easier to read when you look at one tie at a time. Given the rest of the network, the log-odds that i and j are tied is a weighted sum of change statistics: how much each statistic would rise if the tie were added. So an ERGM reads like a logistic regression for each pair (Jacobucci et al. 2023). With only the tie count, the change statistic is always 1, the model is an intercept, and the WeChat estimate \hat\beta=-1.925 (SE 0.026) gives odds of 0.146 and a probability of 12.7%, the Erdős–Rényi answer again (Jacobucci et al. 2023).

Structure. Jacobucci and colleagues then add 2-stars (two ties sharing a node) and triangles (Jacobucci et al. 2023):

Read as a logistic regression, each extra 2-star a tie would create lowers its odds by about 1.8%, and each triangle it would close raises them by about 61% (Jacobucci et al. 2023). Triangles are what a shared friend produces, so this is transitivity, measured. To see the size of it (our arithmetic): for two students who each already have about 20 friends, close to the network’s average, the model puts the chance of a tie at about 1.5% if they share no friends and about 14% if they share five.

Attributes. Covariates enter through pair-level terms: the sum of two students’ values (does higher GPA mean more ties?), the absolute difference (do similar people connect?, the homophily principle), and indicators for same group or both in a particular group (Jacobucci et al. 2023):

Same-gender pairs have about twice the odds of being friends, and so do pairs of smokers. Pairs of non-smokers do not differ from the baseline, and similarity in BMI does not matter (Jacobucci et al. 2023). (The text describes the first covariate as age; the table and the discussion are about GPA.)

There is a catch the authors flag: the statistics in an ERGM are typically not independent of one another, which “can pose serious challenges” in estimation (Jacobucci et al. 2023). A triangle is made of 2-stars, and adding one tie changes many statistics at once. The probability of the whole network also has a normalising constant that sums over every possible network, the same problem as the partition function of a Markov random field, which an ERGM is, with one variable per pair. Reading the model one tie at a time, as above, is the pseudo-likelihood idea from that entry, and it carries the same warning when the dependence is strong (our link, not the authors’).

Latent space models: ties depend on distance

A third approach explains ties by unobserved positions rather than observed patterns (Jacobucci et al. 2023). In the latent space model of Hoff, Raftery and Handcock (2002), every person has a position z_i in an unseen space. The log-odds of a tie is an intercept, plus covariate effects, minus the distance between the two positions, so people who sit close together are likely to be tied (Jacobucci et al. 2023). The positions do the work that triangles do in an ERGM. If i is near j and j is near k, then i is not far from k, so transitivity comes for free.

The dimension of the space is chosen by BIC. For WeChat, models with 1 to 9 dimensions were compared and two dimensions won, so the friendships are best summarised as a map in the plane (Jacobucci et al. 2023). The authors read these as similar to the ERGM: same-gender pairs and pairs of smokers are more likely to be friends, now conditional on the latent positions, that is, with the social map held fixed (Jacobucci et al. 2023). One difference is worth noticing (our reading): GPA, which was a significant positive term in the ERGM, has an interval that includes zero here and a negative point estimate. Once the map absorbs who clusters with whom, the GPA effect is no longer clear. Covariate effects in network models depend on what else the model uses to explain the structure.

The price is computation. The latent space fit took about ten minutes; the covariate ERGM took less than half a second on the same desktop, and both grow quickly with network size (Jacobucci et al. 2023).

Choosing among them

The three families relax the coin-flip baseline in different directions, and the choice follows the question:

  • Groups or roles (factions, cores and peripheries, strata): a stochastic block model, or its mixed-membership version when people straddle groups.
  • Mechanisms (does transitivity matter once homophily is accounted for?): an ERGM, whose terms are hypotheses about how ties form.
  • A map and covariate effects net of it: a latent space model.

Jacobucci and colleagues note that the same methods extend to directed and valued networks, and that networks observed over time have their own models: temporal ERGMs, stochastic actor-oriented models (Snijders and colleagues) and dynamic network models (Jacobucci et al. 2023). In R, the statnet suite, with its ergm and latentnet packages, is the standard toolkit (Jacobucci et al. 2023).

Common pitfalls

WarningWhere this goes wrong
  • Treating ties as independent observations. The Erdős–Rényi model, and any analysis that treats pairs as separate data points, ignores transitivity, which is one of the strongest regularities in social networks. (Jacobucci et al. 2023)
  • Squeezing the network into one number and stopping. Degree or PageRank in a regression uses the network only indirectly. In the WeChat data degree showed nothing, while the ERGM found strong gender and smoking homophily in who is tied to whom. (Jacobucci et al. 2023)
  • Letting a criterion pick the number of blocks unchecked. WeChat’s best-ICL fit had blocks of one, two, four and five students. Look at the blocks before you accept K. (Jacobucci et al. 2023)
  • Reading blocks as communities. A block can be a role, such as a periphery, whose members never tie to one another. Modularity-based methods cannot find these. (Murphy 2012)
  • Reading zeros as choices. A missing tie may be a missing opportunity or a missing record; absence of evidence is not evidence of absence. (Murphy 2012)
  • Fitting an ERGM as if its terms were separate regressors. Network statistics overlap and are typically not independent, which poses serious challenges for estimation. (Jacobucci et al. 2023)
  • Comparing covariate effects across model families. A latent space model’s effects are conditional on the latent positions; GPA’s effect changes sign between the WeChat ERGM and latent space fit. (Jacobucci et al. 2023)
  • Calling homophily a cause. “Smokers befriend smokers” fits selection and influence alike, and the pattern alone cannot separate them (see community detection and peer effects). (Menczer et al. 2020)

Appendix · the mathematics

Erdős–Rényi MLE. For an undirected network with adjacency A, L(p)=\prod_{i<j}p^{a_{ij}}(1-p)^{1-a_{ij}}. Setting the derivative of \log L to zero gives \hat p = M/\binom N2, with M the number of ties.

Stochastic block model. With block labels q_i\in\{1,\dots,K\}, q_i\sim\mathrm{Cat}(\pi) and a_{ij}\mid q_i=a,q_j=b\sim\mathrm{Ber}(\eta_{ab}), \log p(A\mid q,\eta)=\sum_{a\le b}\Big[m_{ab}\log\eta_{ab}+(n_{ab}-m_{ab})\log(1-\eta_{ab})\Big], where m_{ab} counts ties and n_{ab} pairs between blocks a and b (n_{aa}=\binom{n_a}{2}). For fixed labels \hat\eta_{ab}=m_{ab}/n_{ab}, which is the “known blocks” calculation. The figure maximises this profile likelihood over labels by single-node moves. Because p(q\mid A) does not factorise over nodes, the E-step of exact EM is intractable, hence variational EM (a factorised \prod_i r_i(q_i)) or collapsed Gibbs. ICL (Biernacki et al. 2000) scores a K by the complete-data log-likelihood at the best labels, with BIC-style penalties for the K(K+1)/2 block probabilities and the K-1 block proportions.

Mixed membership SBM. \pi_i\sim\mathrm{Dir}(\alpha); for each ordered pair, q_{i\to j}\sim\mathrm{Cat}(\pi_i), q_{i\leftarrow j}\sim\mathrm{Cat}(\pi_j), and P(a_{ij}=1\mid q_{i\to j}=a,q_{i\leftarrow j}=b)=\eta_{ab}. The robust version for unobserved zeros is p(a_{ij}=r)=\rho\,\delta_0(r)+(1-\rho)\,\mathrm{Ber}(r\mid\eta_{ab}).

ERGM. \Pr(Y=A)=\exp\{\beta^\top s(A)\}/c(\beta) with c(\beta)=\sum_{A'}\exp\{\beta^\top s(A')\} over all 2^{\binom N2} networks. Writing A^+_{ij} and A^-_{ij} for A with the tie ij forced on and off, \operatorname{logit}P(a_{ij}=1\mid A_{-ij})=\beta^\top\big[s(A^+_{ij})-s(A^-_{ij})\big], and c(\beta) cancels. Change statistics: ties =1; 2-stars =k_i+k_j (degrees not counting ij); triangles = the number of shared neighbours of i and j. With Table 12.7 and k_i=k_j=20: \operatorname{logit}P=-3.458-0.019(40)+0.473\,t, giving P=0.0145 at t=0 and 0.136 at t=5. The mean degree in the WeChat network is 2(1{,}722)/165\approx20.9. Maximising the product of these conditionals is maximum pseudo-likelihood; the full likelihood needs c(\beta), usually handled by MCMC.

Node-covariate terms. Main effect h_{ij}=x_i+x_j; absolute difference |x_i-x_j|; nodal factor h_{ij}\in\{0,1,2\} (how many of the pair have the level); uniform homophily \mathbb I(x_i=x_j); differential homophily \mathbb I(x_i=x_j=\ell).

Latent space model. a_{ij}\sim\mathrm{Ber}(p_{ij}), \operatorname{logit}p_{ij}=\alpha+\beta^\top h_{ij}-\lVert z_i-z_j\rVert, with the distance coefficient fixed at -1 to set the scale. Distances obey the triangle inequality, so \lVert z_i-z_k\rVert\le\lVert z_i-z_j\rVert+\lVert z_j-z_k\rVert: two ties through j force i and k to be fairly close, which is how the model produces transitivity without a triangle term.

::: {.lead-kernel}
Most of the network entries **describe** a network: its degrees, its centres, its communities. A
**statistical network model** goes one step further and writes down a **probability for every possible tie**,
so you can estimate parameters, compare explanations and say how sure you are. Three families do most of the
work. **Stochastic block models** say the chance of a tie depends on which hidden groups the two people
belong to. **Exponential random graph models** say it depends on the patterns the tie would complete, such as
triangles and shared attributes. **Latent space models** place everyone in an unseen social space where
closeness means connection.
:::

## Why it matters

Suppose you have the friendship network of a college year and a survey of each student. Two questions follow
at once. Do students who smoke befriend each other more than chance would predict? And is the network made of
a few tight groups, a core and a periphery, or something else? [Centrality](centrality.qmd) and
[community detection](community-detection.qmd) give descriptive answers. They do not give a standard error,
they cannot separate "smokers like smokers" from "friends of friends become friends", and community detection
only looks for one shape of group.

Jacobucci, Grimm and Zhang make the case for network data in social science on exactly this ground. Ordinary
dyadic studies usually record only the pairs who *are* related, for instance friends, so they can say little
about who does not become friends. A full network records both the ties and the non-ties within a bounded
group, which is what lets a model ask why some pairs connect and others do not [@jacobucci_ml]. Murphy names
the two goals that models of networks serve: to **discover structure**, such as clusters or communities, and
to **predict links**, such as who will become friends with whom [@murphy_ml].

## The running example: a WeChat network

Jacobucci and colleagues use one data set throughout their network chapter [@jacobucci_ml]. In May 2017 a
Notre Dame lab gave each of the 181 students in a Chinese college's Art and Design major a roster of their
classmates and asked who they were friends with on WeChat. A WeChat friendship has to be accepted by both
people, so the network is undirected. Of the 181, 165 students took part, and the survey also recorded
gender, age, GPA, BMI, smoking, drinking, and scales for happiness, loneliness and depression.

## Step one: use the network inside an ordinary model

The simplest approach extracts a number from the network for each person and puts it into a familiar model
as a predictor, outcome, moderator or mediator [@jacobucci_ml]. The authors compared male and female
students' [PageRank](centrality.qmd), logged because it is right-skewed: the means were −5.21 and −5.18, a
Welch $t$ of −0.43 on 142 degrees of freedom, $p=0.67$. They then tested whether the number of WeChat friends
(degree) mediated the effects of GPA and BMI on happiness and loneliness. It did not: every indirect effect
was small and nonsignificant (GPA → happiness, for example, −0.083, SE 0.089) [@jacobucci_ml].

This is useful, and it uses the network only *indirectly* [@jacobucci_ml]. Everything about who is tied to
whom is squeezed into one number per person. The models below treat the ties themselves as the outcome.

## The baseline: every tie a coin flip

The [Erdős–Rényi model](random-graphs-erdos-renyi.qmd) is the simplest statistical model of a network: every
pair is tied independently with the same probability $p$ [@jacobucci_ml]. Its maximum-likelihood estimate is
the obvious one, the number of ties divided by the number of pairs, which is just the network's **density**.
For WeChat that is $1{,}722/13{,}530 = 12.7\%$ (165 students make $165\times164/2 = 13{,}530$ pairs)
[@jacobucci_ml].

As a model of a social network it is a straw man, and Jacobucci and colleagues say why. It has little
clustering. Its degrees follow a Poisson distribution, where real networks often have heavy tails. And it
cannot represent **transitivity**, the tendency of two people who share a friend to be friends themselves,
because it treats every tie as independent of every other [@jacobucci_ml]. Each model below relaxes the
independence in a different way.

## Stochastic block models: ties depend on groups

A **stochastic block model** (SBM) divides the nodes into $K$ groups, or **blocks**, and lets the tie
probability depend only on the two blocks involved [@jacobucci_ml]. Inside block $k$, every pair is tied with
probability $p_{kk}$. Between blocks $k$ and $l$, with probability $p_{kl}$. Each block is an Erdős–Rényi
graph, and the whole model is summarised by a $K\times K$ matrix of probabilities. Jacobucci and colleagues
credit the model to Holland, Laskey and Leinhardt (1983) [@jacobucci_ml]; Murphy's citation for the version
with hidden, estimated blocks is Nowicki and Snijders (2001) [@murphy_ml].

**When the blocks are known**, fitting is just counting. Splitting the WeChat network by gender gives a
within-male tie probability of 0.18, within-female 0.15, and between the sexes only 0.084 [@jacobucci_ml].
That is **homophily** in its plainest form: a male–female pair is about half as likely to be friends as a
same-sex pair.

**When the blocks are unknown**, finding them is a version of community detection [@jacobucci_ml]. Murphy
writes the model generatively. Each node draws a hidden block $q_i$ from probabilities $\pi$, each pair of
blocks gets a tie probability $\eta_{ab}$ (from a beta prior), and each tie is a Bernoulli draw with the
probability for its two blocks [@murphy_ml]. This looks like a [mixture model](../ml-track/mixture-models-and-em.qmd),
but it cannot be fitted by exact EM: once you condition on the ties, every node's hidden block depends on
every other's. So it is fitted by [variational EM](../ml-track/variational-inference.qmd) or collapsed Gibbs
sampling [@murphy_ml; @jacobucci_ml].

**How many blocks?** Jacobucci and colleagues use the **integrated completed likelihood** (ICL), a criterion
where larger is better, and fitted 1 to 15 blocks [@jacobucci_ml]. Ten blocks gave the highest ICL, but four
of those blocks held one, two, four and five students. Seven blocks scored nearly as well and still left a
block of four. They settled on **six blocks** of 37, 9, 33, 28, 31 and 27 students. That is a judgement, and
a sensible one: a criterion picks a number, and the analyst decides whether the groups it implies are
meaningful [@jacobucci_ml]. The six-block fit is readable. Block 1's members are friends with each other with
probability 0.312 and with block 2's with probability 0.495, but rarely with blocks 3 to 6 (0.018 to 0.066).
Block 2 is a small, very dense group of nine (0.604 inside) that is also well tied to every other block
except block 3 (0.22 to 0.50), a bridging group. Block 3 is
inward-looking: 0.403 inside and at most 0.082 to anyone else [@jacobucci_ml].

### A block is not a community

Murphy's example shows what separates an SBM from community detection [@murphy_ml]. Nine nodes look like a
tangle until they are sorted into three blocks, and then it becomes clear that most ties run from the first
block to the second, from the second to the third, and from the third back to the first. The third block's
members have **no ties to each other at all**. What they share is a *role*: they all link to the same kinds of
other nodes. Murphy shows the same model generating rings, dominance hierarchies and other patterns, and
notes that some social networks are layered into strata while others consist of cliques [@murphy_ml]. A
[modularity](community-detection.qmd)-based method looks only for groups denser inside than out, so it cannot
find a group defined by whom its members connect to.

```{python}
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from matplotlib.colors import ListedColormap
from matplotlib.patches import Rectangle
from itertools import permutations

# --- plant a core, a periphery and a separate community -----------------------
rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
sizes = [14, 26, 20]
K, N = 3, sum(sizes)
z_true = np.repeat(np.arange(K), sizes)
P = np.array([[0.60, 0.30, 0.03],
              [0.30, 0.03, 0.03],
              [0.03, 0.03, 0.50]])
A = np.zeros((N, N), int)
for i in range(N):
    for j in range(i + 1, N):
        if rng.random() < P[z_true[i], z_true[j]]:
            A[i, j] = A[j, i] = 1

# --- SBM log-likelihood with the block probabilities profiled out -------------
def sbm_loglik(z):
    ll = 0.0
    for a in range(K):
        for b in range(a, K):
            ia, ib = z == a, z == b
            if a == b:
                pairs = ia.sum() * (ia.sum() - 1) / 2
                ties = A[np.ix_(ia, ia)].sum() / 2
            else:
                pairs = ia.sum() * ib.sum()
                ties = A[np.ix_(ia, ib)].sum()
            if pairs > 0 and 0 < ties < pairs:
                p = ties / pairs
                ll += ties * np.log(p) + (pairs - ties) * np.log(1 - p)
    return ll

def fit_sbm(restarts=20, seed=0):
    r = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    best_ll, best_z = -np.inf, None
    for _ in range(restarts):
        z = r.integers(0, K, N)
        cur, improved = sbm_loglik(z), True
        while improved:                      # move one node at a time, keep the best block
            improved = False
            for i in r.permutation(N):
                old = z[i]
                best_c, best = old, cur
                for c in range(K):
                    if c != old:
                        z[i] = c
                        ll = sbm_loglik(z)
                        if ll > best + 1e-9:
                            best_c, best = c, ll
                z[i] = best_c
                if best_c != old:
                    cur, improved = best, True
        if cur > best_ll:
            best_ll, best_z = cur, z.copy()
    return best_z

z_sbm = fit_sbm()
# match fitted block labels to the planted ones (labels are arbitrary)
perm = max(permutations(range(K)), key=lambda p: (np.array(p)[z_sbm] == z_true).sum())
z_sbm = np.array(perm)[z_sbm]
sbm_wrong = int((z_sbm != z_true).sum())

# --- modularity maximisation by local moves (as in the community entry) -------
k = A.sum(1); m = A.sum() / 2
adj = [np.where(A[i])[0] for i in range(N)]
comm, improved = np.arange(N), True
while improved:
    improved = False
    for i in rng.permutation(N):
        def gain(c):
            k_in = sum(1 for nb in adj[i] if comm[nb] == c)
            tot = k[comm == c].sum() - (k[i] if comm[i] == c else 0)
            return k_in - k[i] * tot / (2 * m)
        here = gain(comm[i])
        best_c, best_g = comm[i], here
        for c in set(comm[adj[i]]):
            g = gain(c)
            if g > best_g + 1e-9:
                best_c, best_g = c, g
        if best_c != comm[i]:
            comm[i], improved = best_c, True
_, comm = np.unique(comm, return_inverse=True)
n_mod = comm.max() + 1
core_like = np.bincount(comm[z_true == 0]).argmax()          # the module holding the core
periph_merged = int((comm[z_true == 1] == core_like).sum())

order_rand = rng.permutation(N)
order_sbm = np.lexsort((rng.random(N), z_sbm))
order_mod = np.lexsort((z_true, comm))

panels = [(order_rand, "random order\n(what you are handed)"),

          (order_mod, f"sorted by modularity\n({n_mod} modules; {periph_merged} of 26 "
                      "periphery\nnodes merged with the core)")]

    ax.set_title(title, fontsize=9)
    ax.set_xticks([]); ax.set_yticks([])

    axes[1].axhline(c, color="#8a8a7e", lw=0.8); axes[1].axvline(c, color="#8a8a7e", lw=0.8)
lo = (z_sbm == 0).sum() - 0.5; w = (z_sbm == 1).sum()
axes[1].add_patch(Rectangle((lo, lo), w, w, fill=False, ec="#e8531d", lw=2.2))
cuts_m = np.cumsum(np.bincount(comm))[:-1] - 0.5
for c in cuts_m:
    axes[2].axhline(c, color="#8a8a7e", lw=0.8); axes[2].axvline(c, color="#8a8a7e", lw=0.8)
fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

Two things in the figure generalise. The periphery's own corner of the matrix is nearly empty, yet the block
model finds it, because its members all tie to the core and almost nowhere else. Modularity, which rewards
dense insides, has no reason to keep them together, so most of them join the core's module and the rest
scatter. Neither method is wrong. They answer different questions: *which groups are tight?* against *which
nodes play the same part?*

### Softer blocks and messier data

Murphy surveys several extensions [@murphy_ml]:

- **Mixed membership.** In the **mixed membership SBM** (Airoldi et al. 2008), each node has a *profile* of
  block memberships rather than one block, and may act from a different block depending on whom it is
  dealing with. The classic test case is Sampson's 1968 study of who liked whom among 18 monks, collected by
  hand over months. With three blocks the model recovers the groups Sampson named, the "young Turks", the
  "outcasts" and the "loyal opposition", and places some monks, notably monk 15, between two groups, the
  ones Sampson called the "waverers" [@murphy_ml]. Murphy separates two kinds of uncertainty here. A
  membership profile says *to what degree* someone belongs to each group (ontological uncertainty). A
  posterior probability says *how sure we are* which single group they are in (epistemological
  uncertainty) [@murphy_ml].
- **Missing ties.** A zero in the matrix may mean two people chose not to connect, or that they never had
  the chance, or that nobody recorded it. "Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence," as Murphy puts
  it, and he notes that missing data is a prevalent problem in social network analysis. One fix is to let a
  fraction $\rho$ of the zeros come from a background process, so the model only has to explain the rest
  [@murphy_ml]. This is a network version of the [missing-data](../02-inference/multiple-imputation.qmd)
  problem.
- **Features and text.** Node features can enter the tie probability, as in a logistic regression
  [@murphy_ml]. The **relational topic model** (Chang & Blei 2010) models a citation network and the papers'
  text together: whether two papers are linked depends on the [topics](../04-predictive/text-as-data.qmd)
  they actually use. It predicts missing links better than fitting topics first and then regressing links on
  them, for the same reason [partial least squares](../ml-track/model-selection-and-dimension-reduction.qmd)
  can beat principal components followed by regression: the hidden space is chosen to predict the outcome
  [@murphy_ml].
- **No fixed number of blocks, several kinds of node.** The **infinite relational model** (Kemp et al. 2006)
  replaces the fixed $K$ with a Dirichlet process and extends blocks to any number of object types and
  relations [@murphy_ml]. One application is political. A 1965 data set records 14 countries, 54 kinds of
  interaction between them (from "sends tourists to" and "economic aid" to "accusations") and 90 country
  features. The model sorted the countries into five groups that follow geopolitical lines (the US with the
  UK, the communist bloc), the interactions into 18 clusters (for instance negative behaviour together with
  accusations), and the features into five clusters, the first containing "noncommunist", one of the most
  important aspects of this Cold War data [@murphy_ml]. The
  [matrix factorisation](../ml-track/matrix-factorization-and-recommenders.qmd) entry takes the same idea to
  people-by-item data.

## Exponential random graph models: ties depend on patterns

The **exponential random graph model** (ERGM) is, in Jacobucci and colleagues' words, probably the most
widely used model for networks [@jacobucci_ml]. Instead of modelling ties one at a time it puts a probability
on the **whole network**. You choose a set of network statistics, such as the number of ties, the number of
triangles, or how many ties join two smokers. The probability of a network is then proportional to
$\exp(\beta^\top s)$, where $s$ holds those statistics and $\beta$ their weights [@jacobucci_ml]. A positive
weight on triangles makes networks with many triangles more probable. The Erdős–Rényi model is the special
case with only the tie count [@jacobucci_ml].

The model becomes much easier to read when you look at one tie at a time. Given the rest of the network, the
log-odds that $i$ and $j$ are tied is a weighted sum of **change statistics**: how much each statistic would
rise if the tie were added. So an ERGM reads like a [logistic regression](../03-regression/generalized-linear-models.qmd)
for each pair [@jacobucci_ml]. With only the tie count, the change statistic is always 1, the model is an
intercept, and the WeChat estimate $\hat\beta=-1.925$ (SE 0.026) gives odds of 0.146 and a probability of
12.7%, the Erdős–Rényi answer again [@jacobucci_ml].

**Structure.** Jacobucci and colleagues then add 2-stars (two ties sharing a node) and triangles
[@jacobucci_ml]:

| Term | Estimate | SE | Odds ratio |
|---|---|---|---|
| ties (edges) | −3.458 | 0.093 | 0.031 |
| 2-stars | −0.019 | 0.002 | 0.982 |
| triangles | 0.473 | 0.011 | 1.606 |

Read as a logistic regression, each extra 2-star a tie would create lowers its odds by about 1.8%, and each
triangle it would close raises them by about 61% [@jacobucci_ml]. Triangles are what a shared friend
produces, so this is **transitivity**, measured. To see the size of it (our arithmetic): for two students who
each already have about 20 friends, close to the network's average, the model puts the chance of a tie at
about 1.5% if they share no friends and about 14% if they share five.

**Attributes.** Covariates enter through pair-level terms: the **sum** of two students' values (does higher
GPA mean more ties?), the **absolute difference** (do similar people connect?, the
**homophily** principle), and indicators for **same group** or **both in a particular group**
[@jacobucci_ml]:

| Term | Estimate | SE | Odds ratio |
|---|---|---|---|
| ties | −2.917 | 0.257 | 0.054 |
| GPA (sum of the pair) | 0.088 | 0.039 | 1.092 |
| BMI (absolute difference) | −0.005 | 0.007 | 0.995 |
| same gender | 0.685 | 0.057 | 1.984 |
| both non-smokers | −0.025 | 0.059 | 0.975 |
| both smokers | 0.663 | 0.093 | 1.940 |

Same-gender pairs have about twice the odds of being friends, and so do pairs of smokers. Pairs of
non-smokers do not differ from the baseline, and similarity in BMI does not matter [@jacobucci_ml]. (The text
describes the first covariate as age; the table and the discussion are about GPA.)

There is a catch the authors flag: the statistics in an ERGM are typically not independent of one another,
which "can pose serious challenges" in estimation [@jacobucci_ml]. A triangle is made of 2-stars, and adding
one tie changes many statistics at once. The probability of the whole network also has a normalising
constant that sums over every possible network, the same problem as the partition function of a
[Markov random field](../ml-track/markov-random-fields.qmd), which an ERGM is, with one variable per pair.
Reading the model one tie at a time, as above, is the pseudo-likelihood idea from that entry, and it carries
the same warning when the dependence is strong (our link, not the authors').

## Latent space models: ties depend on distance

A third approach explains ties by **unobserved** positions rather than observed patterns [@jacobucci_ml]. In
the latent space model of Hoff, Raftery and Handcock (2002), every person has a position $z_i$ in an unseen
space. The log-odds of a tie is an intercept, plus covariate effects, *minus the distance* between the two
positions, so people who sit close together are likely to be tied [@jacobucci_ml]. The positions do the work
that triangles do in an ERGM. If $i$ is near $j$ and $j$ is near $k$, then $i$ is not far from $k$, so
transitivity comes for free.

The dimension of the space is chosen by [BIC](../02-inference/bayesian-model-comparison.qmd). For WeChat,
models with 1 to 9 dimensions were compared and two dimensions won, so the friendships are best summarised as
a map in the plane [@jacobucci_ml]. Adding the same covariates as before (fitted by MCMC, so the intervals are
credible intervals):

| Term | Estimate | 95% interval | Odds ratio |
|---|---|---|---|
| intercept | 1.284 | 0.446 to 2.197 | 3.611 |
| GPA | −0.087 | −0.221 to 0.035 | 0.916 |
| BMI | −0.017 | −0.037 to 0.001 | 0.983 |
| same gender | 0.964 | 0.835 to 1.101 | 2.623 |
| both non-smokers | −0.091 | −0.238 to 0.057 | 0.913 |
| both smokers | 1.000 | 0.729 to 1.274 | 2.719 |

The authors read these as similar to the ERGM: same-gender pairs and pairs of smokers are more likely to be
friends, now **conditional on the latent positions**, that is, with the social map held fixed
[@jacobucci_ml]. One difference is worth noticing (our reading): GPA, which was a significant positive term
in the ERGM, has an interval that includes zero here and a negative point estimate. Once the map absorbs who
clusters with whom, the GPA effect is no longer clear. Covariate effects in network models depend on what
else the model uses to explain the structure.

The price is computation. The latent space fit took about ten minutes; the covariate ERGM took less than
half a second on the same desktop, and both grow quickly with network size [@jacobucci_ml].

## Choosing among them

The three families relax the coin-flip baseline in different directions, and the choice follows the
question:

- **Groups or roles** (factions, cores and peripheries, strata): a stochastic block model, or its
  mixed-membership version when people straddle groups.
- **Mechanisms** (does transitivity matter once homophily is accounted for?): an ERGM, whose terms are
  hypotheses about how ties form.
- **A map and covariate effects net of it**: a latent space model.

Jacobucci and colleagues note that the same methods extend to directed and valued networks, and that
networks observed over time have their own models: temporal ERGMs, stochastic actor-oriented models
(Snijders and colleagues) and dynamic network models [@jacobucci_ml]. In R, the **statnet** suite, with its
`ergm` and `latentnet` packages, is the standard toolkit [@jacobucci_ml].

## Common pitfalls

::: {.callout-warning title="Where this goes wrong"}
- **Treating ties as independent observations.** The Erdős–Rényi model, and any analysis that treats pairs
  as separate data points, ignores transitivity, which is one of the strongest regularities in social
  networks. [@jacobucci_ml]
- **Squeezing the network into one number and stopping.** Degree or PageRank in a regression uses the
  network only indirectly. In the WeChat data degree showed nothing, while the ERGM found strong gender and
  smoking homophily in who is tied to whom. [@jacobucci_ml]
- **Letting a criterion pick the number of blocks unchecked.** WeChat's best-ICL fit had blocks of one, two,
  four and five students. Look at the blocks before you accept $K$. [@jacobucci_ml]
- **Reading blocks as communities.** A block can be a role, such as a periphery, whose members never tie to
  one another. Modularity-based methods cannot find these. [@murphy_ml]
- **Reading zeros as choices.** A missing tie may be a missing opportunity or a missing record; absence of
  evidence is not evidence of absence. [@murphy_ml]
- **Fitting an ERGM as if its terms were separate regressors.** Network statistics overlap and are
  typically not independent, which poses serious challenges for estimation. [@jacobucci_ml]
- **Comparing covariate effects across model families.** A latent space model's effects are conditional on
  the latent positions; GPA's effect changes sign between the WeChat ERGM and latent space fit.
  [@jacobucci_ml]
- **Calling homophily a cause.** "Smokers befriend smokers" fits selection and influence alike, and the
  pattern alone cannot separate them (see [community detection](community-detection.qmd) and
  [peer effects](../05-causal-inference/peer-effects-and-spillovers.qmd)). [@menczer_network]
:::

## Appendix · the mathematics {#sec-network-models-math .unnumbered}

::: {.math-appendix}

**Erdős–Rényi MLE.** For an undirected network with adjacency $A$, $L(p)=\prod_{i<j}p^{a_{ij}}(1-p)^{1-a_{ij}}$.
Setting the derivative of $\log L$ to zero gives $\hat p = M/\binom N2$, with $M$ the number of ties.

**Stochastic block model.** With block labels $q_i\in\{1,\dots,K\}$, $q_i\sim\mathrm{Cat}(\pi)$ and
$a_{ij}\mid q_i=a,q_j=b\sim\mathrm{Ber}(\eta_{ab})$,
$$
\log p(A\mid q,\eta)=\sum_{a\le b}\Big[m_{ab}\log\eta_{ab}+(n_{ab}-m_{ab})\log(1-\eta_{ab})\Big],
$$
where $m_{ab}$ counts ties and $n_{ab}$ pairs between blocks $a$ and $b$ ($n_{aa}=\binom{n_a}{2}$). For fixed
labels $\hat\eta_{ab}=m_{ab}/n_{ab}$, which is the "known blocks" calculation. The figure maximises this
profile likelihood over labels by single-node moves. Because $p(q\mid A)$ does not factorise over nodes, the
E-step of exact EM is intractable, hence variational EM (a factorised $\prod_i r_i(q_i)$) or collapsed Gibbs.
**ICL** (Biernacki et al. 2000) scores a $K$ by the complete-data log-likelihood at the best labels, with
BIC-style penalties for the $K(K+1)/2$ block probabilities and the $K-1$ block proportions.

**Mixed membership SBM.** $\pi_i\sim\mathrm{Dir}(\alpha)$; for each ordered pair,
$q_{i\to j}\sim\mathrm{Cat}(\pi_i)$, $q_{i\leftarrow j}\sim\mathrm{Cat}(\pi_j)$, and
$P(a_{ij}=1\mid q_{i\to j}=a,q_{i\leftarrow j}=b)=\eta_{ab}$. The robust version for unobserved zeros is
$p(a_{ij}=r)=\rho\,\delta_0(r)+(1-\rho)\,\mathrm{Ber}(r\mid\eta_{ab})$.

**ERGM.** $\Pr(Y=A)=\exp\{\beta^\top s(A)\}/c(\beta)$ with $c(\beta)=\sum_{A'}\exp\{\beta^\top s(A')\}$ over
all $2^{\binom N2}$ networks. Writing $A^+_{ij}$ and $A^-_{ij}$ for $A$ with the tie $ij$ forced on and off,
$$
\operatorname{logit}P(a_{ij}=1\mid A_{-ij})=\beta^\top\big[s(A^+_{ij})-s(A^-_{ij})\big],
$$
and $c(\beta)$ cancels. Change statistics: ties $=1$; 2-stars $=k_i+k_j$ (degrees not counting $ij$);
triangles $=$ the number of shared neighbours of $i$ and $j$. With Table 12.7 and $k_i=k_j=20$:
$\operatorname{logit}P=-3.458-0.019(40)+0.473\,t$, giving $P=0.0145$ at $t=0$ and $0.136$ at $t=5$. The
mean degree in the WeChat network is $2(1{,}722)/165\approx20.9$. Maximising the product of these
conditionals is maximum pseudo-likelihood; the full likelihood needs $c(\beta)$, usually handled by MCMC.

**Node-covariate terms.** Main effect $h_{ij}=x_i+x_j$; absolute difference $|x_i-x_j|$; nodal factor
$h_{ij}\in\{0,1,2\}$ (how many of the pair have the level); uniform homophily $\mathbb I(x_i=x_j)$;
differential homophily $\mathbb I(x_i=x_j=\ell)$.

**Latent space model.** $a_{ij}\sim\mathrm{Ber}(p_{ij})$, $\operatorname{logit}p_{ij}=\alpha+\beta^\top h_{ij}-\lVert z_i-z_j\rVert$,
with the distance coefficient fixed at $-1$ to set the scale. Distances obey the triangle inequality, so
$\lVert z_i-z_k\rVert\le\lVert z_i-z_j\rVert+\lVert z_j-z_k\rVert$: two ties through $j$ force $i$ and $k$
to be fairly close, which is how the model produces transitivity without a triangle term.

:::
