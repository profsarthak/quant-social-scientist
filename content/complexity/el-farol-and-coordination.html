El Farol & Coordination Without a Coordinator

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source

Nobody wants to go where everybody goes. Simple rules of thumb, each a guess about what the others will do, can keep a crowd at the right size better than chance would. And towns form whether people like each other or can’t stand each other, which is a warning for anyone trying to read motives off a map.

Some problems are about avoiding each other. A beach, a bar or a road is pleasant only if not too many others choose it, and there is no ticket office deciding who goes. Miller and Page show three surprises. An equilibrium can exist that the system will almost never reach. A small “ecology” of rules of thumb can coordinate a crowd more smoothly than everyone tossing a coin. And very different motives (loving company or fleeing it) can produce the same pattern of cities, so the pattern alone cannot tell you which motive was at work.

Why it matters

Social scientists are trained to look for the equilibrium: write down what each person wants, find the state where nobody wants to change, and predict that state. Miller and Page’s chapter on social dynamics is a set of small, checkable models that show where that habit fails (Miller and Page 2007). In one, the equilibrium exists but is unstable and almost nothing leads to it. In another, people reach a good collective result without anyone computing an equilibrium, just by acting on simple guesses about the others. In a third, opposite preferences produce the same aggregate result.

Each lesson matters for empirical work. If equilibria are fragile or hard to reach, a model that predicts “the equilibrium” can be confidently wrong. If coordination arises from interacting rules of thumb, then traffic, attendance, market entry and crowding can be more stable than any one person’s behaviour suggests. And if different motives produce the same outcome, then observing the outcome does not identify the motive. That is the identification problem from causal inference, appearing in a model of cities.

An equilibrium you will never reach

Miller and Page start with a single agent walking along a line of sites numbered 0 to N (Miller and Page 2007). In the lower half, at site j \le N/2, it jumps to 2j. In the upper half it jumps to 2(N-j). That is a discrete version of the tent map from chaos theory. The rule has two fixed points, sites where the agent would stay forever: site 0, and site \tfrac23 N when that is a whole number.

Because the system is finite and deterministic, it must eventually repeat itself and cycle. Starting from site 1, the cycle the agent falls into grows with the size of the line (Miller and Page 2007):

Sites N 10 100 1,000 10,000 100,000
Cycle length 2 10 50 250 1,250

Take N = 102, so the fixed point is at 68 (since 2(102-68) = 68). An agent at 68 stays. One step off, at 67, and it goes 70, 64, 76, 52, 100, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, … and is trapped in a cycle of length eight. Each mistake doubles: one site too low becomes two too high, then four too low. That is what an unstable equilibrium means. The fixed point also has a tiny basin of attraction, the set of starting points that lead to it. Only sites 17, 34, 85 and 68 end at 68, and only 0, 51 and 102 end at 0 (Miller and Page 2007). (We recomputed both the table and the basins.)

So the model has equilibria, and a textbook analysis would find them. Yet almost every starting point, and any small amount of noise, leads somewhere else. Miller and Page’s conclusion: when there are several equilibria, the fixed points have small basins, and they are unstable, classical fixed-point analysis may predict very little. The more useful questions are which outcomes are likely and what links behaviour to which one is selected, and those are questions you answer by simulating (Miller and Page 2007).

The beach problem

Schelling’s less famous model is about the beach (Miller and Page 2007). Every Saturday the whole city decides whether to go. An uncrowded beach is lovely; a crowded one is worse than staying at home. How does the city avoid the crowd?

There are top-down answers, such as selling tickets or admitting cars by the parity of their licence plates. Game theory offers a bottom-up one, a mixed strategy. Suppose a million people live in the city and the beach holds a hundred thousand comfortably. Each person rolls a ten-sided die and goes only on a “beach”. On average exactly the right number turn up. The randomness hardly matters: the standard deviation of the crowd is only about 300, so the crowd is almost never more than a thousand people from the target (Miller and Page 2007).

The trouble is that nobody decides like that. People use predictive models. “It was packed last week, so it’ll be empty this week.” “It’s been busy three Saturdays out of five, so it’ll stay busy.” Different people hold different models, the models do better or worse, and people switch to the ones that work. The models compete and adapt to each other rather like species in an ecosystem (Miller and Page 2007).

Brian Arthur’s (1994) El Farol problem is this beach story moved to a Santa Fe bar, as Miller and Page report it. Each agent had several forecasting rules competing for its attention on their past accuracy. The adaptive system was efficient: on average the bar held the right number of people. Later work, mostly by physicists, found that such systems can show less variation in attendance than the dice would, even though different people go each week (Zambrano 2004, as reported) (Miller and Page 2007).

A four-person ecology you can check by hand

Miller and Page make the idea concrete with four friends, A, B, C and D (Miller and Page 2007). Their weekly meet-up is great fun if exactly two come and worse than staying home otherwise. Everyone knows past attendance. All four came in week 1, and only A and B in week 2. Each person has a rule:

  • A goes if average attendance over the last two weeks was below two.
  • B goes unless exactly two came last week.
  • C goes if more came last week than the week before.
  • D goes if exactly two came last week.

In week 3, A stays home (the average was three), B stays home (exactly two came), C stays home (attendance fell) and D goes. Carry on and from week 4 the group cycles AB, ACD, BC, D forever. Average attendance is exactly two, and the variance is ½ (Miller and Page 2007). If everyone flipped a coin instead, average attendance would also be two, but the variance would be 1. The ecology of rules does better than the mixed strategy: the same mean with half the variation.

Miller and Page are careful about how much this shows. They chose the rules (they insist the choice was essentially arbitrary), and a different set might do worse. Then they let one person adapt. Suppose A changes her rule to “go if the two-week average was below two, or if exactly three came the week before last”. From week 11 a new cycle appears: AD, D, AB, ACD, BC. The average is still two. Now exactly two people come 60% of the time instead of 50%, and the variance falls further (Miller and Page 2007) (to 0.4, our arithmetic). By adapting, the group found a better collective pattern without anyone planning one.

Adaptation does not guarantee a happy ending. If the pattern were ABCD, A, ABCD, A, … everyone would be unhappy and someone would change rule. Whether such a process settles down at all is an open question in general (Miller and Page 2007).

The minority game

Physicists turned the beach into the minority game (Challet and Zhang, as reported). An odd number of players each choose A or B, and whoever is in the minority shares a prize (Miller and Page 2007). Each player remembers the last M winning sides. A strategy is a lookup table from each of the 2^M possible histories to a choice. Every so often the worst performer is replaced by a copy of the best, or, with small probability, by a random strategy. The copying exploits what works; the random replacements keep exploring.

The key quantity is \rho = 2^M/N, the number of possible histories per player. If \rho is too small, the strategies are too alike to coordinate. If it is too large, the strategy space is so big that players wander around in it and coordination fails again. There is a critical value in between at which the size of the minority varies least (Miller and Page 2007). Miller and Page see a possibly deep idea here, emergent cognitive depth: adaptive agents may learn to think just deeply enough to coordinate, and no deeper.

Cities from love and from hate

The last model asks where people live (Miller and Page 2007). Sites lie on a line, each able to hold any number of agents. Two quantities stand in for everything that drives location: your home population (how many others share your site) and your average distance to everyone else. Agents update one at a time in random order and move to the best site they can find. Search can range from the whole line, to the neighbouring sites only, to a hill-climb that keeps stepping one site over while that helps.

Three kinds of agent behave as follows:

  • Agents who like company, by maximising home population or minimising average distance, end up in a single city when they can search the whole line. Where it forms depends on the initial conditions. With only local search, several cities can form, spaced further apart than the search reaches (Miller and Page 2007). This is not surprising.
  • Agents who dislike company, by maximising average distance, do something odd. They do not spread out. They pile into two cities at the two ends of the line (Miller and Page 2007). Miller and Page joke that this might explain the motives of people living in New York and Los Angeles.

The argument is short. For an agent at site i, stepping one site left moves it one closer to everyone on its left and one further from everyone on its right, and also gains a step of distance from anyone who shared its site. So it moves left exactly when more agents are to its right than to its left. As it passes others the imbalance only grows, so it keeps going until it reaches the end (Miller and Page 2007). The same holds on a grid with city-block distance and on a circle (Page 1998, as reported).

So loving others and hating them lead to the same macro pattern: people concentrated in cities. Miller and Page call this deductive indeterminism: one consequence (people living in cities) with several irreconcilable causes. Their line is worth remembering: when the details do not matter, it is hard to deduce cause (Miller and Page 2007).

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# --- Left: the four-person attendance ecology (M&P Tables 9.4-9.5) ---
def next_week(h, a_changed):
    last, prev = len(h[-1]), len(h[-2])
    go = {"A": (last + prev) / 2 < 2 or (a_changed and prev == 3),
          "B": last != 2,
          "C": last > prev,
          "D": last == 2}
    return "".join(p for p in "ABCD" if go[p])

hist = ["ABCD", "AB"]
for week in range(3, 21):
    hist.append(next_week(hist, a_changed=(week >= 11)))
rules = np.array([len(s) for s in hist])

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
coins = rng.binomial(4, 0.5, size=20)

# --- Right: city formation on a line (M&P sec. 9.4), hill-climbing search of size 1 ---
def cities(pref, N=40, A=200, seed=0):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    loc = rng.integers(0, N + 1, A)
    start = np.bincount(loc, minlength=N + 1)
    def score(i, x):
        others = np.delete(loc, i)
        return np.abs(others - x).mean() if pref == "hate" else np.sum(others == x)
    for _ in range(50):
        moved = 0
        for i in rng.permutation(A):
            x = loc[i]
            while True:
                cand = [c for c in (x - 1, x + 1) if 0 <= c <= N]
                best = max(cand, key=lambda c: score(i, c))
                if score(i, best) > score(i, x):
                    x = best
                else:
                    break
            if x != loc[i]:
                loc[i] = x; moved += 1
        if moved == 0:
            break
    return start, np.bincount(loc, minlength=N + 1)

start, like_end = cities("pop")
_, hate_end = cities("hate")
sites = np.arange(len(start))

What this means for a social scientist

Put the four models together and the message is about inference, not just about bars and beaches.

  • An equilibrium is not a prediction. Before relying on one, ask whether it is stable and how large its basin of attraction is. The roving agent has two equilibria that it will almost never reach.
  • Coordination can be an emergent property of heterogeneity. The four-person ecology coordinates because its members follow different rules. A representative-agent model in which everyone uses the same rule cannot produce that, as the emergence entry argues in general.
  • Aggregate outcomes can be insulated from motives. Miller and Page’s cities are an extreme case of “the details don’t matter”. That is good news for anyone predicting the aggregate and bad news for anyone trying to read individual motives from it. Seeing people clustered in cities, or in segregated neighbourhoods, does not tell you why they are there. The same caution sits behind the ecological fallacy and behind Schelling’s segregation model.

Common pitfalls

WarningWhere this goes wrong
  • Equating “has an equilibrium” with “goes to the equilibrium”. Check stability and the basin of attraction. An unstable fixed point with a handful of entry points describes almost none of the system’s behaviour. (Miller and Page 2007)
  • Generalising from a hand-picked ecology. The four-person rules beat the coin, but Miller and Page chose the rules, and say so. Show that a result holds across many randomly drawn or evolved rule sets before claiming that ecologies of heuristics coordinate well. (Miller and Page 2007)
  • Treating adaptation as convergence. Agents who switch rules may find a better pattern (the 60% cycle), or may never settle. Run long enough and report whether the dynamics converge at all. (Miller and Page 2007)
  • Reading motive off the map. Two cities at the ends of a line are what agents who hate company produce; a single city is what agents who love it produce; both are “cities”. An aggregate that many micro-motives can generate cannot identify which one is at work. (Miller and Page 2007)
  • Forgetting the search rule. With full search, company-lovers form one city; with local search they form several. How far agents look is part of the model, not a detail. (Miller and Page 2007)

Appendix · the mathematics

The roving agent. The map is f(j)=2j for j\le N/2 and f(j)=2(N-j) for j>N/2. A fixed point solves f(j)=j: either j=0, or 2(N-j)=j \Rightarrow j=\tfrac23N. Near j^*=\tfrac23N the slope is |f'|=2>1, so an error \varepsilon becomes -2\varepsilon, then 4\varepsilon, and so on: the fixed point is unstable. In the continuous tent map on (0,1) the same slope makes almost every orbit wander forever, which is why the cycle length grows with N on the lattice (Miller and Page 2007).

Mixed-strategy beach. With n=10^6 people each going independently with probability p=0.1, the crowd is Binomial(n,p), with mean np=10^5 and standard deviation \sqrt{np(1-p)}=\sqrt{90{,}000}=300. A deviation of 1,000 is about 3.3 standard deviations, so by the normal approximation it is exceeded less than 1% of the time.

Four-person variances. For the cycle AB, ACD, BC, D the attendances are (2,3,2,1): mean 2, variance (0+1+0+1)/4=\tfrac12. For AD, D, AB, ACD, BC they are (2,1,2,3,2): mean 2, variance (0+1+0+1+0)/5=0.4, and exactly two in 3 of 5 weeks. Four independent fair coins give Binomial(4,\tfrac12), with variance 4\cdot\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12=1.

Why distance-maximisers go to the ends. With populations p_l at sites l, an agent at i has total distance D(i)=\sum_{l<i}p_l(i-l)+\sum_{r>i}p_r(r-i). Moving to i-1 changes this by -\sum_{l<i}p_l+\sum_{r>i}p_r+p_i', where p_i' counts the others who shared site i (each now one step away). Ignoring that small extra term, the agent moves left when \sum_{r>i}p_r>\sum_{l<i}p_l and right in the reverse case. Each agent it passes switches from its left to its right, which widens the imbalance, so the move continues to the end point. If everyone but one agent is already at the two ends, D(i)=p_0 i+p_N(N-i)=(p_0-p_N)i+p_NN, which is maximised at i=N when p_0>p_N and at i=0 when p_0<p_N. Only a lone agent with equal numbers on each side can stay in between, and a city of two or more cannot form there because leaving gains distance from its fellow residents (Miller and Page 2007).

Minority game. With memory M, a strategy maps each of the 2^M binary histories to a choice, so there are 2^{2^M} possible strategies. The control parameter is \rho=2^M/N. The variance of the number of agents in the minority is smallest at an intermediate, critical \rho (Miller and Page 2007).

Some problems are about **avoiding** each other. A beach, a bar or a road is pleasant only if not too many
others choose it, and there is no ticket office deciding who goes. Miller and Page show three surprises.
An equilibrium can exist that the system will almost never reach. A small "ecology" of rules of thumb can
coordinate a crowd more smoothly than everyone tossing a coin. And very different motives (loving company
or fleeing it) can produce the **same** pattern of cities, so the pattern alone cannot tell you which motive
was at work.

## Why it matters

Social scientists are trained to look for the equilibrium: write down what each person wants, find the
state where nobody wants to change, and predict that state. Miller and Page's chapter on social dynamics is
a set of small, checkable models that show where that habit fails [@millerpage_cas]. In one, the
equilibrium exists but is unstable and almost nothing leads to it. In another, people reach a good
collective result without anyone computing an equilibrium, just by acting on simple guesses about the others.
In a third, opposite preferences produce the same aggregate result.

Each lesson matters for empirical work. If equilibria are fragile or hard to reach, a model that predicts
"the equilibrium" can be confidently wrong. If coordination arises from interacting rules of thumb, then
traffic, attendance, market entry and crowding can be more stable than any one person's behaviour suggests.
And if different motives produce the same outcome, then observing the outcome does not **identify** the
motive. That is the identification problem from [causal inference](../05-causal-inference/index.qmd),
appearing in a model of cities.

## An equilibrium you will never reach {#sec-roving-agent}

Miller and Page start with a single agent walking along a line of sites numbered 0 to $N$
[@millerpage_cas]. In the lower half, at site $j \le N/2$, it jumps to $2j$. In the upper half it jumps to
$2(N-j)$. That is a discrete version of the **tent map** from chaos theory. The rule has two fixed points,
sites where the agent would stay forever: site 0, and site $\tfrac23 N$ when that is a whole number.

Because the system is finite and deterministic, it must eventually repeat itself and cycle. Starting from
site 1, the cycle the agent falls into grows with the size of the line [@millerpage_cas]:

| Sites $N$ | 10 | 100 | 1,000 | 10,000 | 100,000 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cycle length | 2 | 10 | 50 | 250 | 1,250 |

Take $N = 102$, so the fixed point is at 68 (since $2(102-68) = 68$). An agent at 68 stays. One step off, at
67, and it goes 70, 64, 76, 52, 100, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, … and is trapped in a cycle of length eight. Each
mistake doubles: one site too low becomes two too high, then four too low. That is what an **unstable**
equilibrium means. The fixed point also has a tiny **basin of attraction**, the set of starting points that
lead to it. Only sites 17, 34, 85 and 68 end at 68, and only 0, 51 and 102 end at 0 [@millerpage_cas]. (We
recomputed both the table and the basins.)

So the model has equilibria, and a textbook analysis would find them. Yet almost every starting point, and
any small amount of noise, leads somewhere else. Miller and Page's conclusion: when there are several
equilibria, the fixed points have small basins, and they are unstable, classical fixed-point analysis may
predict very little. The more useful questions are *which* outcomes are likely and *what links behaviour to
which one is selected*, and those are questions you answer by simulating [@millerpage_cas].

## The beach problem {#sec-beach-problem}

Schelling's less famous model is about the beach [@millerpage_cas]. Every Saturday the whole city decides
whether to go. An uncrowded beach is lovely; a crowded one is worse than staying at home. How does the
city avoid the crowd?

There are top-down answers, such as selling tickets or admitting cars by the parity of their licence plates.
Game theory offers a bottom-up one, a **mixed strategy**. Suppose a million people live in the city and the
beach holds a hundred thousand comfortably. Each person rolls a ten-sided die and goes only on a "beach".
On average exactly the right number turn up. The randomness hardly matters: the standard deviation of the
crowd is only about 300, so the crowd is almost never more than a thousand people from the target
[@millerpage_cas].

The trouble is that nobody decides like that. People use **predictive models**. "It was packed last week,
so it'll be empty this week." "It's been busy three Saturdays out of five, so it'll stay busy." Different
people hold different models, the models do better or worse, and people switch to the ones that work. The
models compete and adapt to each other rather like species in an ecosystem [@millerpage_cas].

Brian Arthur's (1994) **El Farol** problem is this beach story moved to a Santa Fe bar, as Miller and Page
report it. Each agent had several forecasting rules competing for its attention on their past accuracy.
The adaptive system was efficient: on average the bar held the right number of people. Later work, mostly by
physicists, found that such systems can show **less** variation in attendance than the dice would, even
though different people go each week (Zambrano 2004, as reported) [@millerpage_cas].

## A four-person ecology you can check by hand {#sec-four-person}

Miller and Page make the idea concrete with four friends, A, B, C and D [@millerpage_cas]. Their weekly
meet-up is great fun if exactly two come and worse than staying home otherwise. Everyone knows past
attendance. All four came in week 1, and only A and B in week 2. Each person has a rule:

- **A** goes if average attendance over the last two weeks was below two.
- **B** goes unless exactly two came last week.
- **C** goes if more came last week than the week before.
- **D** goes if exactly two came last week.

In week 3, A stays home (the average was three), B stays home (exactly two came), C stays home (attendance
fell) and D goes. Carry on and from week 4 the group cycles AB, ACD, BC, D forever. Average attendance is
exactly two, and the variance is ½ [@millerpage_cas]. If everyone flipped a coin instead, average attendance
would also be two, but the variance would be 1. The ecology of rules does **better than the mixed
strategy**: the same mean with half the variation.

Miller and Page are careful about how much this shows. They chose the rules (they insist the choice was
essentially arbitrary), and a different set might do worse. Then they let one person adapt. Suppose A
changes her rule to "go if the two-week average was below two, **or** if exactly three came the week before
last". From week 11 a new cycle appears: AD, D, AB, ACD, BC. The average is still two. Now exactly two people
come 60% of the time instead of 50%, and the variance falls further [@millerpage_cas] (to 0.4, our
arithmetic). By adapting, the group found a better collective pattern without anyone planning one.

Adaptation does not guarantee a happy ending. If the pattern were ABCD, A, ABCD, A, … everyone would be
unhappy and someone would change rule. Whether such a process settles down at all is an open question in
general [@millerpage_cas].

## The minority game {#sec-minority-game}

Physicists turned the beach into the **minority game** (Challet and Zhang, as reported). An odd number of
players each choose A or B, and whoever is in the minority shares a prize [@millerpage_cas]. Each player
remembers the last $M$ winning sides. A strategy is a lookup table from each of the $2^M$ possible histories
to a choice. Every so often the worst performer is replaced by a copy of the best, or, with small
probability, by a random strategy. The copying exploits what works; the random replacements keep exploring.

The key quantity is $\rho = 2^M/N$, the number of possible histories per player. If $\rho$ is too small,
the strategies are too alike to coordinate. If it is too large, the strategy space is so big that players
wander around in it and coordination fails again. There is a critical value in between at which the size of
the minority varies least [@millerpage_cas]. Miller and Page see a possibly deep idea here, **emergent
cognitive depth**: adaptive agents may learn to think just deeply enough to coordinate, and no deeper.

## Cities from love and from hate {#sec-city-formation}

The last model asks where people live [@millerpage_cas]. Sites lie on a line, each able to hold any number of
agents. Two quantities stand in for everything that drives location: your **home population** (how many
others share your site) and your **average distance** to everyone else. Agents update one at a time in
random order and move to the best site they can find. Search can range from the whole line, to the
neighbouring sites only, to a hill-climb that keeps stepping one site over while that helps.

Three kinds of agent behave as follows:

- **Agents who like company, by maximising home population or minimising average distance**, end up in a
  single city when they can search the whole line. Where it forms depends on the initial conditions. With
  only local search, several cities can form, spaced further apart than the search reaches
  [@millerpage_cas]. This is not surprising.
- **Agents who dislike company, by maximising average distance**, do something odd. They do not spread out.
  They pile into **two cities at the two ends of the line** [@millerpage_cas]. Miller and Page joke that
  this might explain the motives of people living in New York and Los Angeles.

The argument is short. For an agent at site $i$, stepping one site left moves it one closer to everyone on
its left and one further from everyone on its right, and also gains a step of distance from anyone who shared
its site. So it moves left exactly when **more agents are to its right than to its left**. As it passes
others the imbalance only grows, so it keeps going until it reaches the end [@millerpage_cas]. The same holds
on a grid with city-block distance and on a circle (Page 1998, as reported).

So loving others and hating them lead to the **same** macro pattern: people concentrated in cities. Miller
and Page call this **deductive indeterminism**: one consequence (people living in cities) with several
irreconcilable causes. Their line is worth remembering: when the details do not matter, it is hard to deduce
cause [@millerpage_cas].

#| label: fig-el-farol-cities
#| fig-cap: "Two of Miller & Page's social-dynamics models, rebuilt by hand. Left: weekly attendance at the four-person meet-up, where two is ideal. The rule ecology (orange) settles from week 4 into the cycle AB, ACD, BC, D (mean 2, variance ½). When A changes her rule in week 11 it moves to AD, D, AB, ACD, BC (exactly two 60% of the time, variance 0.4). Grey shows one run of everyone tossing a fair coin each week, the mixed strategy, which has the same mean and variance 1. Right: where 200 agents on a line of 41 sites end up, starting from a random spread (top). Agents who seek the largest home population, searching only next door, form several towns (middle). Agents who seek the greatest average distance from everyone else crowd into two cities at the two ends (bottom). Opposite motives, the same urban result."
#| code-fold: true
#| code-summary: "Show the figure code"

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# --- Left: the four-person attendance ecology (M&P Tables 9.4-9.5) ---
def next_week(h, a_changed):
    last, prev = len(h[-1]), len(h[-2])
    go = {"A": (last + prev) / 2 < 2 or (a_changed and prev == 3),
          "B": last != 2,
          "C": last > prev,
          "D": last == 2}
    return "".join(p for p in "ABCD" if go[p])

hist = ["ABCD", "AB"]
for week in range(3, 21):
    hist.append(next_week(hist, a_changed=(week >= 11)))
rules = np.array([len(s) for s in hist])

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
coins = rng.binomial(4, 0.5, size=20)

# --- Right: city formation on a line (M&P sec. 9.4), hill-climbing search of size 1 ---
def cities(pref, N=40, A=200, seed=0):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    loc = rng.integers(0, N + 1, A)
    start = np.bincount(loc, minlength=N + 1)
    def score(i, x):
        others = np.delete(loc, i)
        return np.abs(others - x).mean() if pref == "hate" else np.sum(others == x)
    for _ in range(50):
        moved = 0
        for i in rng.permutation(A):
            x = loc[i]
            while True:
                cand = [c for c in (x - 1, x + 1) if 0 <= c <= N]
                best = max(cand, key=lambda c: score(i, c))
                if score(i, best) > score(i, x):
                    x = best
                else:
                    break
            if x != loc[i]:
                loc[i] = x; moved += 1
        if moved == 0:
            break
    return start, np.bincount(loc, minlength=N + 1)

start, like_end = cities("pop")
_, hate_end = cities("hate")
sites = np.arange(len(start))

fig = plt.figure(figsize=(9.6, 4.6))
gs = fig.add_gridspec(3, 2, width_ratios=[1.15, 1], hspace=0.55, wspace=0.28)
ax = fig.add_subplot(gs[:, 0])
weeks = np.arange(1, 21)
ax.axhline(2, color="#161613", lw=0.9, ls="--")
ax.plot(weeks, coins, "s-", color="#b7b6a8", lw=1.4, ms=4, label="everyone tosses a coin (variance 1)")
ax.plot(weeks, rules, "o-", color="#e8531d", lw=2, ms=5, label="rule ecology (variance ½, then 0.4)")
ax.axvline(10.5, color="#8a8a7e", lw=1, ls=":")
ax.text(10.7, 4.25, "A changes\nher rule", fontsize=8, color="#5e5e54", va="top")
ax.set_xlabel("week"); ax.set_ylabel("number who turn up (ideal = 2)")
ax.set_ylim(-0.3, 4.4); ax.set_xticks([1, 5, 10, 15, 20])
ax.set_title("Coordinating without a coordinator")
ax.legend(fontsize=8, frameon=False, loc="lower right")

panels = [(start, "#b7b6a8", "random start"),
          (like_end, "#5e5e54", "like company (largest home population)"),
          (hate_end, "#161613", "dislike company (greatest distance)")]
for r, (counts, col, lab) in enumerate(panels):
    a = fig.add_subplot(gs[r, 1])
    a.bar(sites, counts, color=col, width=0.85, edgecolor="#f4f3ec", linewidth=0.4)
    a.set_title(lab, fontsize=9, loc="left")
    a.text(0.5, 0.86, f"tallest bar: {counts.max()} agents", transform=a.transAxes,
           ha="center", fontsize=8, color="#5e5e54")
    a.set_xlim(-1, len(sites)); a.set_yticks([])
    if r < 2:
        a.set_xticklabels([])
    else:
        a.set_xlabel("site on the line")
fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()

## What this means for a social scientist

Put the four models together and the message is about **inference**, not just about bars and beaches.

- **An equilibrium is not a prediction.** Before relying on one, ask whether it is stable and how large its
  basin of attraction is. The roving agent has two equilibria that it will almost never reach.
- **Coordination can be an emergent property of heterogeneity.** The four-person ecology coordinates
  *because* its members follow different rules. A representative-agent model in which everyone uses the same
  rule cannot produce that, as the [emergence](emergence-and-self-organisation.qmd) entry argues in general.
- **Aggregate outcomes can be insulated from motives.** Miller and Page's cities are an extreme case of
  "the details don't matter". That is good news for anyone predicting the aggregate and bad news for anyone
  trying to read individual motives from it. Seeing people clustered in cities, or in segregated
  neighbourhoods, does not tell you why they are there. The same caution sits behind the
  [ecological fallacy](../00-overture/using-existing-data.qmd) and behind
  [Schelling's segregation model](emergence-and-self-organisation.qmd#sec-tipping-vs-segregation).

## Common pitfalls

::: {.callout-warning title="Where this goes wrong"}
- **Equating "has an equilibrium" with "goes to the equilibrium".** Check stability and the basin of
  attraction. An unstable fixed point with a handful of entry points describes almost none of the system's
  behaviour. [@millerpage_cas]
- **Generalising from a hand-picked ecology.** The four-person rules beat the coin, but Miller and Page chose
  the rules, and say so. Show that a result holds across many randomly drawn or evolved rule sets before
  claiming that ecologies of heuristics coordinate well. [@millerpage_cas]
- **Treating adaptation as convergence.** Agents who switch rules may find a better pattern (the 60% cycle),
  or may never settle. Run long enough and report whether the dynamics converge at all. [@millerpage_cas]
- **Reading motive off the map.** Two cities at the ends of a line are what agents who hate company
  produce; a single city is what agents who love it produce; both are "cities". An aggregate that many
  micro-motives can generate cannot identify which one is at work. [@millerpage_cas]
- **Forgetting the search rule.** With full search, company-lovers form one city; with local search they
  form several. How far agents look is part of the model, not a detail. [@millerpage_cas]
:::

## Appendix · the mathematics {.unnumbered}

::: {.math-appendix}

**The roving agent.** The map is $f(j)=2j$ for $j\le N/2$ and $f(j)=2(N-j)$ for $j>N/2$. A fixed point
solves $f(j)=j$: either $j=0$, or $2(N-j)=j \Rightarrow j=\tfrac23N$. Near $j^*=\tfrac23N$ the slope is
$|f'|=2>1$, so an error $\varepsilon$ becomes $-2\varepsilon$, then $4\varepsilon$, and so on: the fixed point
is **unstable**. In the continuous tent map on $(0,1)$ the same slope makes almost every orbit wander
forever, which is why the cycle length grows with $N$ on the lattice [@millerpage_cas].

**Mixed-strategy beach.** With $n=10^6$ people each going independently with probability $p=0.1$, the crowd
is Binomial$(n,p)$, with mean $np=10^5$ and standard deviation $\sqrt{np(1-p)}=\sqrt{90{,}000}=300$. A
deviation of 1,000 is about 3.3 standard deviations, so by the normal approximation it is exceeded less than
1% of the time.

**Four-person variances.** For the cycle AB, ACD, BC, D the attendances are $(2,3,2,1)$: mean 2, variance
$(0+1+0+1)/4=\tfrac12$. For AD, D, AB, ACD, BC they are $(2,1,2,3,2)$: mean 2, variance $(0+1+0+1+0)/5=0.4$,
and exactly two in 3 of 5 weeks. Four independent fair coins give Binomial$(4,\tfrac12)$, with variance
$4\cdot\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12=1$.

**Why distance-maximisers go to the ends.** With populations $p_l$ at sites $l$, an agent at $i$ has total
distance
$$
D(i)=\sum_{l<i}p_l(i-l)+\sum_{r>i}p_r(r-i).
$$
Moving to $i-1$ changes this by $-\sum_{l<i}p_l+\sum_{r>i}p_r+p_i'$, where $p_i'$ counts the others who shared
site $i$ (each now one step away). Ignoring that small extra term, the agent moves left when
$\sum_{r>i}p_r>\sum_{l<i}p_l$ and right in the reverse case. Each agent it passes switches from its left to
its right, which widens the imbalance, so the move continues to the end point. If everyone but one agent is already at the
two ends, $D(i)=p_0 i+p_N(N-i)=(p_0-p_N)i+p_NN$, which is maximised at $i=N$ when $p_0>p_N$ and at $i=0$ when
$p_0<p_N$. Only a lone agent with equal numbers on each side can stay in between, and a city of two or more
cannot form there because leaving gains distance from its fellow residents [@millerpage_cas].

**Minority game.** With memory $M$, a strategy maps each of the $2^M$ binary histories to a choice, so there
are $2^{2^M}$ possible strategies. The control parameter is $\rho=2^M/N$. The variance of the number of agents in
the minority is smallest at an intermediate, critical $\rho$ [@millerpage_cas].
