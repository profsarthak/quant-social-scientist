Phase Planes, Competition & Limit Cycles

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source

Two variables that push on each other can do what one variable never can: compete for a winner, settle on either side of a dividing line, or oscillate forever at a rhythm set by the system itself rather than by any outside force.

With one variable, a system can only settle down or run away. With two, it can also take turns. Draw the two variables as a point on a plane, and the rules of the system as arrows. The picture shows everything the system can do: which resting states exist, which basin a starting point drains into, and whether there is a closed loop the system will circle for ever. When that loop is isolated (a limit cycle), the oscillation’s size and rhythm come from the system’s own structure, and it returns to the same cycle after every disturbance.

Why it matters

Most of what social scientists model involves at least two quantities that respond to each other. Examples include two parties, two firms, two languages, prices and quantities, and income and spending. The bifurcations entry stays on a line, where Strogatz’s point is that the dynamics are “almost boring”: every trajectory settles at a fixed point or heads off to infinity (Strogatz 2015). Add a second dimension and three new things become possible:

  • Competition with a dividing line. Two stable outcomes, with a curve (not a single threshold value) separating the starting points that lead to each.
  • Rotation. Equilibria approached by damped “ringing,” or not approached at all.
  • Self-sustained oscillation. A rhythm that no outside force imposes and that the system returns to after every knock.

Strogatz’s own exercises point at social applications. They include his playful love-affair model and a Keynesian-cross model of a national economy that is predicted to oscillate in a limiting case (Strogatz 2015). Both are illustrations rather than fitted models, but the toolkit is the same one you would use to ask whether a real two-variable system has one equilibrium or several, and whether it settles, competes or cycles.

From a line to a plane

A two-dimensional system is a pair of rules, \dot x=f(x,y) and \dot y=g(x,y). At every point of the phase plane, those rules give an arrow, and a trajectory follows the arrows. The phase portrait draws a few representative trajectories, and it is the main object of study (Strogatz 2015). Three tools make sense of it:

  1. Fixed points, where both rates are zero.
  2. Nullclines, the curves where one of the rates is zero. Arrows cross a nullcline purely horizontally or purely vertically, so nullclines show at a glance where the flow turns.
  3. Linearisation. Near a fixed point, the system behaves like its linear approximation, given by the Jacobian matrix of partial derivatives (Strogatz 2015).

Linear systems are completely classified by two numbers, the trace \tau and the determinant \Delta of their matrix (Strogatz 2015):

Condition Fixed point What trajectories do
\Delta<0 saddle approach along one direction, leave along another
\Delta>0, \tau^2-4\Delta>0 node approach (or leave) without circling
\Delta>0, \tau^2-4\Delta<0 spiral circle while approaching (or leaving)
\tau<0 / \tau>0 stable / unstable (for nodes and spirals)
\tau=0, \Delta>0 centre closed orbits, neither approaching nor leaving

Saddles, nodes and spirals fill open regions of the (\tau,\Delta) plane. Centres, stars and degenerate nodes sit on borderline curves (Strogatz 2015). That difference matters. A small change in the parameters cannot turn a saddle into a node, but it can turn a centre into a spiral. Hold that thought: it is why so many textbook cycles are fragile.

Romeo and Juliet: the trace and determinant as personalities

Strogatz introduces the classification with a model of love affairs, from his 1988 paper (Strogatz 2015). Let R be Romeo’s love (positive) or hate (negative) for Juliet, and J hers for him. Romeo echoes Juliet: he warms up when she loves him. Juliet is fickle: the more Romeo loves her, the more she wants to run away. So \dot R=aJ and \dot J=-bR, with a,b>0. The origin is a centre, so the affair is a never-ending cycle of love and hate. At least, Strogatz notes, they manage simultaneous love a quarter of the time (Strogatz 2015).

The general linear model, \dot R=aR+bJ, \dot J=cR+dJ, turns each sign into a romantic style. For example, a>0, b>0 is a student’s “eager beaver,” who is spurred on by both Juliet’s feelings and his own (Strogatz 2015). In the worked example, two identically cautious lovers each damp their own feelings (a<0) but respond to the other’s (b>0). Then \tau=2a<0 and \Delta=a^2-b^2 (Strogatz 2015):

  • If caution outweighs responsiveness (a^2>b^2), the origin is a stable node and the relationship “always fizzles out to mutual indifference.” Excessive caution can lead to apathy.
  • If responsiveness wins (a^2<b^2), the origin is a saddle. The relationship becomes a love fest or a war depending on how it started, but either way the feelings end up mutual, along the line R=J.

The model is a joke with a serious point. The same two personalities can produce a stable outcome or an explosive one, and which you get depends on a single inequality between two parameters. That is exactly what the trace–determinant table encodes.

Rabbits versus sheep: competition and basins of attraction

Strogatz’s first nonlinear example is the Lotka–Volterra competition model. Rabbits (x) and sheep (y) compete for the same limited grass. Each grows logistically on its own, rabbits faster. Encounters hurt both, but the sheep usually nudge the rabbits aside, so conflict hurts rabbits more (Strogatz 2015). His specific version is \dot x = x(3-x-2y),\qquad \dot y = y(2-x-y), with coefficients chosen to fit this story but otherwise arbitrary (Strogatz 2015).

There are four fixed points (Strogatz 2015):

  • (0,0), both extinct: an unstable node.
  • (3,0), rabbits only: a stable node.
  • (0,2), sheep only: a stable node.
  • (1,1), coexistence: a saddle.

The coexistence point exists but cannot be reached in practice. Only trajectories that start exactly on its stable manifold go there. Every other trajectory ends up at one of the two single-species outcomes. Strogatz’s reading is that one species generally drives the other to extinction. The pattern recurs in other competition models and underlies the biologists’ principle of competitive exclusion: two species competing for the same limited resource typically cannot coexist (Strogatz 2015).

Two general ideas come out of the example (Strogatz 2015):

  • The basin of attraction of a stable point is the set of starting conditions that end up there.
  • The saddle’s stable manifold separates the two basins. It is the basin boundary, and its two branches are called separatrices.

In two dimensions the “tipping point” is a curve. Whether a system ends in one state or the other depends on where it starts relative to that curve, not on whether one variable crosses a fixed value. A small head start for the rabbits can matter more than a large one in the wrong direction.

TipA social exercise: the power of true believers

One of Strogatz’s exercises applies the same phase-plane reasoning to opinion change. It is based on Marvel et al. (2012), after Xie et al. (2011), and motivated by movements such as civil rights and women’s suffrage in the United States (Strogatz 2015). A fixed fraction p of the population are committed believers in opinion A, and nothing changes their minds. Everyone else holds A, B, or sits on the fence (AB). An argument for the other side moves a believer to the fence, and any argument pushes a fence-sitter to that side. The two remaining fractions, n_A and n_B, form a two-dimensional system.

Start with everyone except the committed holding B. The final state then changes discontinuously with p. Below a critical fraction most people still hold B, and above it everyone comes round to A. The exercise shows that the critical fraction is p_c=1-\sqrt3/2\approx0.134. In this model, only about 13% of the population need to be unwavering advocates (Strogatz 2015). As with the rabbits, the number belongs to a stylised model with random mixing. It is not an estimate for any real movement.

Limit cycles: oscillation from the inside

A limit cycle is an isolated closed trajectory: nearby trajectories are not closed but spiral towards it (stable) or away from it (unstable) (Strogatz 2015). Stable limit cycles model self-sustained oscillations, systems that oscillate without any periodic outside push. Strogatz’s examples are the heartbeat, pacemaker neurons, daily rhythms in body temperature and hormones, spontaneously oscillating chemical reactions, and dangerous self-excited vibrations in bridges and aircraft wings. In each case there is a preferred period, waveform and amplitude, and after a small disturbance the system returns to that cycle (Strogatz 2015).

The key claim is that limit cycles are inherently nonlinear. A linear system can have closed orbits, but never isolated ones: if \mathbf x(t) is periodic, so is c\,\mathbf x(t) for any constant c. So a linear oscillation’s amplitude is set entirely by its starting point, and any disturbance to it persists for ever. A limit cycle’s amplitude is set by the system’s structure (Strogatz 2015).

The classic example is the van der Pol oscillator, \ddot x+\mu(x^2-1)\dot x+x=0, which arose from the circuits of early radios. Its damping is positive for large swings and negative for small ones. Big oscillations are damped down, small ones are pumped up, and the system settles where energy dissipated balances energy supplied. For each \mu>0 there is a unique stable limit cycle (Strogatz 2015).

This explains Strogatz’s verdict on the famous Lotka–Volterra predator–prey model (rabbits and foxes). It does produce population cycles, but as a continuous family of neutral cycles around a centre, one for every starting point. Mathematical biologists dismiss it because it is not structurally stable, and because real predator–prey cycles have a characteristic amplitude. A realistic model should predict one closed orbit, or a few, not a continuum (Strogatz 2015). The same objection applies to fickle Juliet’s endless cycle.

Proving a cycle exists: Poincaré–Bendixson

Showing that a cycle exists is usually hard. The main tool in the plane is the Poincaré–Bendixson theorem. Suppose a trajectory stays for ever in a closed, bounded region R that contains no fixed points. Then it is either a closed orbit or spirals towards one (Strogatz 2015). In practice you build a trapping region, where the flow points inward all along the boundary, and cut out any fixed point inside it. Often the trick is to show that the only fixed point inside is a repeller (Strogatz 2015).

Strogatz’s showcase is glycolysis, the process by which cells break down sugar for energy. In yeast and muscle extracts it can oscillate with a period of several minutes. Sel’kov’s (1968) dimensionless model is \dot x=-x+ay+x^2y, \dot y=b-ay-x^2y, where x and y are concentrations of ADP and F6P (Strogatz 2015). The single fixed point becomes a repeller when its trace turns positive, which happens inside a region of the (a,b) plane. There the theorem guarantees a closed orbit, and numerical integration shows it is a stable limit cycle, for example at a=0.08, b=0.6 (Strogatz 2015).

The theorem’s most important consequence is negative: there is no chaos in the phase plane. A bounded trajectory away from fixed points must approach a closed orbit. Nothing more complicated is possible. In three or more dimensions the theorem no longer applies, and trajectories can wander for ever on a strange attractor (Strogatz 2015). So any claim of chaos from a two-variable continuous-time model is wrong. Chaos needs a third variable, or discrete time, as in the logistic map.

Hopf bifurcations: how oscillations are born

The bifurcations entry covers what happens when a real eigenvalue crosses zero: saddle-nodes, transcritical and pitchfork bifurcations. In two dimensions a stable fixed point has a second way to lose stability. A pair of complex eigenvalues can cross into the right half-plane together. That is a Hopf bifurcation (Strogatz 2015).

Supercritical (gentle). Picture a system that returns to equilibrium through damped oscillations, a “ringing” that dies away. As a parameter \mu changes, the damping weakens and then turns into growth. The equilibrium loses stability, and a small, nearly elliptical limit cycle appears around it. In the normal form \dot r=\mu r-r^3, \dot\theta=\omega+br^2, the cycle has radius \sqrt\mu. Strogatz gives two generic rules of thumb (Strogatz 2015):

  1. The cycle’s size grows from zero like \sqrt{\mu-\mu_c}.
  2. Its period is about 2\pi/\operatorname{Im}\lambda, evaluated at the bifurcation.

Subcritical (dangerous). In \dot r=\mu r+r^3-r^5, the cubic term is destabilising. For \mu<0 a stable equilibrium and a large stable cycle coexist, separated by an unstable cycle. As \mu rises, that unstable cycle “tightens like a noose” around the equilibrium. At \mu=0 it swallows the equilibrium, and the system must jump to the distant large-amplitude cycle (Strogatz 2015). The large oscillation then shows hysteresis: lowering \mu back to zero does not stop it. It persists until the stable and unstable cycles collide and annihilate at \mu=-\tfrac14 (Strogatz 2015). Strogatz’s examples are nerve cells, aeroelastic flutter of aircraft wings, and fluid instabilities.

The linearisation cannot tell the two apart, because in both cases a complex pair crosses the axis. Strogatz’s quick and dirty test is to use the computer. If a small cycle appears right after the equilibrium goes unstable, and shrinks back to zero when the parameter is reversed, the bifurcation is supercritical. Otherwise it is probably subcritical. He adds that computer experiments are not proofs (Strogatz 2015). There is also a degenerate case, such as the damped pendulum with damping passing through zero. Here the system becomes conservative at the bifurcation point and shows a band of closed orbits but no limit cycle on either side (Strogatz 2015).

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def rk4(f, z0, dt, n):
    z = np.array(z0, float); out = np.empty((n + 1, 2)); out[0] = z
    for i in range(n):
        k1 = f(z); k2 = f(z + dt / 2 * k1); k3 = f(z + dt / 2 * k2); k4 = f(z + dt * k3)
        z = z + dt / 6 * (k1 + 2 * k2 + 2 * k3 + k4); out[i + 1] = z
    return out

# --- Left: Lotka-Volterra competition (Strogatz Sec. 6.4) ---
lv = lambda z: np.array([z[0] * (3 - z[0] - 2 * z[1]), z[1] * (2 - z[0] - z[1])])
lv_back = lambda z: -lv(z)

fig, (axL, axR) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(8.6, 4.1))
for x0 in np.linspace(0.15, 3.3, 7):
    for y0 in np.linspace(0.15, 2.6, 6):
        tr = rk4(lv, (x0, y0), 0.02, 600)
        axL.plot(tr[:, 0], tr[:, 1], color="#b7b6a8", lw=0.8)
        axL.plot(tr[0, 0], tr[0, 1], ".", ms=3, color="#b7b6a8")

# stable manifold of the saddle (1,1): stable eigenvalue -1 - sqrt(2), eigenvector (1, 1/sqrt(2))
v = np.array([1.0, 1 / np.sqrt(2)]); v /= np.linalg.norm(v)
for s in (+1, -1):
    tr = rk4(lv_back, np.array([1.0, 1.0]) + s * 1e-4 * v, 0.005, 1400)
    keep = (tr[:, 0] > -0.01) & (tr[:, 0] < 3.6) & (tr[:, 1] > -0.01) & (tr[:, 1] < 2.9)
    tr = tr[: np.argmin(keep) if not keep.all() else len(tr)]
    axL.plot(tr[:, 0], tr[:, 1], color="#e8531d", lw=2.2)

axL.plot([3, 0], [0, 2], "o", ms=7, color="#161613")                       # stable nodes
axL.plot([1], [1], "o", ms=7, mfc="#f4f3ec", mec="#161613", mew=1.4)      # saddle
axL.plot([0], [0], "o", ms=6, mfc="#f4f3ec", mec="#5e5e54", mew=1.2)      # unstable node
axL.text(3.0, 0.12, "rabbits win", ha="center", fontsize=8.5, color="#161613")
axL.text(0.08, 2.12, "sheep win", fontsize=8.5, color="#161613")

axL.text(2.55, 1.45, "basin\nboundary", fontsize=8.5, color="#e8531d")

axL.set_xlabel("rabbits  x"); axL.set_ylabel("sheep  y")

# --- Right: Sel'kov glycolysis (Strogatz Example 7.3.2), a = 0.08, b = 0.6 ---
a, b = 0.08, 0.6
sk = lambda z: np.array([-z[0] + a * z[1] + z[0] ** 2 * z[1], b - a * z[1] - z[0] ** 2 * z[1]])
xs, ys = b, b / (a + b ** 2)                                  # the fixed point

axR.text(xs + 0.07, ys - 0.12, "unstable\nfixed point", fontsize=8, color="#5e5e54")
axR.text(1.75, 2.3, "stable limit cycle", fontsize=8.5, color="#161613")
axR.set_xlabel("ADP  x"); axR.set_ylabel("F6P  y")
axR.set_title("Glycolysis: a self-sustained cycle")
fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()

Common pitfalls

WarningWhere this goes wrong
  • Treating a centre as a robust prediction. Centres sit on a borderline of the trace–determinant plane, so any small friction or feedback turns them into spirals. The neutral cycles of the Lotka–Volterra predator–prey model are the standard example: not structurally stable, and unlike real cycles they have no characteristic amplitude (Strogatz 2015). A model that predicts a realistic rhythm needs a limit cycle.
  • Trusting the linearisation in borderline cases. Linearisation classifies saddles, nodes and spirals reliably, but not centres. At the Hopf point of \dot r=\mu r-r^3 the linearisation predicts a centre while the real origin is a weak stable spiral (Strogatz 2015).
  • Assuming oscillation needs an oscillating cause. A self-sustained cycle needs no periodic outside driver (Strogatz 2015). Finding a cycle in data is not evidence of a cyclical external shock. The rhythm may be built into the feedbacks themselves.
  • Claiming chaos from a two-variable flow. By Poincaré–Bendixson, a bounded continuous-time system in the plane can only settle to a fixed point or a closed orbit (Strogatz 2015). Erratic-looking output from such a model is noise, numerical error, or an unnoticed third variable.
  • Mistaking a subcritical Hopf for a gentle one. Both look the same in the linearisation. If reversing the parameter does not make the oscillation shrink back to zero, expect a jump and hysteresis (Strogatz 2015). See bifurcations for the one-dimensional version of the same trap.
  • Reading illustrative coefficients as findings. The rabbits–sheep and Romeo–Juliet coefficients are chosen to tell a story (Strogatz 2015). Competitive exclusion holds for these coefficients. Whether a real market or language contest has two basins or a stable coexistence point is an empirical question about its parameters.

Appendix · the mathematics

Linearisation. Near a fixed point (x^\*,y^\*), write u=x-x^\*, v=y-y^\*. Dropping quadratic terms, \begin{pmatrix}\dot u\\ \dot v\end{pmatrix}\approx \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix} f_x & f_y\\ g_x & g_y\end{pmatrix}_{(x^\*,y^\*)}}_{A} \begin{pmatrix}u\\ v\end{pmatrix}. The eigenvalues of A satisfy \lambda^2-\tau\lambda+\Delta=0, so \lambda_{1,2}=\tfrac12\left(\tau\pm\sqrt{\tau^2-4\Delta}\right),\qquad \tau=\lambda_1+\lambda_2,\quad \Delta=\lambda_1\lambda_2. \Delta<0 means real eigenvalues of opposite sign (a saddle). With \Delta>0, the discriminant \tau^2-4\Delta separates nodes (real) from spirals (complex), and the sign of \tau gives stability.

Rabbits versus sheep. For \dot x=x(3-x-2y), \dot y=y(2-x-y), A=\begin{pmatrix}3-2x-2y & -2x\\ -y & 2-x-2y\end{pmatrix}. At (1,1), A=\begin{pmatrix}-1&-2\\-1&-1\end{pmatrix}, so \tau=-2, \Delta=-1 and \lambda=-1\pm\sqrt2: a saddle. The stable direction (for \lambda=-1-\sqrt2) is (1,1/\sqrt2). The figure integrates backwards in time from just either side of (1,1) along it to trace the separatrix. At (3,0) the eigenvalues are -3 and -1, and at (0,2) they are -1 and -2: both stable nodes.

Why linear closed orbits are never isolated. If \dot{\mathbf x}=A\mathbf x and \mathbf x(t) is periodic, then \tfrac{d}{dt}(c\,\mathbf x)=A(c\,\mathbf x), so c\,\mathbf x(t) is also a periodic solution for every c\neq0. Closed orbits come in continuous families, and none is a limit cycle.

Hopf normal forms. In polar coordinates, \dot r=\mu r-r^3 has fixed points r=0 and r=\sqrt\mu (for \mu>0). The radial slope at \sqrt\mu is \mu-3\mu=-2\mu<0, so the cycle is stable and its radius grows like \sqrt\mu. In Cartesian form the Jacobian at the origin has eigenvalues \mu\pm i\omega, which cross the imaginary axis at \mu=0. For the subcritical form \dot r=\mu r+r^3-r^5, nonzero cycles satisfy r^4-r^2-\mu=0, so r^2=\tfrac12\left(1\pm\sqrt{1+4\mu}\right). The two roots (the large stable cycle and the small unstable one) merge and vanish when 1+4\mu=0, at \mu=-\tfrac14. This is the end of the hysteresis loop.

Sel’kov’s fixed point. Setting \dot y=0 and \dot x=0 gives x^\*=b, y^\*=b/(a+b^2). The Jacobian there has \Delta=a+b^2>0 and \tau=-\frac{b^4+(2a-1)b^2+(a+a^2)}{a+b^2}. At a=0.08, b=0.6, \tau=0.0864/0.44\approx0.196>0: an unstable spiral or node, so a trapping region with the fixed point removed contains a closed orbit. The boundary \tau=0 is b^2=\tfrac12\left(1-2a\pm\sqrt{1-8a}\right) (Strogatz 2015).

::: {.lead-kernel}
*With one variable, a system can only settle down or run away. With two, it can also **take turns**.*
Draw the two variables as a point on a plane, and the rules of the system as arrows. The picture shows
everything the system can do: which resting states exist, which **basin** a starting point drains into,
and whether there is a closed loop the system will circle for ever. When that loop is **isolated** (a
*limit cycle*), the oscillation's size and rhythm come from the system's own structure, and it returns
to the same cycle after every disturbance.

## Why it matters

Most of what social scientists model involves at least two quantities that respond to each other.
Examples include two parties, two firms, two languages, prices and quantities, and income and spending.
The [bifurcations entry](bifurcations-and-tipping-points.qmd) stays on a line, where Strogatz's point is
that the dynamics are "almost boring": every trajectory settles at a fixed point or heads off to
infinity [@strogatz_nonlinear]. Add a second dimension and three new things become possible:

- **Competition with a dividing line.** Two stable outcomes, with a curve (not a single threshold value)
  separating the starting points that lead to each.
- **Rotation.** Equilibria approached by damped "ringing," or not approached at all.
- **Self-sustained oscillation.** A rhythm that no outside force imposes and that the system returns to
  after every knock.

Strogatz's own exercises point at social applications. They include his playful **love-affair** model
and a **Keynesian-cross** model of a national economy that is predicted to oscillate in a limiting case
[@strogatz_nonlinear]. Both are illustrations rather than fitted models, but the toolkit is the same one
you would use to ask whether a real two-variable system has one equilibrium or several, and whether it
settles, competes or cycles.

## From a line to a plane

A two-dimensional system is a pair of rules, $\dot x=f(x,y)$ and $\dot y=g(x,y)$. At every point of the
**phase plane**, those rules give an arrow, and a trajectory follows the arrows. The **phase portrait**
draws a few representative trajectories, and it is the main object of study [@strogatz_nonlinear]. Three
tools make sense of it:

1. **Fixed points**, where both rates are zero.
2. **Nullclines**, the curves where one of the rates is zero. Arrows cross a nullcline purely
   horizontally or purely vertically, so nullclines show at a glance where the flow turns.
3. **Linearisation.** Near a fixed point, the system behaves like its linear approximation, given by the
   **Jacobian** matrix of partial derivatives [@strogatz_nonlinear].

Linear systems are completely classified by two numbers, the **trace** $\tau$ and the **determinant**
$\Delta$ of their matrix [@strogatz_nonlinear]:

| Condition | Fixed point | What trajectories do |
|---|---|---|
| $\Delta<0$ | **saddle** | approach along one direction, leave along another |
| $\Delta>0$, $\tau^2-4\Delta>0$ | **node** | approach (or leave) without circling |
| $\Delta>0$, $\tau^2-4\Delta<0$ | **spiral** | circle while approaching (or leaving) |
| $\tau<0$ / $\tau>0$ | stable / unstable | (for nodes and spirals) |
| $\tau=0$, $\Delta>0$ | **centre** | closed orbits, neither approaching nor leaving |

Saddles, nodes and spirals fill open regions of the $(\tau,\Delta)$ plane. Centres, stars and degenerate
nodes sit on borderline curves [@strogatz_nonlinear]. That difference matters. A small change in the
parameters cannot turn a saddle into a node, but it can turn a centre into a spiral. Hold that thought:
it is why so many textbook cycles are fragile.

## Romeo and Juliet: the trace and determinant as personalities

Strogatz introduces the classification with a model of love affairs, from his 1988 paper
[@strogatz_nonlinear]. Let $R$ be Romeo's love (positive) or hate (negative) for Juliet, and $J$ hers for
him. Romeo echoes Juliet: he warms up when she loves him. Juliet is fickle: the more Romeo loves her,
the more she wants to run away. So $\dot R=aJ$ and $\dot J=-bR$, with $a,b>0$. The origin is a
**centre**, so the affair is a never-ending cycle of love and hate. At least, Strogatz notes, they
manage simultaneous love a quarter of the time [@strogatz_nonlinear].

The general linear model, $\dot R=aR+bJ$, $\dot J=cR+dJ$, turns each sign into a romantic style. For
example, $a>0$, $b>0$ is a student's "eager beaver," who is spurred on by both Juliet's feelings and his
own [@strogatz_nonlinear]. In the worked example, two **identically cautious** lovers each damp their own
feelings ($a<0$) but respond to the other's ($b>0$). Then $\tau=2a<0$ and $\Delta=a^2-b^2$
[@strogatz_nonlinear]:

- If caution outweighs responsiveness ($a^2>b^2$), the origin is a stable node and the relationship
  "always fizzles out to mutual indifference." Excessive caution can lead to apathy.
- If responsiveness wins ($a^2<b^2$), the origin is a saddle. The relationship becomes a love fest or a
  war depending on how it started, but either way the feelings end up mutual, along the line $R=J$.

The model is a joke with a serious point. The *same* two personalities can produce a stable outcome or
an explosive one, and which you get depends on a single inequality between two parameters. That is
exactly what the trace–determinant table encodes.

## Rabbits versus sheep: competition and basins of attraction

Strogatz's first nonlinear example is the **Lotka–Volterra competition model**. Rabbits ($x$) and sheep
($y$) compete for the same limited grass. Each grows logistically on its own, rabbits faster. Encounters
hurt both, but the sheep usually nudge the rabbits aside, so conflict hurts rabbits more
[@strogatz_nonlinear]. His specific version is
$$
\dot x = x(3-x-2y),\qquad \dot y = y(2-x-y),
$$
with coefficients chosen to fit this story but otherwise arbitrary [@strogatz_nonlinear].

There are four fixed points [@strogatz_nonlinear]:

- $(0,0)$, both extinct: an unstable node.
- $(3,0)$, rabbits only: a stable node.
- $(0,2)$, sheep only: a stable node.
- $(1,1)$, coexistence: a **saddle**.

The coexistence point exists but cannot be reached in practice. Only trajectories that start exactly
on its **stable manifold** go there. Every other trajectory ends up at one of the two
single-species outcomes. Strogatz's reading is that one species generally drives the other to
extinction. The pattern recurs in other competition models and underlies the biologists' **principle of
competitive exclusion**: two species competing for the same limited resource typically cannot coexist
[@strogatz_nonlinear].

Two general ideas come out of the example [@strogatz_nonlinear]:

- The **basin of attraction** of a stable point is the set of starting conditions that end up there.
- The saddle's stable manifold separates the two basins. It is the **basin boundary**, and its two
  branches are called **separatrices**.

In two dimensions the "tipping point" is a *curve*. Whether a system ends in one state or the other
depends on where it starts relative to that curve, not on whether one variable crosses a fixed value. A
small head start for the rabbits can matter more than a large one in the wrong direction.

::: {.callout-tip title="A social exercise: the power of true believers"}
One of Strogatz's exercises applies the same phase-plane reasoning to opinion change. It is based on
Marvel et al. (2012), after Xie et al. (2011), and motivated by movements such as civil rights and
women's suffrage in the United States [@strogatz_nonlinear]. A fixed fraction $p$ of the population are
**committed** believers in opinion A, and nothing changes their minds. Everyone else holds A, B, or sits
on the fence (AB). An argument for the other side moves a believer to the fence, and any argument
pushes a fence-sitter to that side. The two remaining fractions, $n_A$ and $n_B$, form a
two-dimensional system.

Start with everyone except the committed holding B. The final state then changes **discontinuously**
with $p$. Below a critical fraction most people still hold B, and above it everyone comes round to A.
The exercise shows that the critical fraction is $p_c=1-\sqrt3/2\approx0.134$. In this model, only about
13% of the population need to be unwavering advocates [@strogatz_nonlinear]. As with the rabbits, the
number belongs to a stylised model with random mixing. It is not an estimate for any real movement.
:::

## Limit cycles: oscillation from the inside

A **limit cycle** is an *isolated* closed trajectory: nearby trajectories are not closed but spiral
towards it (stable) or away from it (unstable) [@strogatz_nonlinear]. Stable limit cycles model
**self-sustained oscillations**, systems that oscillate without any periodic outside push. Strogatz's
examples are the heartbeat, pacemaker neurons, daily rhythms in body temperature and hormones,
spontaneously oscillating chemical reactions, and dangerous self-excited vibrations in bridges and
aircraft wings. In each case there is a preferred period, waveform and amplitude, and after a small
disturbance the system returns to that cycle [@strogatz_nonlinear].

The key claim is that **limit cycles are inherently nonlinear**. A linear system can have closed orbits,
but never isolated ones: if $\mathbf x(t)$ is periodic, so is $c\,\mathbf x(t)$ for any constant $c$. So
a linear oscillation's amplitude is set entirely by its starting point, and any disturbance to it
persists for ever. A limit cycle's amplitude is set by the system's structure [@strogatz_nonlinear].

The classic example is the **van der Pol oscillator**, $\ddot x+\mu(x^2-1)\dot x+x=0$, which arose from
the circuits of early radios. Its damping is positive for large swings and negative for small ones. Big
oscillations are damped down, small ones are pumped up, and the system settles where energy dissipated
balances energy supplied. For each $\mu>0$ there is a unique stable limit cycle [@strogatz_nonlinear].

This explains Strogatz's verdict on the famous **Lotka–Volterra predator–prey** model (rabbits and
foxes). It does produce population cycles, but as a continuous family of neutral cycles around a
centre, one for every starting point. Mathematical biologists dismiss it because it is **not
structurally stable**, and because real predator–prey cycles have a characteristic amplitude. A
realistic model should predict one closed orbit, or a few, not a continuum [@strogatz_nonlinear]. The
same objection applies to fickle Juliet's endless cycle.

## Proving a cycle exists: Poincaré–Bendixson

Showing that a cycle exists is usually hard. The main tool in the plane is the **Poincaré–Bendixson
theorem**. Suppose a trajectory stays for ever in a closed, bounded region $R$ that contains no fixed
points. Then it is either a closed orbit or spirals towards one [@strogatz_nonlinear]. In practice you
build a **trapping region**, where the flow points inward all along the boundary, and cut out any fixed
point inside it. Often the trick is to show that the only fixed point inside is a **repeller**
[@strogatz_nonlinear].

Strogatz's showcase is **glycolysis**, the process by which cells break down sugar for energy. In yeast
and muscle extracts it can oscillate with a period of several minutes. Sel'kov's (1968) dimensionless
model is $\dot x=-x+ay+x^2y$, $\dot y=b-ay-x^2y$, where $x$ and $y$ are concentrations of ADP and F6P
[@strogatz_nonlinear]. The single fixed point becomes a repeller when its trace turns positive, which
happens inside a region of the $(a,b)$ plane. There the theorem guarantees a closed orbit, and
numerical integration shows it is a stable limit cycle, for example at $a=0.08$, $b=0.6$
[@strogatz_nonlinear].

The theorem's most important consequence is negative: **there is no chaos in the phase plane.** A
bounded trajectory away from fixed points must approach a closed orbit. Nothing more complicated is
possible. In three or more dimensions the theorem no longer applies, and trajectories can wander for
ever on a **strange attractor** [@strogatz_nonlinear]. So any claim of chaos from a two-variable
*continuous-time* model is wrong. Chaos needs a third variable, or discrete time, as in the [logistic
map](nonlinear-dynamics-and-chaos.qmd).

## Hopf bifurcations: how oscillations are born

The [bifurcations entry](bifurcations-and-tipping-points.qmd) covers what happens when a *real*
eigenvalue crosses zero: saddle-nodes, transcritical and pitchfork bifurcations. In two dimensions a
stable fixed point has a second way to lose stability. A pair of **complex** eigenvalues can cross into
the right half-plane together. That is a **Hopf bifurcation** [@strogatz_nonlinear].

**Supercritical (gentle).** Picture a system that returns to equilibrium through damped oscillations, a
"ringing" that dies away. As a parameter $\mu$ changes, the damping weakens and then turns into growth.
The equilibrium loses stability, and a small, nearly elliptical limit cycle appears around it. In the
normal form $\dot r=\mu r-r^3$, $\dot\theta=\omega+br^2$, the cycle has radius $\sqrt\mu$. Strogatz gives
two generic rules of thumb [@strogatz_nonlinear]:

1. The cycle's size grows from zero like $\sqrt{\mu-\mu_c}$.
2. Its period is about $2\pi/\operatorname{Im}\lambda$, evaluated at the bifurcation.

**Subcritical (dangerous).** In $\dot r=\mu r+r^3-r^5$, the cubic term is destabilising. For $\mu<0$ a
stable equilibrium and a large stable cycle coexist, separated by an **unstable cycle**. As $\mu$ rises,
that unstable cycle "tightens like a noose" around the equilibrium. At $\mu=0$ it swallows the
equilibrium, and the system must jump to the distant large-amplitude cycle [@strogatz_nonlinear]. The
large oscillation then shows **hysteresis**: lowering $\mu$ back to zero does not stop it. It persists
until the stable and unstable cycles collide and annihilate at $\mu=-\tfrac14$ [@strogatz_nonlinear].
Strogatz's examples are nerve cells, aeroelastic flutter of aircraft wings, and fluid instabilities.

The linearisation cannot tell the two apart, because in both cases a complex pair crosses the axis.
Strogatz's quick and dirty test is to use the computer. If a small cycle appears right after the
equilibrium goes unstable, and shrinks back to zero when the parameter is reversed, the bifurcation is
supercritical. Otherwise it is probably subcritical. He adds that computer experiments are not proofs
[@strogatz_nonlinear]. There is also a **degenerate** case, such as the damped pendulum
with damping passing through zero. Here the system becomes conservative at the bifurcation point and
shows a band of closed orbits but no limit cycle on either side [@strogatz_nonlinear].

```{python}
#| label: fig-phase-plane
#| fig-cap: "Two things a plane can do that a line cannot, both computed by hand (fourth-order Runge–Kutta). Left: Strogatz's rabbits-versus-sheep competition model, ẋ = x(3 − x − 2y), ẏ = y(2 − x − y). Grey trajectories from a grid of starting points drain to one of two stable nodes, rabbits only (3, 0) or sheep only (0, 2). The orange curve is the saddle's stable manifold, found by integrating backwards from the coexistence saddle at (1, 1). It is the basin boundary, so which species survives depends on which side of the curve you start. Right: Sel'kov's glycolysis model at a = 0.08, b = 0.6. A trajectory starting near the unstable fixed point spirals outwards (light grey) and one starting far outside spirals inwards (mid grey). Both converge on the same closed loop (ink), a stable limit cycle whose size is set by the equations, not by the starting point."
#| code-fold: true
#| code-summary: "Show the figure code (RK4 integration, separatrix and limit cycle, by hand)"

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def rk4(f, z0, dt, n):
    z = np.array(z0, float); out = np.empty((n + 1, 2)); out[0] = z
    for i in range(n):
        k1 = f(z); k2 = f(z + dt / 2 * k1); k3 = f(z + dt / 2 * k2); k4 = f(z + dt * k3)
        z = z + dt / 6 * (k1 + 2 * k2 + 2 * k3 + k4); out[i + 1] = z
    return out

# --- Left: Lotka-Volterra competition (Strogatz Sec. 6.4) ---
lv = lambda z: np.array([z[0] * (3 - z[0] - 2 * z[1]), z[1] * (2 - z[0] - z[1])])
lv_back = lambda z: -lv(z)

fig, (axL, axR) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(8.6, 4.1))
for x0 in np.linspace(0.15, 3.3, 7):
    for y0 in np.linspace(0.15, 2.6, 6):
        tr = rk4(lv, (x0, y0), 0.02, 600)
        axL.plot(tr[:, 0], tr[:, 1], color="#b7b6a8", lw=0.8)
        axL.plot(tr[0, 0], tr[0, 1], ".", ms=3, color="#b7b6a8")

# stable manifold of the saddle (1,1): stable eigenvalue -1 - sqrt(2), eigenvector (1, 1/sqrt(2))
v = np.array([1.0, 1 / np.sqrt(2)]); v /= np.linalg.norm(v)
for s in (+1, -1):
    tr = rk4(lv_back, np.array([1.0, 1.0]) + s * 1e-4 * v, 0.005, 1400)
    keep = (tr[:, 0] > -0.01) & (tr[:, 0] < 3.6) & (tr[:, 1] > -0.01) & (tr[:, 1] < 2.9)
    tr = tr[: np.argmin(keep) if not keep.all() else len(tr)]
    axL.plot(tr[:, 0], tr[:, 1], color="#e8531d", lw=2.2)

axL.plot([3, 0], [0, 2], "o", ms=7, color="#161613")                       # stable nodes
axL.plot([1], [1], "o", ms=7, mfc="#f4f3ec", mec="#161613", mew=1.4)      # saddle
axL.plot([0], [0], "o", ms=6, mfc="#f4f3ec", mec="#5e5e54", mew=1.2)      # unstable node
axL.text(3.0, 0.12, "rabbits win", ha="center", fontsize=8.5, color="#161613")
axL.text(0.08, 2.12, "sheep win", fontsize=8.5, color="#161613")
axL.text(1.12, 1.08, "saddle", fontsize=8, color="#5e5e54")
axL.text(2.55, 1.45, "basin\nboundary", fontsize=8.5, color="#e8531d")
axL.set_xlim(0, 3.5); axL.set_ylim(0, 2.8)
axL.set_xlabel("rabbits  x"); axL.set_ylabel("sheep  y")
axL.set_title("Competition: two basins")

# --- Right: Sel'kov glycolysis (Strogatz Example 7.3.2), a = 0.08, b = 0.6 ---
a, b = 0.08, 0.6
sk = lambda z: np.array([-z[0] + a * z[1] + z[0] ** 2 * z[1], b - a * z[1] - z[0] ** 2 * z[1]])
xs, ys = b, b / (a + b ** 2)                                  # the fixed point
inner = rk4(sk, (xs + 0.02, ys), 0.01, 6000)
outer = rk4(sk, (0.1, 2.9), 0.01, 6000)
cycle = rk4(sk, inner[-1], 0.01, 1200)                         # one lap after transients
axR.plot(inner[:3500, 0], inner[:3500, 1], color="#b7b6a8", lw=0.9)
axR.plot(outer[:2500, 0], outer[:2500, 1], color="#5e5e54", lw=0.9)
axR.plot(cycle[:, 0], cycle[:, 1], color="#161613", lw=2.4)
axR.plot(xs, ys, "o", ms=6, mfc="#f4f3ec", mec="#161613", mew=1.3)
axR.text(xs + 0.07, ys - 0.12, "unstable\nfixed point", fontsize=8, color="#5e5e54")
axR.text(1.75, 2.3, "stable limit cycle", fontsize=8.5, color="#161613")
axR.set_xlabel("ADP  x"); axR.set_ylabel("F6P  y")
axR.set_title("Glycolysis: a self-sustained cycle")
fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

## Common pitfalls

::: {.callout-warning title="Where this goes wrong"}
- **Treating a centre as a robust prediction.** Centres sit on a borderline of the trace–determinant
  plane, so any small friction or feedback turns them into spirals. The neutral cycles of the
  Lotka–Volterra predator–prey model are the standard example: not structurally stable, and unlike
  real cycles they have no characteristic amplitude [@strogatz_nonlinear]. A model that predicts a
  realistic rhythm needs a limit cycle.
- **Trusting the linearisation in borderline cases.** Linearisation classifies saddles, nodes and
  spirals reliably, but not centres. At the Hopf point of $\dot r=\mu r-r^3$ the linearisation predicts
  a centre while the real origin is a weak stable spiral [@strogatz_nonlinear].
- **Assuming oscillation needs an oscillating cause.** A self-sustained cycle needs no periodic outside
  driver [@strogatz_nonlinear]. Finding a cycle in data is not evidence of a cyclical external shock.
  The rhythm may be built into the feedbacks themselves.
- **Claiming chaos from a two-variable flow.** By Poincaré–Bendixson, a bounded continuous-time system
  in the plane can only settle to a fixed point or a closed orbit [@strogatz_nonlinear]. Erratic-looking
  output from such a model is noise, numerical error, or an unnoticed third variable.
- **Mistaking a subcritical Hopf for a gentle one.** Both look the same in the linearisation. If
  reversing the parameter does not make the oscillation shrink back to zero, expect a jump and
  hysteresis [@strogatz_nonlinear]. See [bifurcations](bifurcations-and-tipping-points.qmd) for the
  one-dimensional version of the same trap.
- **Reading illustrative coefficients as findings.** The rabbits–sheep and Romeo–Juliet coefficients are
  chosen to tell a story [@strogatz_nonlinear]. Competitive exclusion holds for *these* coefficients.
  Whether a real market or language contest has two basins or a stable coexistence point is an
  empirical question about its parameters.
:::

## Appendix · the mathematics {.unnumbered}

::: {.math-appendix}

**Linearisation.** Near a fixed point $(x^\*,y^\*)$, write $u=x-x^\*$, $v=y-y^\*$. Dropping quadratic
terms,
$$
\begin{pmatrix}\dot u\\ \dot v\end{pmatrix}\approx
\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix} f_x & f_y\\ g_x & g_y\end{pmatrix}_{(x^\*,y^\*)}}_{A}
\begin{pmatrix}u\\ v\end{pmatrix}.
$$
The eigenvalues of $A$ satisfy $\lambda^2-\tau\lambda+\Delta=0$, so
$$
\lambda_{1,2}=\tfrac12\left(\tau\pm\sqrt{\tau^2-4\Delta}\right),\qquad \tau=\lambda_1+\lambda_2,\quad \Delta=\lambda_1\lambda_2.
$$
$\Delta<0$ means real eigenvalues of opposite sign (a saddle). With $\Delta>0$, the discriminant
$\tau^2-4\Delta$ separates nodes (real) from spirals (complex), and the sign of $\tau$ gives stability.

**Rabbits versus sheep.** For $\dot x=x(3-x-2y)$, $\dot y=y(2-x-y)$,
$$
A=\begin{pmatrix}3-2x-2y & -2x\\ -y & 2-x-2y\end{pmatrix}.
$$
At $(1,1)$, $A=\begin{pmatrix}-1&-2\\-1&-1\end{pmatrix}$, so $\tau=-2$, $\Delta=-1$ and
$\lambda=-1\pm\sqrt2$: a saddle. The stable direction (for $\lambda=-1-\sqrt2$) is $(1,1/\sqrt2)$. The
figure integrates backwards in time from just either side of $(1,1)$ along it to trace the separatrix.
At $(3,0)$ the eigenvalues are $-3$ and $-1$, and at $(0,2)$ they are $-1$ and $-2$: both stable nodes.

**Why linear closed orbits are never isolated.** If $\dot{\mathbf x}=A\mathbf x$ and $\mathbf x(t)$ is
periodic, then $\tfrac{d}{dt}(c\,\mathbf x)=A(c\,\mathbf x)$, so $c\,\mathbf x(t)$ is also a periodic
solution for every $c\neq0$. Closed orbits come in continuous families, and none is a limit cycle.

**Hopf normal forms.** In polar coordinates, $\dot r=\mu r-r^3$ has fixed points $r=0$ and
$r=\sqrt\mu$ (for $\mu>0$). The radial slope at $\sqrt\mu$ is $\mu-3\mu=-2\mu<0$, so the cycle is stable
and its radius grows like $\sqrt\mu$. In Cartesian form the Jacobian at the origin has eigenvalues
$\mu\pm i\omega$, which cross the imaginary axis at $\mu=0$. For the subcritical form
$\dot r=\mu r+r^3-r^5$, nonzero cycles satisfy $r^4-r^2-\mu=0$, so
$$
r^2=\tfrac12\left(1\pm\sqrt{1+4\mu}\right).
$$
The two roots (the large stable cycle and the small unstable one) merge and vanish when $1+4\mu=0$, at
$\mu=-\tfrac14$. This is the end of the hysteresis loop.

**Sel'kov's fixed point.** Setting $\dot y=0$ and $\dot x=0$ gives $x^\*=b$, $y^\*=b/(a+b^2)$. The
Jacobian there has $\Delta=a+b^2>0$ and
$$
\tau=-\frac{b^4+(2a-1)b^2+(a+a^2)}{a+b^2}.
$$
At $a=0.08$, $b=0.6$, $\tau=0.0864/0.44\approx0.196>0$: an unstable spiral or node, so a trapping
region with the fixed point removed contains a closed orbit. The boundary $\tau=0$ is
$b^2=\tfrac12\left(1-2a\pm\sqrt{1-8a}\right)$ [@strogatz_nonlinear].

:::
