Network Robustness & Cascading Failures

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source

How much of a network can fail before it falls apart? Usually much more than you would guess, if failures are random, and much less, if someone aims at the hubs. And when failures stop being independent, one broken part can take down the rest.

A network does not wear out gradually; it holds together until a threshold and then shatters. Where that threshold sits depends on just two numbers from the degree distribution. For networks with hubs it sits almost at “remove everything” under random failure, and very low under attack on the hubs. Once a failing node pushes its load onto its neighbours, failures stop being independent, and a local fault can become a blackout.

Why it matters

Social scientists study systems that keep working while parts of them fail: an economy through bank failures, an organisation through staff turnover, a supply chain through a flooded factory, a society through war or famine. Barabási puts the social sciences on his list of fields that need to understand robustness, next to biology, ecology and engineering, and makes the common point that in all of them the robustness is carried by a network (Barabási 2016). “Whenever nature seeks robustness,” he writes, “it resorts to networks.”

The questions are concrete. What fraction of Internet routers must fail before the Internet splits into islands that cannot talk to each other? Would closing a few airports stop air travel? Why does a sagging power line in Oregon black out eleven US states and two Canadian provinces? The answers come from one idea, percolation, plus one complication, dependence between failures (Barabási 2016).

Breaking a network is a phase transition

Remove one node from a network and usually little happens. Remove more and small clusters start to break off. Barabási’s small example loses three nodes and ends up in five separate pieces of sizes 2, 2, 2, 5 and 6 (Barabási 2016). The question is how many nodes must go before the giant component, the connected core that holds most nodes, disappears.

Percolation theory answers it in reverse. Place pebbles (or trees) on a grid, each site filled with probability p. For small p there are only small clusters; at a critical p_c a cluster suddenly spans the whole grid. On a square grid p_c \approx 0.593 (Barabási 2016). It is the same transition as the forest fire in cellular automata: below the threshold a fire burns one small grove; above it, it crosses the forest. Removing a fraction f of nodes is the same process run backwards, with f = 1-p. Up to a critical f_c a giant component survives; past it, the network is dust (Barabási 2016). The breakdown is not gradual. It is a phase transition, the same kind as the giant component appearing in a random graph.

The hand-holding rule

To form a human chain, everyone in the middle has to hold two other hands. Barabási uses this as the intuition for the Molloy–Reed criterion (the book spells it “Malloy–Reed”): a giant component can only exist if a node inside it has, on average, at least two neighbours, one to arrive through and one to leave by (Barabási 2016). Written with the first two moments of the degree distribution, the condition is

\kappa \;=\; \frac{\langle k^2\rangle}{\langle k\rangle} \;>\; 2 .

In plain words: \langle k^2\rangle/\langle k\rangle is the average degree of a node you reach by following a link — the same quantity that drives the friendship paradox. If the node at the end of a link usually has at least one other link to carry on through, the network hangs together.

For a random network \langle k^2\rangle = \langle k\rangle(1+\langle k\rangle), so the rule reduces to \langle k\rangle > 1, which is exactly the random-graph condition for a giant component (Barabási 2016).

How much damage before collapse

Apply the rule after removing a random fraction f of nodes and you get the breakdown threshold, due to Cohen, Havlin and colleagues (Barabási 2016):

f_c \;=\; 1 - \frac{1}{\langle k^2\rangle/\langle k\rangle - 1}.

In plain words: the more the degrees vary, the larger \langle k^2\rangle is compared with \langle k\rangle, and the more of the network you can lose before it breaks.

Three consequences follow (Barabási 2016):

  • Random networks always break. With \langle k^2\rangle = \langle k\rangle(\langle k\rangle+1) the threshold is f_c = 1 - 1/\langle k\rangle. Denser networks last longer, but there is always a finite breaking point. With \langle k\rangle = 4, removing three-quarters of the nodes destroys it.
  • Scale-free networks with \gamma < 3 essentially never break by chance. Their \langle k^2\rangle diverges as the network grows, so f_c \to 1: you would have to remove nearly every node. The reason is numbers. Random failure is blind to degree, and almost all nodes are small, so it almost always removes a node nobody relied on. Close a random airport and you have probably closed a small regional one; you can still fly from New York to Tokyo.
  • Real networks are finite, and still extremely robust. The router-level Internet has \langle k^2\rangle/\langle k\rangle = 37.94, giving f_c = 0.972. About 220,000 of its 228,263 routers would have to fail at random before it fragmented, a coincidence with probability effectively zero.

Barabási calls a network enhanced-robust if its f_c beats the random-network value. That does not require a strict power law. Any network whose \langle k^2\rangle is larger than a random network’s qualifies, which covers almost all the real networks in his reference set (Barabási 2016).

The Achilles’ heel: attacks on the hubs

The same hubs that make random failure harmless make deliberate attack devastating. Remove nodes in decreasing order of degree, the biggest hub first, and the critical point that had vanished under random failure comes back, at a very low value (Barabási 2016). An attack needs knowledge of the network and the intent to cripple it, which is why it is modelled separately from failure. Barabási’s airport version: close Atlanta, Chicago, Denver and New York at once and US air travel stops within hours.

His analysis gives these results (Barabási 2016):

  • The attack threshold is always lower than the failure threshold, even for a random network, though there the gap is modest because there are no real hubs.
  • For scale-free networks it is small, and it does not change steadily with \gamma the way the failure threshold does. Solving Barabási’s attack equation numerically (our calculation), a network with minimum degree 2 never survives the loss of more than about 21% of its top hubs. That maximum is near \gamma = 3, and the threshold falls towards zero as \gamma approaches 2, where a few hubs carry almost everything.
  • As \gamma grows, the hubs disappear and attack and failure become the same thing, both thresholds tending to 1 - 1/(k_{\min}-1). That is 0 for minimum degree 2 and one half for minimum degree 3, the limits Barabási marks on his figure.

Barabási states the result as a law: “Scale-free networks are robust to random failures and fragile to attacks.” (Barabási 2016) The fragility is bad news for the Internet and good news for medicine, since a bacterium’s hub proteins are targets for drugs. There is also a history. In 1959 Paul Baran at RAND, designing a communication system to survive a nuclear strike, rejected both the star and the “stars connected in the form of a larger star” architecture as too centralised, and recommended a distributed mesh. The military ignored him, and the Internet grew instead into the hub-dominated, decentralised shape he had warned against (Barabási 2016).

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(8)
N, m = 10000, 2

# Barabási–Albert growth: sampling a random link end picks a node in proportion to its degree
edges, ends = [(0, 1)], [0, 1]
for new in range(2, N):
    chosen = set()
    while len(chosen) < min(m, new):
        chosen.add(ends[rng.integers(len(ends))])
    for t in chosen:
        edges.append((new, t)); ends += [new, t]
E_ba = np.array(edges)

# Erdős–Rényi with the same number of links (so the same mean degree)
pairs = set()
while len(pairs) < len(E_ba):
    a, b = rng.integers(N, size=2)
    if a != b:
        pairs.add((min(a, b), max(a, b)))
E_er = np.array(sorted(pairs))

def giant_after_removal(E, order):
    # put nodes back in reverse removal order; union-find tracks the largest component
    adj = [[] for _ in range(N)]
    for a, b in E:
        adj[a].append(b); adj[b].append(a)
    parent, size = np.arange(N), np.ones(N, int)
    present = np.zeros(N, bool)
    def find(x):
        while parent[x] != x:
            parent[x] = parent[parent[x]]; x = parent[x]
        return x
    best, giant = 0, np.zeros(N + 1)
    for i, v in enumerate(order[::-1]):
        present[v] = True
        for u in adj[v]:
            if present[u]:
                ru, rv = find(u), find(v)
                if ru != rv:
                    if size[ru] < size[rv]:
                        ru, rv = rv, ru
                    parent[rv] = ru; size[ru] += size[rv]
        best = max(best, size[find(v)])
        giant[i + 1] = best                     # giant[n] = largest component with n nodes present
    return giant[N - np.arange(N + 1)] / giant[N]   # indexed by number removed

f = np.arange(N + 1) / N
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(8.6, 4.6))
styles = {("ba", "fail"): ("#161613", "-", "hub network, random failure"),
          ("ba", "att"):  ("#e8531d", "-", "hub network, attack on hubs"),
          ("er", "fail"): ("#5e5e54", "--", "random network, random failure"),
          ("er", "att"):  ("#b7b6a8", "--", "random network, attack")}
for name, E in [("ba", E_ba), ("er", E_er)]:
    deg = np.bincount(E.ravel(), minlength=N)
    kappa = (deg**2).mean() / deg.mean()
    fc_pred = 1 - 1 / (kappa - 1)
    curves = {"fail": giant_after_removal(E, rng.permutation(N)),
              "att": giant_after_removal(E, np.argsort(-deg, kind="stable"))}
    for mode, P in curves.items():
        col, ls, lab = styles[(name, mode)]
        ax.plot(f, P, color=col, ls=ls, lw=2.0 if col == "#e8531d" else 1.5, label=lab)
    col = styles[(name, "fail")][0]
    ax.axvline(fc_pred, color=col, ls=":", lw=1.0)
    ax.text(fc_pred - 0.01, 0.93 if name == "ba" else 0.83,
            f"predicted $f_c$ = {fc_pred:.2f}\n($\\kappa$ = {kappa:.1f})", color=col,
            fontsize=8.3, ha="right")
    if name == "ba":
        f_att = f[np.argmax(curves["att"] < 0.01)]
        ax.annotate(f"gone after removing\nthe top {f_att:.0%} of hubs", xy=(f_att, 0.01),
                    xytext=(0.22, 0.42), color="#e8531d", fontsize=9,
                    arrowprops=dict(arrowstyle="->", color="#e8531d", lw=1))
ax.set_xlim(0, 1); ax.set_ylim(0, 1.03)
ax.set_xlabel("fraction of nodes removed  f")
ax.set_ylabel("giant component (share of starting size)")
ax.set_title("Robust to failure, fragile to attack")
ax.legend(fontsize=8.5, frameon=False, loc="upper right", bbox_to_anchor=(1.0, 0.72))
fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()

Read the figure for two lessons. The orange curve is the Achilles’ heel: the hub network loses its giant component after only about 18% of nodes, its biggest hubs, are removed, while the random network survives an attack until nearly 40% are gone. That 18% sits close to the attack equation’s value of about 21% for a \gamma = 3 network with minimum degree 2, the kind preferential attachment grows. The ink curve shows the robustness, with a long tail to the right. Both predicted thresholds land a little away from the simulated ones. That is expected: the formula is exact only for very large networks without degree correlations, and a 10,000-node grown network is neither. Notice also that the two failure curves are close for moderate f. “Robust” here means the network does not disintegrate. It does not mean it loses fewer nodes from its core.

Cascading failures: when failures stop being independent

Everything so far assumed nodes fail independently. In real systems they rarely do: a failed node passes its load to its neighbours, which may then fail too (Barabási 2016). On 10 August 1996, a hot day in Oregon, a line carrying 1,300 megawatts sagged into a tree and snapped. Its current moved to two lower-voltage lines that could not carry it, they failed, and within seconds thirteen generators went down, blacking out eleven US states and two Canadian provinces. In the 2003 Northeast blackout, a software bug in an alarm system in Ohio meant operators missed an overloaded line; more than 508 generating units at 265 plants shut down, leaving about 10 million people in Ontario and 45 million in eight US states without power (Barabási 2016). Barabási counts the 2008 financial crisis, failed routers and the 2011 Thai floods (more than 1,000 car factories disrupted, $20 billion in insurance claims) as the same phenomenon (Barabási 2016).

Sizes follow a power law. The distribution of cascade sizes is heavy-tailed, p(s) \sim s^{-\alpha}: most cascades are tiny and a few are enormous (Barabási 2016). Blackouts measured by energy not supplied, retweet cascades on Twitter (74 million sharing events in 2009, \alpha \approx 1.75, average size only 1.14) and earthquakes (\alpha \approx 1.67) all have exponents between about 1.6 and 2. That similarity, across engineers, media researchers and seismologists, is what calls for an explanation (Barabási 2016). See power laws and scaling for why a heavy tail makes “the average blackout” a poor guide.

Three ingredients. The cascade models Barabási surveys share three features (Barabási 2016): (i) some flow over a network (current, data, money); (ii) a local rule for when a component fails or passes something on; (iii) a way of redistributing the flow when a component fails. The simplest, the failure-propagation model of Watts, puts a threshold on each node: a node fails once at least a fraction \varphi of its neighbours have failed. That is the complex contagion rule and the threshold cascade of collective behaviour, now read as breakdown (Barabási 2016). It has three regimes:

  • Dense network (subcritical). Each node has many healthy neighbours, so one failure rarely tips anyone. Cascades stay small, with an exponential size distribution.
  • Very sparse network (supercritical). One failure tips several neighbours and almost any spark becomes global.
  • In between (critical). Cascades of every size, following a power law with \alpha = 3/2 on a random network.

Why 3/2? The cleanest explanation is the branching model. Treat the cascade as a family tree: each failed node “gives birth” to k new failures, drawn from some distribution. If the average number of offspring is below 1, every tree dies out quickly; above 1, trees can grow without limit; at exactly 1 the process is critical (Barabási 2016). At the critical point the number of active failures behaves like a random walk that started at 1, and the cascade ends when the walk first hits 0. First-return times of a random walk follow a power law with exponent 3/2 (Barabási 2016). If the offspring distribution is itself scale-free with 2<\gamma<3, the exponent becomes \gamma/(\gamma-1), between 3/2 and 2, which covers the observed range. The two other models in the chapter, an overload model of power grids and a network sandpile, give the same 3/2 on random networks. Barabási reads this agreement across different mechanisms as a sign that the exponent is universal (Barabási 2016). The probability machinery is in the branching-processes appendix.

Building robustness

Designing for both threats. A hub-and-spoke network survives random failure (only the centre matters, and the chance that a random failure hits it is 1/(N-1)) but dies with its hub. Connecting the spokes to each other protects it, at a cost: in Barabási’s seven-node example the average degree, his proxy for cost, doubles from 12/7 to 24/7 (Barabási 2016). Holding cost fixed, the topology that maximises robustness to failure plus attack is bimodal: one hub of degree about A N^{2/3} and every other node at the same minimum degree. If that minimum is high enough, the small nodes form a giant component of their own, so even losing the hub does not break the network (Barabási 2016).

That result answers a tempting story. Perhaps real networks are scale-free because robustness was selected for? No: the robustness-optimal network is a single hub with identical spokes, not a power law. Networks are scale-free because of growth and preferential attachment, and their robustness is a by-product. It is not the most robust design available (Barabási 2016).

Stopping a cascade once it starts. Adding links is too slow. A new power line can take up to two decades to approve and build; a cascade crosses a grid in seconds. Simulations suggest a counter-intuitive fix: remove carefully chosen nodes and links right after the first failure, such as low-load nodes and links carrying large excess load near the fault, as firefighters burn a firebreak ahead of a wildfire (Barabási 2016). Biology has a version, the Lazarus effect: an E. coli mutant that cannot grow because it lacks an essential gene can be revived by knocking out five more (Barabási 2016).

A case study with outcomes. The European power grid (more than twenty national grids, over 3,000 generators and substations, 200,000 km of lines) has an exponential degree distribution described by a single number, \langle k\rangle. Using it to predict each national grid’s attack threshold splits the 33 grids in two. Where \langle k\rangle > 1.5 the prediction matches the observed threshold; the sparser grids, \langle k\rangle < 1.5, are more robust than predicted (Barabási 2016). The difference shows up in reliability records. The first group, two-thirds of the network, had more than five times the average interruption time, more than twice the power losses and almost four times the undelivered energy (Barabási 2016). Sparser grids were the more reliable ones, which is not what intuition expects.

Three words that are not synonyms. Barabási separates them (Barabási 2016). Robustness is keeping basic functions while parts are missing. Resilience is adapting how the system operates in order to keep functioning, a dynamic property. Redundancy is having parallel components or paths that can stand in for a lost one, like the many short acquaintance chains between you and a head of state. An organisation can be robust without being resilient, and redundancy is one way, not the only way, to buy robustness.

Common pitfalls

WarningWhere this goes wrong
  • Calling a system “robust” without saying robust to what. The same network can be nearly unbreakable under random failure and broken by removing 18% of its nodes in the right order. Name the threat. (Barabási 2016)
  • Assuming damage accumulates smoothly. Breakdown is a phase transition. A system can look fine at 60% damage and be gone at 75%, with little warning in between. (Barabási 2016)
  • Treating failures as independent when they share load. The percolation thresholds assume independent failures. When a failed component passes its load on, the right model is a cascade, and its size distribution has a heavy tail. (Barabási 2016)
  • Planning for the average cascade. With p(s)\sim s^{-\alpha} and \alpha around 1.5–2, the average blackout or retweet chain is dominated by rare giants (the Twitter mean of 1.14 hides cascades of thousands). Plan for the tail. (Barabási 2016)
  • Reading robustness as the reason a structure exists. Scale-free networks are robust, but the most robust design is bimodal. Robustness is a consequence of how they grew, not the cause. (Barabási 2016)
  • Assuming more links always help. Denser grids were less reliable in the European data, and extra links can give a cascade more routes to spread. (Barabási 2016)
  • Trusting f_c formulas on small or correlated networks. The formula assumes a large network with no degree correlations; the figure’s 10,000-node grown network already misses it by about 0.05. Degree correlations shift it further.

Appendix · the mathematics

The Molloy–Reed criterion (sketch). Take a node i in the giant component, reached from a neighbour j. For i to be part of a connected structure rather than a dead end, it must on average have at least one link besides the one to j, so \langle k_i \mid i \leftrightarrow j\rangle \ge 2. Without degree correlations the degree of a node reached by a link follows q_k = k p_k/\langle k\rangle (see the friendship-paradox appendix), so

\langle k_i \mid i\leftrightarrow j\rangle=\sum_k k\,q_k=\frac{\langle k^2\rangle}{\langle k\rangle}\ge 2 .

The breakdown threshold. Remove each node independently with probability f. A node of original degree k keeps each of its links with probability 1-f, so its new degree is binomial. The new moments are

\langle k'\rangle=(1-f)\langle k\rangle,\qquad \langle k'^2\rangle=(1-f)^2\langle k^2\rangle+f(1-f)\langle k\rangle .

Setting \kappa' = \langle k'^2\rangle/\langle k'\rangle = (1-f)\kappa + f equal to 2 and solving gives

f_c=\frac{\kappa-2}{\kappa-1}=1-\frac{1}{\kappa-1},\qquad \kappa=\frac{\langle k^2\rangle}{\langle k\rangle}.

For a Poisson degree distribution \kappa = \langle k\rangle+1, so f_c = 1-1/\langle k\rangle. For a power law with 2<\gamma<3, \langle k^2\rangle \sim k_{\max}^{3-\gamma} grows with network size, since k_{\max} \sim k_{\min}N^{1/(\gamma-1)}, so \kappa\to\infty and f_c\to1. Barabási’s finite-size version is f_c \approx 1 - C/N^{(3-\gamma)/(\gamma-1)} (Barabási 2016).

Why the critical cascade exponent is 3/2. In the branching model with p_0=p_2=\tfrac12 (each active failure either dies or splits in two), the number of active failures x moves by -1 or +1 with equal probability: a symmetric random walk started at x=1. The cascade ends at the walk’s first visit to 0, so the cascade size is a first-passage time. For a symmetric walk the probability that the first passage takes exactly t steps falls off like t^{-3/2}, hence p(s)\sim s^{-3/2} (Barabási 2016). The branching-processes appendix covers the extinction side of the same process: the tree dies out for sure when the mean offspring is at most 1.

*A network does not wear out gradually; it holds together until a threshold and then shatters.* Where that
threshold sits depends on just two numbers from the degree distribution. For networks with hubs it sits
almost at "remove everything" under random failure, and very low under attack on the hubs. Once a failing
node pushes its load onto its neighbours, failures stop being independent, and a local fault can become a

## Why it matters

Social scientists study systems that keep working while parts of them fail: an economy through bank
failures, an organisation through staff turnover, a supply chain through a flooded factory, a society
through war or famine. Barabási puts the social sciences on his list of fields that need to understand
robustness, next to biology, ecology and engineering, and makes the common point that in all of them the
robustness is carried by a **network** [@barabasi_network]. "Whenever nature seeks robustness," he writes,
"it resorts to networks."

The questions are concrete. What fraction of Internet routers must fail before the Internet splits into
islands that cannot talk to each other? Would closing a few airports stop air travel? Why does a sagging
power line in Oregon black out eleven US states and two Canadian provinces? The answers come from one idea,
**percolation**, plus one complication, **dependence between failures** [@barabasi_network].

## Breaking a network is a phase transition

Remove one node from a network and usually little happens. Remove more and small clusters start to break
off. Barabási's small example loses three nodes and ends up in five separate pieces of sizes 2, 2, 2, 5 and 6
[@barabasi_network]. The question is how many nodes must go before the **giant component**, the connected
core that holds most nodes, disappears.

Percolation theory answers it in reverse. Place pebbles (or trees) on a grid, each site filled with
probability $p$. For small $p$ there are only small clusters; at a critical $p_c$ a cluster suddenly spans
the whole grid. On a square grid $p_c \approx 0.593$ [@barabasi_network]. It is the same transition as the
forest fire in [cellular automata](cellular-automata.qmd): below the threshold a fire burns one small
grove; above it, it crosses the forest. **Removing** a fraction $f$ of nodes is the same process run
backwards, with $f = 1-p$. Up to a critical $f_c$ a giant component survives; past it, the network is
dust [@barabasi_network]. The breakdown is **not gradual**. It is a phase transition, the same kind as the
[giant component appearing in a random graph](random-graphs-erdos-renyi.qmd).

## The hand-holding rule

To form a human chain, everyone in the middle has to hold two other hands. Barabási uses this as the
intuition for the **Molloy–Reed criterion** (the book spells it "Malloy–Reed"): a giant component can only
exist if a node inside it has, on average, at least two neighbours, one to arrive through and one to leave by
[@barabasi_network]. Written with the first two moments of the degree distribution, the condition is

$$
\kappa \;=\; \frac{\langle k^2\rangle}{\langle k\rangle} \;>\; 2 .
$$

> **In plain words:** $\langle k^2\rangle/\langle k\rangle$ is the average degree of a node you reach by
> following a link — the same quantity that drives the [friendship
> paradox](degree-correlations-and-the-friendship-paradox.qmd). If the node at the end of a link usually has
> at least one *other* link to carry on through, the network hangs together.

For a random network $\langle k^2\rangle = \langle k\rangle(1+\langle k\rangle)$, so the rule reduces to
$\langle k\rangle > 1$, which is exactly the random-graph condition for a giant component
[@barabasi_network].

## How much damage before collapse

Apply the rule *after* removing a random fraction $f$ of nodes and you get the breakdown threshold, due to
Cohen, Havlin and colleagues [@barabasi_network]:

$$
f_c \;=\; 1 - \frac{1}{\langle k^2\rangle/\langle k\rangle - 1}.
$$

> **In plain words:** the more the degrees vary, the larger $\langle k^2\rangle$ is compared with
> $\langle k\rangle$, and the more of the network you can lose before it breaks.

Three consequences follow [@barabasi_network]:

- **Random networks always break.** With $\langle k^2\rangle = \langle k\rangle(\langle k\rangle+1)$ the
  threshold is $f_c = 1 - 1/\langle k\rangle$. Denser networks last longer, but there is always a finite
  breaking point. With $\langle k\rangle = 4$, removing three-quarters of the nodes destroys it.
- **Scale-free networks with $\gamma < 3$ essentially never break by chance.** Their $\langle k^2\rangle$
  diverges as the network grows, so $f_c \to 1$: you would have to remove nearly every node. The reason is
  numbers. Random failure is blind to degree, and almost all nodes are small, so it almost always removes
  a node nobody relied on. Close a random airport and you have probably closed a small regional one; you
  can still fly from New York to Tokyo.
- **Real networks are finite, and still extremely robust.** The router-level Internet has
  $\langle k^2\rangle/\langle k\rangle = 37.94$, giving $f_c = 0.972$. About 220,000 of its 228,263 routers
  would have to fail at random before it fragmented, a coincidence with probability effectively zero.

Barabási calls a network **enhanced-robust** if its $f_c$ beats the random-network value. That does **not**
require a strict power law. Any network whose $\langle k^2\rangle$ is larger than a random network's
qualifies, which covers almost all the real networks in his reference set [@barabasi_network].

## The Achilles' heel: attacks on the hubs

The same hubs that make random failure harmless make deliberate attack devastating. Remove nodes in
decreasing order of degree, the biggest hub first, and the critical point that had vanished under random
failure comes back, at a **very low** value [@barabasi_network]. An attack needs knowledge of the network
and the intent to cripple it, which is why it is modelled separately from failure. Barabási's airport
version: close Atlanta, Chicago, Denver and New York at once and US air travel stops within hours.

His analysis gives these results [@barabasi_network]:

- The attack threshold is **always lower** than the failure threshold, even for a random network, though
  there the gap is modest because there are no real hubs.
- For scale-free networks it is small, and it does not change steadily with $\gamma$ the way the failure
  threshold does. Solving Barabási's attack equation numerically (our calculation), a network with minimum
  degree 2 never survives the loss of more than about 21% of its top hubs. That maximum is near
  $\gamma = 3$, and the threshold falls towards zero as $\gamma$ approaches 2, where a few hubs carry almost
  everything.
- As $\gamma$ grows, the hubs disappear and attack and failure become the same thing, both thresholds tending
  to $1 - 1/(k_{\min}-1)$. That is 0 for minimum degree 2 and one half for minimum degree 3, the limits
  Barabási marks on his figure.

Barabási states the result as a law: **"Scale-free networks are robust to random failures and fragile to
attacks."** [@barabasi_network] The fragility is bad news for the Internet and good news for medicine,
since a bacterium's hub proteins are targets for drugs. There is also a history. In 1959 Paul Baran at RAND,
designing a communication system to survive a nuclear strike, rejected both the star and the
"stars connected in the form of a larger star" architecture as too centralised, and recommended a
distributed mesh. The military ignored him, and the Internet grew instead into the hub-dominated,
decentralised shape he had warned against [@barabasi_network].

```{python}
#| label: fig-robustness
#| fig-cap: "Failure versus attack, simulated by hand on two 10,000-node networks with the same mean degree ⟨k⟩ = 4: one grown by preferential attachment (Barabási–Albert, two links per new node, so it has hubs) and one random (Erdős–Rényi). Each curve shows the giant component, as a share of its starting size, after a fraction f of nodes has been removed, either at random (failure) or biggest hub first (attack). The dotted verticals mark the Molloy–Reed prediction for random failure, f_c = 1 − 1/(κ − 1). Under random failure the hub network outlasts the random one (it still has a giant component past f = 0.85, the random one is gone soon after 0.75). Under attack it is the more fragile network by far: removing its top fifth of hubs destroys it."
#| code-fold: true
#| code-summary: "Show the figure code (network growth + union-find, by hand)"

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(8)
N, m = 10000, 2

# Barabási–Albert growth: sampling a random link end picks a node in proportion to its degree
edges, ends = [(0, 1)], [0, 1]
for new in range(2, N):
    chosen = set()
    while len(chosen) < min(m, new):
        chosen.add(ends[rng.integers(len(ends))])
    for t in chosen:
        edges.append((new, t)); ends += [new, t]
E_ba = np.array(edges)

# Erdős–Rényi with the same number of links (so the same mean degree)
pairs = set()
while len(pairs) < len(E_ba):
    a, b = rng.integers(N, size=2)
    if a != b:
        pairs.add((min(a, b), max(a, b)))
E_er = np.array(sorted(pairs))

def giant_after_removal(E, order):
    # put nodes back in reverse removal order; union-find tracks the largest component
    adj = [[] for _ in range(N)]
    for a, b in E:
        adj[a].append(b); adj[b].append(a)
    parent, size = np.arange(N), np.ones(N, int)
    present = np.zeros(N, bool)
    def find(x):
        while parent[x] != x:
            parent[x] = parent[parent[x]]; x = parent[x]
        return x
    best, giant = 0, np.zeros(N + 1)
    for i, v in enumerate(order[::-1]):
        present[v] = True
        for u in adj[v]:
            if present[u]:
                ru, rv = find(u), find(v)
                if ru != rv:
                    if size[ru] < size[rv]:
                        ru, rv = rv, ru
                    parent[rv] = ru; size[ru] += size[rv]
        best = max(best, size[find(v)])
        giant[i + 1] = best                     # giant[n] = largest component with n nodes present
    return giant[N - np.arange(N + 1)] / giant[N]   # indexed by number removed

f = np.arange(N + 1) / N
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(8.6, 4.6))
styles = {("ba", "fail"): ("#161613", "-", "hub network, random failure"),
          ("ba", "att"):  ("#e8531d", "-", "hub network, attack on hubs"),
          ("er", "fail"): ("#5e5e54", "--", "random network, random failure"),
          ("er", "att"):  ("#b7b6a8", "--", "random network, attack")}
for name, E in [("ba", E_ba), ("er", E_er)]:
    deg = np.bincount(E.ravel(), minlength=N)
    kappa = (deg**2).mean() / deg.mean()
    fc_pred = 1 - 1 / (kappa - 1)
    curves = {"fail": giant_after_removal(E, rng.permutation(N)),
              "att": giant_after_removal(E, np.argsort(-deg, kind="stable"))}
    for mode, P in curves.items():
        col, ls, lab = styles[(name, mode)]
        ax.plot(f, P, color=col, ls=ls, lw=2.0 if col == "#e8531d" else 1.5, label=lab)
    col = styles[(name, "fail")][0]
    ax.axvline(fc_pred, color=col, ls=":", lw=1.0)
    ax.text(fc_pred - 0.01, 0.93 if name == "ba" else 0.83,
            f"predicted $f_c$ = {fc_pred:.2f}\n($\\kappa$ = {kappa:.1f})", color=col,
            fontsize=8.3, ha="right")
    if name == "ba":
        f_att = f[np.argmax(curves["att"] < 0.01)]
        ax.annotate(f"gone after removing\nthe top {f_att:.0%} of hubs", xy=(f_att, 0.01),
                    xytext=(0.22, 0.42), color="#e8531d", fontsize=9,
                    arrowprops=dict(arrowstyle="->", color="#e8531d", lw=1))
ax.set_xlim(0, 1); ax.set_ylim(0, 1.03)
ax.set_xlabel("fraction of nodes removed  f")
ax.set_ylabel("giant component (share of starting size)")
ax.set_title("Robust to failure, fragile to attack")
ax.legend(fontsize=8.5, frameon=False, loc="upper right", bbox_to_anchor=(1.0, 0.72))
fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

Read the figure for two lessons. The **orange** curve is the Achilles' heel: the hub network loses its
giant component after only about 18% of nodes, its biggest hubs, are removed, while the random network
survives an attack until nearly 40% are gone. That 18% sits close to the attack equation's value of about
21% for a $\gamma = 3$ network with minimum degree 2, the kind preferential attachment grows. The **ink** curve shows the robustness, with a long tail to
the right. Both predicted thresholds land a little away from the simulated ones. That is expected: the
formula is exact only for very large networks without degree correlations, and a 10,000-node grown network
is neither. Notice also that the two failure curves are close for moderate $f$. "Robust" here means the
network does not **disintegrate**. It does not mean it loses fewer nodes from its core.

## Cascading failures: when failures stop being independent

Everything so far assumed nodes fail independently. In real systems they rarely do: a failed node passes
its load to its neighbours, which may then fail too [@barabasi_network]. On 10 August 1996, a hot day in
Oregon, a line carrying 1,300 megawatts sagged into a tree and snapped. Its current moved to two
lower-voltage lines that could not carry it, they failed, and within seconds thirteen generators went down,
blacking out eleven US states and two Canadian provinces. In the 2003 Northeast blackout, a software bug in
an alarm system in Ohio meant operators missed an overloaded line; more than 508 generating units at 265
plants shut down, leaving about 10 million people in Ontario and 45 million in eight US states without
power [@barabasi_network]. Barabási counts the 2008 financial crisis, failed routers and the 2011 Thai
floods (more than 1,000 car factories disrupted, \$20 billion in insurance claims) as the same phenomenon
[@barabasi_network].

**Sizes follow a power law.** The distribution of cascade sizes is heavy-tailed, $p(s) \sim s^{-\alpha}$:
most cascades are tiny and a few are enormous [@barabasi_network]. Blackouts measured by energy not
supplied, retweet cascades on Twitter (74 million sharing events in 2009, $\alpha \approx 1.75$, average size
only 1.14) and earthquakes ($\alpha \approx 1.67$) all have exponents between about 1.6 and 2. That
similarity, across engineers, media researchers and seismologists, is what calls for an explanation
[@barabasi_network]. See [power laws and scaling](power-laws-and-scaling.qmd) for why a heavy tail makes
"the average blackout" a poor guide.

**Three ingredients.** The cascade models Barabási surveys share three features [@barabasi_network]: (i) some
**flow** over a network (current, data, money); (ii) a **local rule** for when a component fails or passes
something on; (iii) a way of **redistributing** the flow when a component fails. The simplest, the
**failure-propagation model** of Watts, puts a threshold on each node: a node fails once at least a fraction
$\varphi$ of its neighbours have failed. That is the [complex contagion](spreading-on-networks.qmd) rule
and the [threshold cascade](complex-adaptive-systems.qmd) of collective behaviour, now read as
breakdown [@barabasi_network]. It has three regimes:

- **Dense network (subcritical).** Each node has many healthy neighbours, so one failure rarely tips anyone.
  Cascades stay small, with an exponential size distribution.
- **Very sparse network (supercritical).** One failure tips several neighbours and almost any spark becomes
  global.
- **In between (critical).** Cascades of every size, following a power law with $\alpha = 3/2$ on a random
  network.

**Why 3/2?** The cleanest explanation is the **branching model**. Treat the cascade as a family tree: each
failed node "gives birth" to $k$ new failures, drawn from some distribution. If the average number of
offspring is below 1, every tree dies out quickly; above 1, trees can grow without limit; at exactly 1
the process is critical [@barabasi_network]. At the critical point the number of active failures behaves
like a random walk that started at 1, and the cascade ends when the walk first hits 0. First-return times of
a random walk follow a power law with exponent 3/2 [@barabasi_network]. If the offspring distribution is
itself scale-free with $2<\gamma<3$, the exponent becomes $\gamma/(\gamma-1)$, between 3/2 and 2, which
covers the observed range. The two other models in the chapter, an overload model of power grids and a
network [sandpile](self-organised-criticality.qmd), give the same 3/2 on random networks. Barabási reads
this agreement across different mechanisms as a sign that the exponent is **universal**
[@barabasi_network]. The probability machinery is in the [branching-processes
appendix](../../appendices/advanced-probability/branching-processes.qmd).

## Building robustness

**Designing for both threats.** A hub-and-spoke network survives random failure (only the centre matters,
and the chance that a random failure hits it is $1/(N-1)$) but dies with its hub. Connecting the spokes to
each other protects it, at a cost: in Barabási's seven-node example the average degree, his proxy for
cost, doubles from 12/7 to 24/7 [@barabasi_network]. Holding cost fixed, the topology that maximises
robustness to failure *plus* attack is **bimodal**: one hub of degree about $A N^{2/3}$ and every other
node at the same minimum degree. If that minimum is high enough, the small nodes form a giant component of
their own, so even losing the hub does not break the network [@barabasi_network].

That result answers a tempting story. Perhaps real networks are scale-free *because* robustness was
selected for? No: the robustness-optimal network is a single hub with identical spokes, not a power law.
Networks are scale-free because of growth and [preferential attachment](scale-free-networks.qmd), and
their robustness is a by-product. It is not the most robust design available [@barabasi_network].

**Stopping a cascade once it starts.** Adding links is too slow. A new power line can take up to two
decades to approve and build; a cascade crosses a grid in seconds. Simulations suggest a counter-intuitive
fix: **remove** carefully chosen nodes and links right after the first failure, such as low-load nodes and
links carrying large excess load near the fault, as firefighters burn a firebreak ahead of a wildfire
[@barabasi_network]. Biology has a version, the **Lazarus effect**: an *E. coli* mutant that cannot grow
because it lacks an essential gene can be revived by knocking out five more [@barabasi_network].

**A case study with outcomes.** The European power grid (more than twenty national grids, over 3,000
generators and substations, 200,000 km of lines) has an exponential degree distribution described by a
single number, $\langle k\rangle$. Using it to predict each national grid's attack threshold splits the 33
grids in two. Where $\langle k\rangle > 1.5$ the prediction matches the observed threshold; the sparser
grids, $\langle k\rangle < 1.5$, are **more** robust than predicted [@barabasi_network]. The difference
shows up in reliability records. The first group, two-thirds of the network, had more than five times the
average interruption time, more than twice the power losses and almost four times the undelivered energy
[@barabasi_network]. Sparser grids were the more reliable ones, which is not what intuition expects.

**Three words that are not synonyms.** Barabási separates them [@barabasi_network]. **Robustness** is
keeping basic functions while parts are missing. **Resilience** is adapting how the system operates in
order to keep functioning, a dynamic property. **Redundancy** is having parallel components or paths that
can stand in for a lost one, like the many short acquaintance chains between you and a head of state. An
organisation can be robust without being resilient, and redundancy is one way, not the only way, to buy
robustness.

## Common pitfalls

::: {.callout-warning title="Where this goes wrong"}
- **Calling a system "robust" without saying robust to what.** The same network can be nearly unbreakable
  under random failure and broken by removing 18% of its nodes in the right order. Name the threat.
  [@barabasi_network]
- **Assuming damage accumulates smoothly.** Breakdown is a phase transition. A system can look fine at
  60% damage and be gone at 75%, with little warning in between. [@barabasi_network]
- **Treating failures as independent when they share load.** The percolation thresholds assume
  independent failures. When a failed component passes its load on, the right model is a cascade, and its
  size distribution has a heavy tail. [@barabasi_network]
- **Planning for the average cascade.** With $p(s)\sim s^{-\alpha}$ and $\alpha$ around 1.5–2, the average
  blackout or retweet chain is dominated by rare giants (the Twitter mean of 1.14 hides cascades of
  thousands). Plan for the tail. [@barabasi_network]
- **Reading robustness as the reason a structure exists.** Scale-free networks are robust, but the most
  robust design is bimodal. Robustness is a consequence of how they grew, not the cause.
  [@barabasi_network]
- **Assuming more links always help.** Denser grids were *less* reliable in the European data, and extra
  links can give a cascade more routes to spread. [@barabasi_network]
- **Trusting $f_c$ formulas on small or correlated networks.** The formula assumes a large network with
  no degree correlations; the figure's 10,000-node grown network already misses it by about 0.05.
  [Degree correlations](degree-correlations-and-the-friendship-paradox.qmd) shift it further.
:::

## Appendix · the mathematics {.unnumbered}

::: {.math-appendix}

**The Molloy–Reed criterion (sketch).** Take a node $i$ in the giant component, reached from a neighbour
$j$. For $i$ to be part of a connected structure rather than a dead end, it must on average have at least
one link besides the one to $j$, so $\langle k_i \mid i \leftrightarrow j\rangle \ge 2$. Without degree
correlations the degree of a node reached by a link follows $q_k = k p_k/\langle k\rangle$ (see the
[friendship-paradox appendix](degree-correlations-and-the-friendship-paradox.qmd)), so

$$
\langle k_i \mid i\leftrightarrow j\rangle=\sum_k k\,q_k=\frac{\langle k^2\rangle}{\langle k\rangle}\ge 2 .
$$

**The breakdown threshold.** Remove each node independently with probability $f$. A node of original
degree $k$ keeps each of its links with probability $1-f$, so its new degree is binomial. The new moments are

$$
\langle k'\rangle=(1-f)\langle k\rangle,\qquad
\langle k'^2\rangle=(1-f)^2\langle k^2\rangle+f(1-f)\langle k\rangle .
$$

Setting $\kappa' = \langle k'^2\rangle/\langle k'\rangle = (1-f)\kappa + f$ equal to 2 and solving gives

$$
f_c=\frac{\kappa-2}{\kappa-1}=1-\frac{1}{\kappa-1},\qquad \kappa=\frac{\langle k^2\rangle}{\langle k\rangle}.
$$

For a Poisson degree distribution $\kappa = \langle k\rangle+1$, so $f_c = 1-1/\langle k\rangle$. For a power
law with $2<\gamma<3$, $\langle k^2\rangle \sim k_{\max}^{3-\gamma}$ grows with network size, since $k_{\max}
\sim k_{\min}N^{1/(\gamma-1)}$, so $\kappa\to\infty$ and $f_c\to1$. Barabási's finite-size version is
$f_c \approx 1 - C/N^{(3-\gamma)/(\gamma-1)}$ [@barabasi_network].

**Why the critical cascade exponent is 3/2.** In the branching model with $p_0=p_2=\tfrac12$ (each active
failure either dies or splits in two), the number of active failures $x$ moves by $-1$ or $+1$ with equal
probability: a symmetric random walk started at $x=1$. The cascade ends at the walk's first visit to 0, so
the cascade size is a first-passage time. For a symmetric walk the probability that the first passage
takes exactly $t$ steps falls off like $t^{-3/2}$, hence $p(s)\sim s^{-3/2}$ [@barabasi_network]. The
[branching-processes appendix](../../appendices/advanced-probability/branching-processes.qmd) covers the
extinction side of the same process: the tree dies out for sure when the mean offspring is at most 1.

:::
