Degree Correlations & the Friendship Paradox

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source

Knowing how many links each node has is not the same as knowing who links to whom. Whether hubs befriend hubs or shun them reshapes a network’s giant component, its epidemics, and its fragility — and it is why your friends have more friends than you do.

Your friends are, on average, more popular than you are — not because you are unusually unpopular, but because a friend is something you reach by following a link, and links lead to hubs more often than to anyone else. Push that idea one step further and ask whose friends the hubs are: in social networks hubs tend to link to other hubs (assortative), in many biological and technological networks they avoid each other (disassortative). The degree distribution alone cannot tell the two apart.

Why it matters

The network entries so far summarise a network largely by its degree distribution p_k: Poisson for a random graph, a power law for a scale-free network. That is a powerful summary, but it is silent on a question every social scientist cares about: who connects to whom? Two networks can have exactly the same list of degrees and yet wire it in completely different ways (Barabási 2016).

Barabási opens the topic with celebrity marriage. If a celebrity picked a partner at random from roughly a billion eligible people, the chance of choosing one of a generous list of 1,000 fellow celebrities would be about 10^{-6} — they would essentially never marry each other. Yet they do (Barabási 2016). Celebrities, political leaders and CEOs are hubs of the social network, and hubs, it seems, tend to have ties to other hubs. The yeast protein-interaction network does the opposite: its hubs link mostly to small proteins and avoid each other (Barabási 2016). That difference — degree correlations — changes how fast a disease spreads, how easily a network falls apart, and how biased any sample built by “asking people about their friends” will be.

The friendship paradox: following a link is biased sampling

Pick a person at random and their number of friends is, on average, the network’s mean degree \langle k \rangle. Now pick a random person and then one of their friends. That friend is not a random person. A node with k links is reached through any of its k link-ends, so the chance of landing on it is proportional to k: the degree at the end of a random link follows q_k = k\,p_k/\langle k\rangle, tilted toward hubs (Barabási 2016). “We are more likely to be friends with hubs than with small-degree nodes, simply because hubs have more friends” (Barabási 2016).

So in a network with no degree correlations, the average degree of a node’s neighbours is

k_{nn} = \frac{\langle k^2\rangle}{\langle k\rangle} \;>\; \langle k\rangle ,

which is the friendship paradox, discovered by the sociologist Scott Feld: on average your friends are more popular than you are — even though most people believe the opposite (Barabási 2016). Even in a random graph the gap is one whole friend (k_{nn} = 1 + \langle k\rangle). In scale-free networks, where \langle k^2\rangle is huge, it is enormous: \langle k^2\rangle/\langle k\rangle is about 390 in the email network and about 566 in the actor network, so “the average degree of the friends of a randomly selected node can be hundreds of times higher” than the node’s own expected degree (Barabási 2016).

For a quantitative social scientist this is first a sampling lesson. Any design that reaches people through other people — snowball samples, contact tracing, “name your friends” modules, link-tracing on social media — over-represents the well connected. Formally it is an unplanned version of probability-proportional-to-size selection from complex survey estimation, with degree as the size measure: each unit’s chance of selection depends on the very quantity you are trying to measure, and the same Horvitz–Thompson logic (weight by 1/\pi, here \propto 1/k) is what undoes it.

Assortative, neutral, disassortative

Now refine the question. Not “how popular are my friends on average?” but “does a node’s popularity predict its friends’ popularity?” Barabási draws three networks with an identical degree sequence and different wiring (Barabási 2016):

  • Neutral: links fall where chance puts them. The expected number of links between nodes of degree k and k' is proportional to k k' and nothing more.
  • Assortative: hubs link to hubs and small nodes to small nodes, more than chance predicts. The extreme is a network made of separate complete clusters, every degree-k node linked only to other degree-k nodes.
  • Disassortative: hubs avoid each other and link to small nodes, giving a hub-and-spoke look.

The terms come from the social sciences, where assortative mating describes people marrying people like themselves — college graduates marrying college graduates (Barabási 2016). Network science borrowed the word for similarity in degree; the older, attribute-based sense (liberal blogs citing liberal blogs) is the homophily of the community-detection entry. Disassortative mixing also has social examples: trade links people with different skills — “the baker does not sell bread to other bakers” (Barabási 2016).

Measuring it: k_{nn}(k) and the coefficient r

The complete information sits in the matrix e_{ij}, the probability that a randomly chosen link joins a degree-i node to a degree-j node — but a matrix is hard to read and harder to compare (Barabási 2016). Two compact summaries do the work in practice:

  • The degree correlation function k_{nn}(k): for each node, average the degrees of its neighbours; then average that over all nodes of degree k. Flat means neutral; rising means assortative; falling means disassortative (Barabási 2016). Barabási’s worked example: a node of degree 4 whose neighbours have degrees 4, 3, 3 and 1 has k_{nn} = 11/4 = 2.75.
  • The degree correlation coefficient r (Newman): simply the Pearson correlation between the degrees at the two ends of a link, between -1 and 1. Positive is assortative, zero neutral, negative disassortative (Barabási 2016). The astrophysics collaboration network gives r=0.13, the protein interaction network r=-0.04, the power grid r=0 (Barabási 2016).

Barabási also fits k_{nn}(k)\sim k^{\mu}: \mu = 0.37\pm0.11 for scientific collaboration, 0.04\pm0.05 for the power grid (indistinguishable from zero), -0.76\pm0.04 for the metabolic network (Barabási 2016). He cautions that r quietly assumes a linear k_{nn}(k) while \mu assumes a power law; both cannot hold at once, so use r with care (Barabási 2016).

Show the figure code (BA growth + degree-preserving rewiring, by hand) 
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(11)

def neighbour_degree_sum(E, d, n):
    # for every node, the sum of its neighbours' degrees
    return (np.bincount(E[:, 0], weights=d[E[:, 1]], minlength=n)
            + np.bincount(E[:, 1], weights=d[E[:, 0]], minlength=n))

# ---- Left: the friendship paradox on a grown (Barabási–Albert) network ----
N, m = 3000, 2
edges, ends = [(0, 1)], [0, 1]        # 'ends' lists every link end: sampling it is degree-proportional
for new in range(2, N):
    chosen = set()
    while len(chosen) < min(m, new):
        chosen.add(ends[rng.integers(len(ends))])
    for t in chosen:
        edges.append((new, t))
        ends += [new, t]
E = np.array(edges)
deg = np.bincount(E.ravel(), minlength=N)
friends_mean = neighbour_degree_sum(E, deg, N) / deg
kbar, k2_over_k = deg.mean(), (deg**2).mean() / deg.mean()
share = np.mean(friends_mean > deg)

# ---- Right: tune degree correlations on one Erdős–Rényi graph (Xalvi-Brunet & Sokolov) ----
n2, p_rule = 1500, 0.5
iu = np.triu_indices(n2, 1)
keep = rng.random(len(iu[0])) < 6.0 / (n2 - 1)
E0 = np.column_stack([iu[0][keep], iu[1][keep]])

def xbs(E_in, assortative, steps=12000):
    # pick two links, order their four end-nodes by degree, rewire; degrees never change
    E = E_in.copy()
    present = set(map(tuple, np.sort(E, axis=1)))
    d = np.bincount(E.ravel(), minlength=n2)
    for _ in range(steps):
        i, j = rng.choice(len(E), 2, replace=False)
        quad = [E[i, 0], E[i, 1], E[j, 0], E[j, 1]]
        if len(set(quad)) < 4:
            continue
        if rng.random() < p_rule:
            o = sorted(quad, key=lambda v: d[v])     # lowest ... highest degree
            pairs = [(o[3], o[2]), (o[1], o[0])] if assortative else [(o[3], o[0]), (o[2], o[1])]
        else:
            rng.shuffle(quad)                          # otherwise a random degree-preserving swap
            pairs = [(quad[0], quad[1]), (quad[2], quad[3])]
        pairs = [tuple(sorted(p)) for p in pairs]
        if pairs[0] in present or pairs[1] in present:
            continue                                   # keep the network simple
        present.discard(tuple(sorted(E[i]))); present.discard(tuple(sorted(E[j])))
        present.update(pairs)
        E[i], E[j] = pairs
    return E

def knn_curve(E):
    d = np.bincount(E.ravel(), minlength=n2)
    knn_i = neighbour_degree_sum(E, d, n2) / np.maximum(d, 1)
    ks = np.array([k for k in range(1, d.max() + 1) if np.sum(d == k) >= 8])
    r = np.corrcoef(np.r_[d[E[:, 0]], d[E[:, 1]]], np.r_[d[E[:, 1]], d[E[:, 0]]])[0, 1]
    return ks, np.array([knn_i[d == k].mean() for k in ks]), r

axL.set_title("The friendship paradox")

The right panel is the chapter’s central claim made visible: the three networks share the same degree of every single node, so any statistic computed from p_k alone is identical across them. Only the wiring differs — and k_{nn}(k) sees it.

What real networks do

Comparing k_{nn}(k) for ten reference networks against degree-preserving randomisations of themselves, Barabási finds (Barabási 2016):

  • Social networks are assortative. The mobile-phone call network, scientific collaboration and the actor network all show rising k_{nn}(k) that their randomised versions do not reproduce — the assortativity is real, not an artefact of the degree distribution.
  • The power grid is the only truly neutral network in the set.
  • Email, protein-interaction and metabolic networks are disassortative, but their randomised versions are too: the disassortativity is structural, a by-product of being scale-free (below).

Why would social networks be assortative? Barabási lists candidate mechanisms rather than a settled answer: the tendency to form communities, society’s many devices for bringing hubs together (“from professional committees to TV shows”), and homophily — which may also explain the celebrity marriages (Barabási 2016).

Structural disassortativity: when hubs simply cannot all connect

There is a subtle trap here. In a simple network (at most one link between any pair), the biggest hubs of a scale-free network “should” be joined by several links to look neutral. Barabási’s example: two hubs of degree 55 and 46 in a 300-node network are expected to share about 2.8 links — but only one is allowed (Barabási 2016). Above a structural cutoff k_s \sim (\langle k\rangle N)^{1/2} that conflict is unavoidable, and when the degree exponent is \gamma<3 the natural largest hub exceeds k_s. The network then has fewer hub–hub links than chance predicts and looks disassortative even though no process favoured that (Barabási 2016). He states it as a network law: “Simple scale-free networks are disassortative.” (Barabási 2016)

The diagnostic is the one used above: compare the observed k_{nn}(k) with a degree-preserving randomisation that keeps the network simple. If they match, the correlation is structural; if they differ, something beyond the degree sequence is at work (Barabási 2016).

Why it matters for what happens on the network

Degree correlations are not a curiosity; they change the network’s behaviour (Barabási 2016):

  • The giant component. In assortative networks the giant component appears earlier (below the random graph’s \langle k\rangle=1 threshold), because hubs linked to hubs form a connected core cheaply. Disassortative networks form it later. At high \langle k\rangle the order flips: the assortative giant component is smaller, because the hub core fails to pull in the many small nodes (Barabási 2016).
  • Epidemics. Since social networks are assortative, their high-degree nodes form a core that can act as a reservoir for a disease, sustaining it even when the network as a whole looks too sparse (Barabási 2016). See spreading on networks.
  • Robustness. Removing hubs does less damage in assortative networks — the hubs cluster together and are partly redundant — and more in disassortative ones, where each hub is the only link for many small nodes (Barabási 2016). This refines the scale-free entry’s “robust yet fragile” story.
  • Path lengths. Assortative networks have a slightly shorter average path but a markedly larger diameter, because similar-degree linking builds long chains of degree-2 nodes (Barabási 2016).

Common pitfalls

WarningWhere this goes wrong
  • Reading the friendship paradox as a fact about individuals. It is a statement about averages under link-based sampling. Some people are more popular than their friends; the paradox is that the typical friend is drawn from a hub-tilted distribution (Barabási 2016).
  • Estimating population averages from link-traced samples. Snowball and contact samples reach nodes in proportion to their degree, so naive means of degree (and of anything correlated with degree) are biased upward — the weighting problem in disguise.
  • Calling a network disassortative without randomising. In simple scale-free networks, disassortativity can be purely structural. Only a comparison with a degree-preserving, simple randomisation tells you whether there is anything to explain (Barabási 2016).
  • Trusting r uncritically. Newman’s r is a linear correlation and can disagree with the shape of k_{nn}(k); mixed patterns (assortative at low k, disassortative at high k, as in the citation network) collapse into one misleading number (Barabási 2016). Plot k_{nn}(k).
  • Confusing degree assortativity with homophily. “Hubs link to hubs” is about degree; “liberals link to liberals” is about attributes. A network can show one without the other.
  • Summarising a network by its degree distribution alone. Identical p_k can hide opposite wiring, with different epidemic and robustness behaviour (Barabási 2016).

Appendix · the mathematics

Why friends are hub-tilted. A network with N nodes and L links has 2L = N\langle k\rangle link ends. A node of degree k owns k of them, so the probability that the end of a randomly chosen link is a degree-k node is

q_k=\frac{N p_k\,k}{2L}=\frac{k\,p_k}{\langle k\rangle}.

The friendship paradox. If there are no degree correlations, a neighbour’s degree is drawn from q_k, so the mean neighbour degree is

k_{nn}=\sum_k k\,q_k=\frac{\sum_k k^2 p_k}{\langle k\rangle}=\frac{\langle k^2\rangle}{\langle k\rangle} =\langle k\rangle+\frac{\sigma_k^2}{\langle k\rangle}\;\ge\;\langle k\rangle,

with equality only if every node has the same degree. The excess is the degree variance divided by the mean, which is why heavy-tailed networks show the effect so strongly. For a Poisson (Erdős–Rényi) network \sigma_k^2=\langle k\rangle, giving k_{nn}=1+\langle k\rangle.

Neutral wiring. Without correlations the link-end degrees are independent: e_{jk}=q_j q_k, and the expected number of links between a degree-k and a degree-k' node is kk'/(2L) (allowing multi-links). Then k_{nn}(k)=\sum_{k'}k'\,q_{k'} does not depend on k: a flat line at \langle k^2\rangle/\langle k\rangle.

Newman’s coefficient. With \sigma_r^2=\sum_k k^2 q_k-\left(\sum_k k q_k\right)^2,

r=\frac{\sum_{jk} jk\,(e_{jk}-q_jq_k)}{\sigma_r^2}\in[-1,1],

the Pearson correlation of the degrees at the two ends of a link. The figure computes it directly by listing each link in both directions and correlating the two columns.

The structural cutoff. Requiring at most one expected link between two nodes, kk'/(\langle k\rangle N) \le 1, and taking k=k' gives k_s\sim(\langle k\rangle N)^{1/2}. For a scale-free network the largest expected hub grows like k_{\max}\sim N^{1/(\gamma-1)}, which outruns N^{1/2} exactly when \gamma<3 — the regime of structural disassortativity.

*Your friends are, on average, more popular than you are* — not because you are unusually unpopular, but
because a friend is something you reach by following a link, and links lead to hubs more often than to
anyone else. Push that idea one step further and ask *whose* friends the hubs are: in social networks hubs
tend to link to other hubs (**assortative**), in many biological and technological networks they avoid each
other (**disassortative**). The degree distribution alone cannot tell the two apart.

## Why it matters

The network entries so far summarise a network largely by its **degree distribution** $p_k$: Poisson for a
random graph, a power law for a [scale-free network](scale-free-networks.qmd). That is a powerful summary, but
it is silent on a question every social scientist cares about: **who connects to whom?** Two networks can
have *exactly* the same list of degrees and yet wire it in completely different ways
[@barabasi_network].

Barabási opens the topic with celebrity marriage. If a celebrity picked a partner at random from roughly a
billion eligible people, the chance of choosing one of a generous list of 1,000 fellow celebrities would be
about $10^{-6}$ — they would essentially never marry each other. Yet they do [@barabasi_network]. Celebrities,
political leaders and CEOs are **hubs** of the social network, and hubs, it seems, tend to have ties to other
hubs. The yeast protein-interaction network does the opposite: its hubs link mostly to small proteins and
avoid each other [@barabasi_network]. That difference — **degree correlations** — changes how fast a disease
spreads, how easily a network falls apart, and how biased any sample built by "asking people about their
friends" will be.

## The friendship paradox: following a link is biased sampling

Pick a person at random and their number of friends is, on average, the network's mean degree $\langle k
\rangle$. Now pick a random person and then one of their friends. That friend is *not* a random person. A
node with $k$ links is reached through any of its $k$ link-ends, so the chance of landing on it is
proportional to $k$: the degree at the end of a random link follows $q_k = k\,p_k/\langle k\rangle$, tilted
toward hubs [@barabasi_network]. "We are more likely to be friends with hubs than with small-degree nodes,
simply because hubs have more friends" [@barabasi_network].

So in a network with no degree correlations, the average degree of a node's neighbours is

$$
k_{nn} = \frac{\langle k^2\rangle}{\langle k\rangle} \;>\; \langle k\rangle ,
$$

which is the **friendship paradox**, discovered by the sociologist Scott Feld: on average your friends are
more popular than you are — even though most people believe the opposite [@barabasi_network]. Even in a
random graph the gap is one whole friend ($k_{nn} = 1 + \langle k\rangle$). In scale-free networks, where
$\langle k^2\rangle$ is huge, it is enormous: $\langle k^2\rangle/\langle k\rangle$ is about 390 in the email
network and about 566 in the actor network, so "the average degree of the friends of a randomly selected
node can be hundreds of times higher" than the node's own expected degree [@barabasi_network].

For a quantitative social scientist this is first a **sampling lesson**. Any design that reaches people
*through* other people — snowball samples, contact tracing, "name your friends" modules, link-tracing on
social media — over-represents the well connected. Formally it is an unplanned version of
**probability-proportional-to-size** selection from [complex survey
estimation](../02-inference/complex-survey-estimation.qmd), with degree as the size measure: each unit's
chance of selection depends on the very quantity you are trying to measure, and the same Horvitz–Thompson
logic (weight by $1/\pi$, here $\propto 1/k$) is what undoes it.

## Assortative, neutral, disassortative

Now refine the question. Not "how popular are my friends on average?" but "does a node's popularity
predict its friends' popularity?" Barabási draws three networks with an **identical degree sequence** and
different wiring [@barabasi_network]:

- **Neutral**: links fall where chance puts them. The expected number of links between nodes of degree $k$
  and $k'$ is proportional to $k k'$ and nothing more.
- **Assortative**: hubs link to hubs and small nodes to small nodes, more than chance predicts. The extreme
  is a network made of separate complete clusters, every degree-$k$ node linked only to other degree-$k$
  nodes.
- **Disassortative**: hubs avoid each other and link to small nodes, giving a **hub-and-spoke** look.

The terms come from the social sciences, where **assortative mating** describes people marrying people
like themselves — college graduates marrying college graduates [@barabasi_network]. Network science borrowed
the word for similarity *in degree*; the older, attribute-based sense (liberal blogs citing liberal blogs)
is the [homophily](community-detection.qmd) of the community-detection entry. Disassortative mixing also
has social examples: trade links people with *different* skills — "the baker does not sell bread to other
bakers" [@barabasi_network].

## Measuring it: $k_{nn}(k)$ and the coefficient $r$

The complete information sits in the matrix $e_{ij}$, the probability that a randomly chosen link joins a
degree-$i$ node to a degree-$j$ node — but a matrix is hard to read and harder to compare
[@barabasi_network]. Two compact summaries do the work in practice:

- **The degree correlation function** $k_{nn}(k)$: for each node, average the degrees of its neighbours;
  then average that over all nodes of degree $k$. Flat means neutral; rising means assortative; falling means
  disassortative [@barabasi_network]. Barabási's worked example: a node of degree 4 whose neighbours have
  degrees 4, 3, 3 and 1 has $k_{nn} = 11/4 = 2.75$.
- **The degree correlation coefficient** $r$ (Newman): simply the **Pearson correlation** between the
  degrees at the two ends of a link, between $-1$ and $1$. Positive is assortative, zero neutral, negative
  disassortative [@barabasi_network]. The astrophysics collaboration network gives $r=0.13$, the protein
  interaction network $r=-0.04$, the power grid $r=0$ [@barabasi_network].

Barabási also fits $k_{nn}(k)\sim k^{\mu}$: $\mu = 0.37\pm0.11$ for scientific collaboration, $0.04\pm0.05$
for the power grid (indistinguishable from zero), $-0.76\pm0.04$ for the metabolic network
[@barabasi_network]. He cautions that $r$ quietly assumes a *linear* $k_{nn}(k)$ while $\mu$ assumes a power
law; both cannot hold at once, so use $r$ with care [@barabasi_network].

```{python}
#| label: fig-friendship-paradox
#| fig-cap: "Two faces of degree correlation, computed by hand. Left: a 3,000-node network grown by preferential attachment (Barabási–Albert, two links per new node). Each dot is a person: their own number of friends against their friends' average number of friends, on log axes. Orange dots sit above the dashed diagonal — people whose friends are more popular than they are — and they are the large majority; the dotted line marks ⟨k²⟩/⟨k⟩, the expected friend-degree in an uncorrelated network, far above the mean degree ⟨k⟩ = 4. Right: one Erdős–Rényi graph (1,500 nodes, mean degree 6) rewired with the Xalvi-Brunet & Sokolov algorithm. Every rewiring preserves every node's degree, yet the average neighbour degree k_nn(k) rises with k in the assortative version, falls in the disassortative one, and stays flat in the original. The legend gives Newman's coefficient r for each."
#| code-fold: true
#| code-summary: "Show the figure code (BA growth + degree-preserving rewiring, by hand)"

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(11)

def neighbour_degree_sum(E, d, n):
    # for every node, the sum of its neighbours' degrees
    return (np.bincount(E[:, 0], weights=d[E[:, 1]], minlength=n)
            + np.bincount(E[:, 1], weights=d[E[:, 0]], minlength=n))

# ---- Left: the friendship paradox on a grown (Barabási–Albert) network ----
N, m = 3000, 2
edges, ends = [(0, 1)], [0, 1]        # 'ends' lists every link end: sampling it is degree-proportional
for new in range(2, N):
    chosen = set()
    while len(chosen) < min(m, new):
        chosen.add(ends[rng.integers(len(ends))])
    for t in chosen:
        edges.append((new, t))
        ends += [new, t]
E = np.array(edges)
deg = np.bincount(E.ravel(), minlength=N)
friends_mean = neighbour_degree_sum(E, deg, N) / deg
kbar, k2_over_k = deg.mean(), (deg**2).mean() / deg.mean()
share = np.mean(friends_mean > deg)

# ---- Right: tune degree correlations on one Erdős–Rényi graph (Xalvi-Brunet & Sokolov) ----
n2, p_rule = 1500, 0.5
iu = np.triu_indices(n2, 1)
keep = rng.random(len(iu[0])) < 6.0 / (n2 - 1)
E0 = np.column_stack([iu[0][keep], iu[1][keep]])

def xbs(E_in, assortative, steps=12000):
    # pick two links, order their four end-nodes by degree, rewire; degrees never change
    E = E_in.copy()
    present = set(map(tuple, np.sort(E, axis=1)))
    d = np.bincount(E.ravel(), minlength=n2)
    for _ in range(steps):
        i, j = rng.choice(len(E), 2, replace=False)
        quad = [E[i, 0], E[i, 1], E[j, 0], E[j, 1]]
        if len(set(quad)) < 4:
            continue
        if rng.random() < p_rule:
            o = sorted(quad, key=lambda v: d[v])     # lowest ... highest degree
            pairs = [(o[3], o[2]), (o[1], o[0])] if assortative else [(o[3], o[0]), (o[2], o[1])]
        else:
            rng.shuffle(quad)                          # otherwise a random degree-preserving swap
            pairs = [(quad[0], quad[1]), (quad[2], quad[3])]
        pairs = [tuple(sorted(p)) for p in pairs]
        if pairs[0] in present or pairs[1] in present:
            continue                                   # keep the network simple
        present.discard(tuple(sorted(E[i]))); present.discard(tuple(sorted(E[j])))
        present.update(pairs)
        E[i], E[j] = pairs
    return E

def knn_curve(E):
    d = np.bincount(E.ravel(), minlength=n2)
    knn_i = neighbour_degree_sum(E, d, n2) / np.maximum(d, 1)
    ks = np.array([k for k in range(1, d.max() + 1) if np.sum(d == k) >= 8])
    r = np.corrcoef(np.r_[d[E[:, 0]], d[E[:, 1]]], np.r_[d[E[:, 1]], d[E[:, 0]]])[0, 1]
    return ks, np.array([knn_i[d == k].mean() for k in ks]), r

fig, (axL, axR) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(9.4, 4.3))

axL.scatter(deg, friends_mean, s=6, color="#b7b6a8", alpha=0.6, lw=0)
above = friends_mean > deg
axL.scatter(deg[above], friends_mean[above], s=6, color="#e8531d", alpha=0.5, lw=0)
lim = [1.5, deg.max() * 1.3]
axL.plot(lim, lim, color="#161613", lw=1.2, ls="--")
axL.axhline(k2_over_k, color="#5e5e54", lw=1.0, ls=":")
axL.set_xscale("log"); axL.set_yscale("log"); axL.set_xlim(lim); axL.set_ylim(lim)
axL.text(22, k2_over_k * 1.12, rf"$\langle k^2\rangle/\langle k\rangle \approx {k2_over_k:.1f}$" + "\n" + rf"vs  $\langle k\rangle = {kbar:.1f}$",
         fontsize=8.3, color="#5e5e54")
axL.text(30, 3.0, "your friends = you\n(diagonal)", fontsize=8.3, color="#161613")
axL.text(14, 75, f"{share:.0%} of people:\nfriends more popular\nthan they are",
         fontsize=8.8, color="#e8531d")
axL.set_xlabel("your number of friends  k  (log)")
axL.set_ylabel("your friends' average number of friends (log)")
axL.set_title("The friendship paradox")

styles = [("assortative", True, "#161613", "o"), ("neutral (ER)", None, "#8a8a7e", "s"),
          ("disassortative", False, "#5e5e54", "^")]
for label, mode, col, mk in styles:
    Ex = E0 if mode is None else xbs(E0, mode)
    ks, kn, r = knn_curve(Ex)
    axR.plot(ks, kn, marker=mk, ms=4.5, color=col, lw=1.4, label=f"{label}:  r = {r:+.2f}")
axR.set_xlabel("degree  k")
axR.set_ylabel("average neighbour degree  $k_{nn}(k)$")
axR.set_title("Same degrees, different wiring")
axR.legend(fontsize=8.3, frameon=False, loc="upper center")
fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

The right panel is the chapter's central claim made visible: the three networks share **the same degree of
every single node**, so any statistic computed from $p_k$ alone is identical across them. Only the wiring
differs — and $k_{nn}(k)$ sees it.

## What real networks do

Comparing $k_{nn}(k)$ for ten reference networks against degree-preserving randomisations of themselves,
Barabási finds [@barabasi_network]:

- **Social networks are assortative.** The mobile-phone call network, scientific collaboration and the actor
  network all show rising $k_{nn}(k)$ that their randomised versions do not reproduce — the assortativity is
  real, not an artefact of the degree distribution.
- **The power grid is the only truly neutral** network in the set.
- **Email, protein-interaction and metabolic networks are disassortative**, but their randomised versions
  are too: the disassortativity is **structural**, a by-product of being scale-free (below).

Why would social networks be assortative? Barabási lists candidate mechanisms rather than a settled answer:
the tendency to form communities, society's many devices for bringing hubs together ("from professional
committees to TV shows"), and **homophily** — which may also explain the celebrity marriages
[@barabasi_network].

## Structural disassortativity: when hubs simply cannot all connect

There is a subtle trap here. In a **simple** network (at most one link between any pair), the biggest hubs
of a scale-free network "should" be joined by several links to look neutral. Barabási's example: two hubs of
degree 55 and 46 in a 300-node network are expected to share about 2.8 links — but only one is allowed
[@barabasi_network]. Above a **structural cutoff** $k_s \sim (\langle k\rangle N)^{1/2}$ that conflict is
unavoidable, and when the degree exponent is $\gamma<3$ the natural largest hub exceeds $k_s$. The network
then has fewer hub–hub links than chance predicts and looks disassortative even though no process favoured
that [@barabasi_network]. He states it as a network law: **"Simple scale-free networks are
disassortative."** [@barabasi_network]

The diagnostic is the one used above: compare the observed $k_{nn}(k)$ with a **degree-preserving
randomisation** that keeps the network simple. If they match, the correlation is structural; if they differ,
something beyond the degree sequence is at work [@barabasi_network].

## Why it matters for what happens on the network

Degree correlations are not a curiosity; they change the network's behaviour [@barabasi_network]:

- **The giant component.** In assortative networks the giant component appears *earlier* (below the random
  graph's $\langle k\rangle=1$ threshold), because hubs linked to hubs form a connected core cheaply.
  Disassortative networks form it later. At high $\langle k\rangle$ the order flips: the assortative giant
  component is *smaller*, because the hub core fails to pull in the many small nodes
  [@barabasi_network].
- **Epidemics.** Since social networks are assortative, their high-degree nodes form a core that can act as a
  **reservoir** for a disease, sustaining it even when the network as a whole looks too sparse
  [@barabasi_network]. See [spreading on networks](spreading-on-networks.qmd).
- **Robustness.** Removing hubs does *less* damage in assortative networks — the hubs cluster together and
  are partly redundant — and *more* in disassortative ones, where each hub is the only link for many small
  nodes [@barabasi_network]. This refines the [scale-free](scale-free-networks.qmd) entry's
  "robust yet fragile" story.
- **Path lengths.** Assortative networks have a slightly shorter average path but a markedly *larger*
  diameter, because similar-degree linking builds long chains of degree-2 nodes [@barabasi_network].

## Common pitfalls

::: {.callout-warning title="Where this goes wrong"}
- **Reading the friendship paradox as a fact about individuals.** It is a statement about averages under
  link-based sampling. Some people are more popular than their friends; the paradox is that the
  *typical friend* is drawn from a hub-tilted distribution [@barabasi_network].
- **Estimating population averages from link-traced samples.** Snowball and contact samples reach nodes in
  proportion to their degree, so naive means of degree (and of anything correlated with degree) are biased
  upward — the [weighting](../02-inference/complex-survey-estimation.qmd) problem in disguise.
- **Calling a network disassortative without randomising.** In simple scale-free networks, disassortativity
  can be purely **structural**. Only a comparison with a degree-preserving, simple randomisation tells you
  whether there is anything to explain [@barabasi_network].
- **Trusting $r$ uncritically.** Newman's $r$ is a linear correlation and can disagree with the shape of
  $k_{nn}(k)$; mixed patterns (assortative at low $k$, disassortative at high $k$, as in the citation
  network) collapse into one misleading number [@barabasi_network]. Plot $k_{nn}(k)$.
- **Confusing degree assortativity with homophily.** "Hubs link to hubs" is about *degree*; "liberals link to
  liberals" is about *attributes*. A network can show one without the other.
- **Summarising a network by its degree distribution alone.** Identical $p_k$ can hide opposite wiring, with
  different epidemic and robustness behaviour [@barabasi_network].
:::

## Appendix · the mathematics {.unnumbered}

::: {.math-appendix}

**Why friends are hub-tilted.** A network with $N$ nodes and $L$ links has $2L = N\langle k\rangle$ link
ends. A node of degree $k$ owns $k$ of them, so the probability that the end of a randomly chosen link is a
degree-$k$ node is

$$
q_k=\frac{N p_k\,k}{2L}=\frac{k\,p_k}{\langle k\rangle}.
$$

**The friendship paradox.** If there are no degree correlations, a neighbour's degree is drawn from $q_k$,
so the mean neighbour degree is

$$
k_{nn}=\sum_k k\,q_k=\frac{\sum_k k^2 p_k}{\langle k\rangle}=\frac{\langle k^2\rangle}{\langle k\rangle}
=\langle k\rangle+\frac{\sigma_k^2}{\langle k\rangle}\;\ge\;\langle k\rangle,
$$

with equality only if every node has the same degree. The excess is the degree **variance** divided by the
mean, which is why heavy-tailed networks show the effect so strongly. For a Poisson (Erdős–Rényi) network
$\sigma_k^2=\langle k\rangle$, giving $k_{nn}=1+\langle k\rangle$.

**Neutral wiring.** Without correlations the link-end degrees are independent: $e_{jk}=q_j q_k$, and the
expected number of links between a degree-$k$ and a degree-$k'$ node is $kk'/(2L)$ (allowing multi-links).
Then $k_{nn}(k)=\sum_{k'}k'\,q_{k'}$ does not depend on $k$: a flat line at $\langle k^2\rangle/\langle k\rangle$.

**Newman's coefficient.** With $\sigma_r^2=\sum_k k^2 q_k-\left(\sum_k k q_k\right)^2$,

$$
r=\frac{\sum_{jk} jk\,(e_{jk}-q_jq_k)}{\sigma_r^2}\in[-1,1],
$$

the Pearson correlation of the degrees at the two ends of a link. The figure computes it directly by listing
each link in both directions and correlating the two columns.

**The structural cutoff.** Requiring at most one expected link between two nodes, $kk'/(\langle k\rangle N)
\le 1$, and taking $k=k'$ gives $k_s\sim(\langle k\rangle N)^{1/2}$. For a scale-free network the largest
expected hub grows like $k_{\max}\sim N^{1/(\gamma-1)}$, which outruns $N^{1/2}$ exactly when $\gamma<3$ —
the regime of structural disassortativity.
