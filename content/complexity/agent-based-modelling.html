Agent-Based Modelling

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source

If emergence is the what, agent-based modelling is the how: grow a society from the bottom up — autonomous agents, simple rules, local interaction — and watch the macro patterns appear.

Emergence says macro patterns arise from micro rules. Agent-based modelling (ABM) is the method that produces them: simulate a population of autonomous, heterogeneous agents following simple behavioural rules and interacting locally, then watch what collective structure emerges. It is “generative” social science. Epstein and Axtell hope that one day the question “Can you explain it?” will be heard as “Can you grow it?” ABM reaches the heterogeneity, adaptation, and out-of-equilibrium dynamics that equation-based models can’t.

Why it matters

ABM is a third mode of doing science alongside induction (find patterns in data) and deduction (prove theorems): you grow a phenomenon and study the conditions that generate it. For social science — full of heterogeneous, adapting, interacting people who are rarely in equilibrium — it reaches questions that regression and closed-form game theory cannot, and it makes the otherwise-vague claim “this macro pattern could arise from these micro rules” into a runnable demonstration.

It is also a laboratory. You cannot order a real society to stop trading, or abolish inheritance and rerun the last century, but you can do both to an artificial society and measure what changes. Epstein and Axtell call the ability to “noiselessly compute” the effect of a rule change on a summary statistic one of the method’s most powerful features (Epstein and Axtell 1996).

What ABM is

A bottom-up computational method: simulate many autonomous, heterogeneous agents interacting locally with each other and an environment, and observe the macro patterns that arise (Epstein and Axtell 1996; Miller and Page 2007). Epstein & Axtell build every artificial society from three ingredients: agents (with internal states and behavioural rules), an environment (the medium they act on), and rules (for agent–environment, agent–agent and environment–environment interaction). The whole society is then a coupled dynamical system: the agents’ next state depends on the agents and the environment now, and so does the environment’s (Epstein and Axtell 1996).

Their test of an explanation is generative: can you grow the phenomenon from agent rules? Growing it shows that a set of micro-rules is sufficient to produce the macro pattern. It does not show the rules are the true ones, because “the mapping from micro-rules to macrostructure could be many to one.” Epstein and Axtell add that in the social sciences that would be an embarrassment of riches, since in many areas any generating rule set would be an advance (Epstein and Axtell 1996).

Sugarscape: growing a society

The foundational example is Sugarscape (Epstein and Axtell 1996). A 50 × 50 grid (wrapped into a torus) holds a renewable resource, “sugar,” piled into two mountains with capacities from 0 up to 4. Each period, every site grows back one unit toward its capacity (rule G_1). Agents are born with a vision (1–6 sites, along the four lattice directions only) and a metabolism (1–4 units of sugar burned per period), and they follow one rule:

Rule M. Look as far as your vision allows, find the nearest unoccupied site with the most sugar, go there, and collect the sugar.

No two agents may share a site. An agent whose sugar runs out dies (Epstein and Axtell 1996).

That is the whole model, and it already yields three textbook ideas:

  • Carrying capacity. Start 400 agents on the landscape and the population falls to about 224 and stays there. The environment supports only so many harvesters. Raising agents’ mean vision or lowering their metabolism raises the capacity (Epstein and Axtell 1996).
  • Selection without sex. Low-metabolism, high-vision agents survive more often, so the population’s make-up drifts. In one run mean vision rose from 3.5 to 4.1 and mean metabolism fell from 2.5 to 1.8 over 500 periods, with no reproduction at all (Epstein and Axtell 1996).
  • Decentralised harvesting. You could write a central plan telling each agent which site to harvest when, and with agents of differing abilities it would be very complicated. M is also a harvesting programme, just a highly decentralised one that nobody wrote down as a plan (Epstein and Axtell 1996).

Add seasons, pollution, sex, culture, combat, trade, credit and disease, one rule at a time, and the same landscape produces migration and hibernation, a tragedy of the commons, population cycles, cultural “tribes,” wars, markets and epidemics. Epstein and Axtell describe the full system as realising “a vast panoply of possible histories,” including a proto-history of their own (Epstein and Axtell 1996). None of it is written into any agent.

Two kinds of emergence

Sugarscape also sharpens what “emergent” means. Epstein and Axtell reserve the word for stable macroscopic patterns arising from local interaction, not for anything that surprised the modeller (“surprising to whom?”). Then they separate two types (Epstein and Axtell 1996):

  1. A collective behaviour an individual could have but doesn’t. With wide vision, agents march across the landscape in coherent diagonal waves, though each agent can only move north, south, east or west. We know what it would mean for one agent to move diagonally; the rule forbids it.
  2. A property only a group can have. A wealth distribution is meaningful only for a collection. There is no such thing as one agent’s distribution.

The distinction matters for measurement. Type 2 is where social statistics live: inequality, segregation indices and turnout rates are properties of groups, and ABM is a way to grow them.

An inequality laboratory: Lorenz curves and the Gini

To study wealth, Epstein and Axtell change two things. Agents who lived forever would accumulate without limit, so they give each one a finite lifespan. They also add a replacement rule R[60,100], so the population stays constant: when an agent dies, a newborn with random genes, position and endowment takes its place, with a maximum age drawn from 60 to 100. They place 250 agents (about the carrying capacity) with endowments of 5 to 25 (Epstein and Axtell 1996).

The wealth histogram starts roughly symmetric and ends highly skewed. Such skewed distributions appear “for wide ranges of agent and environment specifications” and seem characteristic of heterogeneous agents extracting resources from a landscape of fixed capacity. Income, meaning sugar harvested minus metabolism, is much less skewed, since it can never exceed 3 (the peak sugar of 4 minus the minimum metabolism of 1). The wealthiest agents tend to have high vision and low metabolism, but to reach the very top an agent must also be born high on the sugarscape and live long: endowment plus luck plus time (Epstein and Axtell 1996).

How should you summarise that inequality? You could fit a Pareto or Pareto–Lévy curve and report its exponent, but Epstein and Axtell note that the exponent’s interpretation “is far from unambiguous.” They use the Gini coefficient, which is distribution-free, read off the Lorenz curve (Epstein and Axtell 1996):

  • Rank agents from poorest to richest. The Lorenz curve plots the share of total wealth held by the poorest fraction x of the population.
  • Perfect equality is the 45° line. Any inequality bows the curve below it: the poorest x% hold less than x% of the wealth.
  • The Gini measures how far the curve sags: 0 if everyone holds the same, 1 if one agent holds everything.

In their run the Gini starts at about 0.230 and settles near 0.503, roughly constant thereafter. That is “much lower than that seen in industrial societies.” When the agents also reproduce and pass their wealth to their children (inheritance rule I), the Gini climbs as high as 0.743. Trade skews the distribution further: it raises the carrying capacity, so more agents survive, but it also raises inequality, which Epstein and Axtell read as a tradeoff between economic equality and economic performance (Epstein and Axtell 1996). Each extra rule is an experiment with the rest of the world held fixed.

The point is directed at an old argument. Some have read the Pareto “law” as a natural order, an immutable law of nature. In an artificial society you can change the local rules on inheritance or taxation and see whether the same global pattern comes back (Epstein and Axtell 1996). Their inheritance result has a sting: some Social Darwinists oppose transfers to the poor because selection is “best for the species,” but in Sugarscape inheritance from the rich to their children is exactly what retards selection on vision (Epstein and Axtell 1996).

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from matplotlib.colors import LinearSegmentedColormap

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
G = 50
xx, yy = np.meshgrid(np.arange(G), np.arange(G))
def mountain(cx, cy):                                  # terraced peak, capacity 4 -> 0
    d = np.sqrt((xx - cx)**2 + (yy - cy)**2)
    return np.clip(4 - d // 4, 0, 4)
cap = np.maximum(mountain(14, 35), mountain(35, 14))
sugar = cap.copy()

n = 250
occ = -np.ones((G, G), int)                            # who sits on each site (-1 = empty)
pos = np.zeros((n, 2), int); vision = np.zeros(n, int); metab = np.zeros(n, int)
wealth = np.zeros(n); age = np.zeros(n, int); maxage = np.zeros(n, int)

def newborn(a):                                        # replacement rule R[60,100]
    while True:
        i, j = rng.integers(0, G, 2)
        if occ[i, j] < 0:
            break
    pos[a] = (i, j); occ[i, j] = a
    vision[a] = rng.integers(1, 7); metab[a] = rng.integers(1, 5)
    wealth[a] = rng.integers(5, 26); age[a] = 0; maxage[a] = rng.integers(60, 101)

for a in range(n):
    newborn(a)
age[:] = rng.integers(0, 60, n)                        # stagger ages so deaths don't bunch
w_start = wealth.copy()

def gini(w):
    w = np.sort(w); k = np.arange(1, len(w) + 1)
    return 2 * np.sum(k * w) / (len(w) * np.sum(w)) - (len(w) + 1) / len(w)

def lorenz(w):
    w = np.sort(w)
    return np.r_[0, np.arange(1, len(w) + 1) / len(w)], np.r_[0, np.cumsum(w) / w.sum()]

T = 500
g_path = [gini(wealth)]
for step in range(T):
    sugar = np.minimum(sugar + 1, cap)                 # growback G_1
    for a in rng.permutation(n):                       # random activation order
        i, j = pos[a]
        best = (sugar[i, j], 0, i, j)                  # (sugar, distance, row, col)
        for di, dj in ((-1, 0), (1, 0), (0, -1), (0, 1)):
            for d in range(1, vision[a] + 1):
                ni, nj = (i + di * d) % G, (j + dj * d) % G
                if occ[ni, nj] >= 0:
                    continue                           # occupied sites are off-limits
                s = sugar[ni, nj]
                if s > best[0] or (s == best[0] and d < best[1]):
                    best = (s, d, ni, nj)              # most sugar, nearest on ties
        _, _, bi, bj = best
        occ[i, j] = -1; occ[bi, bj] = a; pos[a] = (bi, bj)
        wealth[a] += sugar[bi, bj] - metab[a]; sugar[bi, bj] = 0
        age[a] += 1
        if wealth[a] <= 0 or age[a] >= maxage[a]:      # starvation or old age
            occ[bi, bj] = -1
            newborn(a)
    g_path.append(gini(wealth))

Economics and demography are not separable

Switch on sexual reproduction (rule S: fertile agents with enough sugar mate with neighbours and the child inherits genes from both) and population size becomes an output of the model. With the baseline fertility ages the population holds roughly constant. Small changes to the rule produce very different histories (Epstein and Axtell 1996):

  • End fertility ten years earlier and the population oscillates with a period of about 200 years, never falling below about 250. The average agent lives about 80 years, so no agent ever lives through a full swing. The cycle is a property of the society, not of anyone’s experience.
  • Restore the fertility ages but lower the sugar needed to reproduce, and the oscillations become larger, starting above 1,000 in amplitude and settling near 800, with a period of about 125 years.
  • Combine both changes and the swings become so violent that at one trough the population is too sparse or too uniform to recover. The society goes extinct after three growth surges.

The most arresting demonstration comes in their conclusion. Take 500 agents on a two-good (sugar and spice) landscape, with movement and reproduction but no trade: the population crashes. Rerun everything identically but switch on bilateral trade. For the first 75 years it looks like the same crash, and a statistician extrapolating from those years would predict one. Then the society pulls out of the dive, grows to more than twice its starting size, and settles into sustained oscillations with troughs near 700 and a period of about 115 years, again longer than any agent lives (Epstein and Axtell 1996). (How that bilateral trade works, and why it never reaches the textbook equilibrium, is the subject of markets without an auctioneer.)

Epstein and Axtell draw two conclusions. One is methodological: it questions “the very feasibility of a separate science of demography” that ignores the economic regime. The other concerns policy: “economic policy can be a kind of population policy” (Epstein and Axtell 1996). For a social scientist the lesson is that subsystems you are used to modelling separately may be coupled, and a model that cuts the coupling can mispredict even the direction of change.

Why ABM complements equation-based models

Traditional neoclassical / equation-based models lean on a representative agent (homogeneity) and assume equilibrium. ABM relaxes both (Miller and Page 2007; Epstein and Axtell 1996):

  • Heterogeneity. Agents can differ in traits, endowments, vision, metabolism — diversity that often makes mathematical models intractable but is trivial in a simulation (Epstein and Axtell 1996).
  • Adaptation. Agents can learn and change their behaviour from feedback, so you can study how systems evolve (Miller and Page 2007). The evolution of cooperation is the classic case: strategies that adapt to each other can reach outcomes a one-shot rational analysis rules out.
  • Out-of-equilibrium dynamics. Where closed-form tools “purify” a model down to static rest points, ABM follows the transient paths and perpetual motion that real social life usually exhibits (Epstein and Axtell 1996).

Common pitfalls

WarningWhere this goes wrong
  • Confusing ‘can grow it’ with ‘is true’. Generating a pattern shows it’s sufficient, not that it’s the real mechanism — many rule-sets can grow the same macro fact. (Epstein and Axtell 1996)
  • Measuring a statistic that never settles. If agents live forever, wealth accumulates without limit and there is no stationary distribution to describe. Sugarscape’s Gini is meaningful only because finite lives and a replacement rule keep the population stationary. Check that a reported summary has actually converged. (Epstein and Axtell 1996)
  • Reading a fitted curve as a law. A skewed distribution can be fitted by a Pareto curve, but that does not make it a law of nature. The laboratory question is whether the shape survives a change in the rules. (Epstein and Axtell 1996)
  • Cutting couplings for convenience. Population dynamics modelled without the economy, or vice versa, can get the trajectory qualitatively wrong. The no-trade and trade societies look identical for 75 years and then diverge completely. (Epstein and Axtell 1996)
  • Parameter overload. ABMs have many knobs; without disciplined calibration and sensitivity analysis they can be made to show almost anything. (Miller and Page 2007)
  • Skipping validation. Verification (the code does what you meant) is not validation (it matches the world); do both. (Miller and Page 2007)
  • Over-reading a single run. Stochastic ABMs need many runs and distributions of outcomes, not one pretty picture. Epstein and Axtell’s carrying-capacity curves average ten runs per point. (Miller and Page 2007; Epstein and Axtell 1996)
  • Mistaking richness for rigour. A baroque model is harder to understand, not more credible; prefer the simplest model that grows the phenomenon. (Miller and Page 2007)

Appendix · the mathematics

Artificial society as a dynamical system. Writing A(t) for the state of all agents and E(t) for the environment, Epstein and Axtell’s society is the coupled map

A(t+1)=f\big(A(t),E(t)\big),\qquad E(t+1)=g\big(A(t),E(t)\big).

The Sugarscape rules in the figure. Growback G_1: s_{ij}(t+1)=\min\{s_{ij}(t)+1,\ c_{ij}\}. Movement rule M: an agent at (i,j) with vision v inspects the unoccupied sites (i\pm d, j) and (i, j\pm d) for d=1,\dots,v (and its own site), moves to the one with maximum sugar (nearest on ties), and updates w\leftarrow w+s-m, setting that site’s sugar to 0. It dies if w\le0 or its age reaches its maximum, and replacement rule R[a,b] puts a newborn with random attributes and maximum age \sim U\{a,\dots,b\} in its place, so n is constant. With a=60,\ b=100 the mean death age is 80 when few agents starve.

Lorenz curve and Gini. Sort wealths w_{(1)}\le\dots\le w_{(n)}. The Lorenz curve passes through the points \big(k/n,\ \sum_{i\le k}w_{(i)}/\sum_i w_{(i)}\big). The Gini is twice the area between the 45° line and the curve:

G = 1-2\int_0^1 L(x)\,dx \;=\;\frac{2\sum_k k\,w_{(k)}}{n\sum_k w_{(k)}}-\frac{n+1}{n},

so G=0 under perfect equality and G\to1 as one agent holds everything. Unlike a Pareto exponent, G needs no assumed distributional form, which is why Epstein and Axtell prefer it.

Why inequality emerges. With identical rules but heterogeneous vision, metabolism, birthplace and lifespan, agents who start near a peak or see farther accumulate faster; the renewable-but-finite sugar means their gains are others’ losses, and cumulative advantage (preferential attachment’s cousin) amplifies small initial differences into a skewed wealth distribution. Income is capped at \max c - \min m = 4-1 = 3 per period, so its spread stays narrow while wealth, which integrates income over a lifetime, spreads out. Inequality is emergent, not assumed.

::: {.lead-kernel}
[Emergence](emergence-and-self-organisation.qmd) says macro patterns arise from micro rules. **Agent-based
modelling (ABM)** is the method that *produces* them: simulate a population of **autonomous, heterogeneous
agents** following simple behavioural rules and interacting locally, then watch what **collective structure
emerges**. It is **"generative" social science**. Epstein and Axtell hope that one day the question *"Can you
explain it?"* will be heard as *"Can you grow it?"* ABM reaches the heterogeneity, adaptation, and
out-of-equilibrium dynamics that equation-based models can't.
:::

## Why it matters

ABM is a **third mode of doing science** alongside induction (find patterns in data) and deduction (prove
theorems): you *grow* a phenomenon and study the conditions that generate it. For social science — full of
heterogeneous, adapting, interacting people who are rarely in equilibrium — it reaches questions that
regression and closed-form game theory cannot, and it makes the otherwise-vague claim "this macro pattern
*could* arise from these micro rules" into a runnable demonstration.

It is also a **laboratory**. You cannot order a real society to stop trading, or abolish inheritance and
rerun the last century, but you can do both to an artificial society and measure what changes. Epstein and
Axtell call the ability to "noiselessly compute" the effect of a rule change on a summary statistic one of
the method's most powerful features [@epstein_axtell].

## What ABM is

A **bottom-up computational method**: simulate many **autonomous, heterogeneous agents** interacting locally
with each other and an environment, and **observe the macro patterns that arise** [@epstein_axtell;
@millerpage_cas]. Epstein & Axtell build every artificial society from three ingredients: **agents** (with
internal states and behavioural rules), an **environment** (the medium they act on), and **rules** (for
agent–environment, agent–agent and environment–environment interaction). The whole society is then a coupled
dynamical system: the agents' next state depends on the agents and the environment now, and so does the
environment's [@epstein_axtell].

Their test of an explanation is **generative**: can you grow the phenomenon from agent rules? Growing it
shows that a set of micro-rules is *sufficient* to produce the macro pattern. It does not show the rules are
the true ones, because "the mapping from micro-rules to macrostructure could be many to one." Epstein and
Axtell add that in the social sciences that would be an embarrassment of riches, since in many areas *any*
generating rule set would be an advance [@epstein_axtell].

## Sugarscape: growing a society

The foundational example is **Sugarscape** [@epstein_axtell]. A 50 × 50 grid (wrapped into a torus) holds a
renewable resource, "sugar," piled into two mountains with capacities from 0 up to 4. Each period, every
site grows back one unit toward its capacity (rule $G_1$). Agents are born with a **vision** (1–6 sites,
along the four lattice directions only) and a **metabolism** (1–4 units of sugar burned per period), and
they follow one rule:

> **Rule M.** Look as far as your vision allows, find the **nearest unoccupied** site with the most sugar,
> go there, and collect the sugar.

No two agents may share a site. An agent whose sugar runs out dies [@epstein_axtell].

That is the whole model, and it already yields three textbook ideas:

- **Carrying capacity.** Start 400 agents on the landscape and the population falls to about **224** and
  stays there. The environment supports only so many harvesters. Raising agents' mean vision or lowering
  their metabolism raises the capacity [@epstein_axtell].
- **Selection without sex.** Low-metabolism, high-vision agents survive more often, so the population's
  make-up drifts. In one run mean vision rose from 3.5 to 4.1 and mean metabolism fell from 2.5 to 1.8 over
  500 periods, with no reproduction at all [@epstein_axtell].
- **Decentralised harvesting.** You could write a central plan telling each agent which site to harvest
  when, and with agents of differing abilities it would be very complicated. M is also a harvesting
  programme, just a highly decentralised one that nobody wrote down as a plan [@epstein_axtell].

Add seasons, pollution, sex, culture, combat, trade, credit and disease, one rule at a time, and the same
landscape produces migration and hibernation, a tragedy of the commons, population cycles, cultural
"tribes," wars, markets and epidemics. Epstein and Axtell describe the full system as realising "a vast
panoply of possible histories," including a proto-history of their own [@epstein_axtell]. None of it is
written into any agent.

### Two kinds of emergence

Sugarscape also sharpens what "emergent" means. Epstein and Axtell reserve the word for **stable macroscopic
patterns arising from local interaction**, not for anything that surprised the modeller ("surprising to
whom?"). Then they separate two types [@epstein_axtell]:

1. **A collective behaviour an individual *could* have but doesn't.** With wide vision, agents march across
   the landscape in coherent **diagonal waves**, though each agent can only move north, south, east or west.
   We know what it would mean for one agent to move diagonally; the rule forbids it.
2. **A property only a group can have.** A **wealth distribution** is meaningful only for a collection.
   There is no such thing as one agent's distribution.

The distinction matters for measurement. Type 2 is where social statistics live: inequality, segregation
indices and turnout rates are properties of groups, and ABM is a way to grow them.

## An inequality laboratory: Lorenz curves and the Gini

To study wealth, Epstein and Axtell change two things. Agents who lived forever would accumulate without
limit, so they give each one a **finite lifespan**. They also add a **replacement rule** $R[60,100]$, so the
population stays constant: when an agent dies, a newborn with random genes, position and endowment takes its
place, with a maximum age drawn from 60 to 100. They place 250 agents (about the carrying capacity) with
endowments of 5 to 25 [@epstein_axtell].

The wealth histogram starts roughly symmetric and ends **highly skewed**. Such skewed distributions appear
"for wide ranges of agent and environment specifications" and seem characteristic of heterogeneous agents
extracting resources from a landscape of fixed capacity. **Income**, meaning sugar harvested minus
metabolism, is much less skewed, since it can never exceed 3 (the peak sugar of 4 minus the minimum
metabolism of 1). The wealthiest agents tend to have high vision and low metabolism, but to reach the very
top an agent must also be **born high on the sugarscape and live long**: endowment plus luck plus time
[@epstein_axtell].

How should you summarise that inequality? You could fit a Pareto or Pareto–Lévy curve and report its
exponent, but Epstein and Axtell note that the exponent's interpretation "is far from unambiguous." They use
the **Gini coefficient**, which is *distribution-free*, read off the **Lorenz curve** [@epstein_axtell]:

- Rank agents from poorest to richest. The Lorenz curve plots the share of total wealth held by the poorest
  fraction $x$ of the population.
- Perfect equality is the 45° line. Any inequality bows the curve below it: the poorest $x$% hold less than
  $x$% of the wealth.
- The Gini measures how far the curve sags: **0** if everyone holds the same, **1** if one agent holds
  everything.

In their run the Gini starts at about **0.230** and settles near **0.503**, roughly constant thereafter.
That is "much lower than that seen in industrial societies." When the agents also reproduce and pass their
wealth to their children (inheritance rule $I$), the Gini climbs as high as **0.743**. Trade skews the
distribution further: it raises the carrying capacity, so more agents survive, but it also raises
inequality, which Epstein and Axtell read as a tradeoff between economic equality and economic performance
[@epstein_axtell]. Each extra rule is an experiment with the rest of the world held
fixed.

The point is directed at an old argument. Some have read the Pareto "law" as a *natural order*, an
immutable law of nature. In an artificial society you can change the local rules on inheritance or taxation
and see whether the same global pattern comes back [@epstein_axtell]. Their inheritance result has a sting:
some Social Darwinists oppose transfers to the poor because selection is "best for the species," but in
Sugarscape inheritance from the rich to their children is exactly what **retards selection** on vision
[@epstein_axtell].

```{python}
#| label: fig-sugarscape
#| fig-cap: "Sugarscape rebuilt by hand, following Epstein & Axtell's wealth run: a 50 × 50 torus with two sugar mountains (capacity 0–4, growback 1 per period), 250 agents with vision 1–6, metabolism 1–4, endowments 5–25, one agent per site, death by starvation or old age, and replacement rule R[60,100]. Left: where the agents are at the end; they crowd the two peaks. Middle: the Gini coefficient of wealth over time, rising from the near-equal start and then fluctuating around a stationary level; the dashed lines mark the book's own start and end values (0.230 and 0.503). Right: the Lorenz curve at the start (ink) and the end (orange), bowing away from the line of equality as inequality emerges. Nothing in the rules mentions inequality. Our landscape is a stylised copy of theirs, so the numbers should be close to the book's, not identical."
#| code-fold: true
#| code-summary: "Show the figure code (Sugarscape by hand)"

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from matplotlib.colors import LinearSegmentedColormap

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
G = 50
xx, yy = np.meshgrid(np.arange(G), np.arange(G))
def mountain(cx, cy):                                  # terraced peak, capacity 4 -> 0
    d = np.sqrt((xx - cx)**2 + (yy - cy)**2)
    return np.clip(4 - d // 4, 0, 4)
cap = np.maximum(mountain(14, 35), mountain(35, 14))
sugar = cap.copy()

n = 250
occ = -np.ones((G, G), int)                            # who sits on each site (-1 = empty)
pos = np.zeros((n, 2), int); vision = np.zeros(n, int); metab = np.zeros(n, int)
wealth = np.zeros(n); age = np.zeros(n, int); maxage = np.zeros(n, int)

def newborn(a):                                        # replacement rule R[60,100]
    while True:
        i, j = rng.integers(0, G, 2)
        if occ[i, j] < 0:
            break
    pos[a] = (i, j); occ[i, j] = a
    vision[a] = rng.integers(1, 7); metab[a] = rng.integers(1, 5)
    wealth[a] = rng.integers(5, 26); age[a] = 0; maxage[a] = rng.integers(60, 101)

for a in range(n):
    newborn(a)
age[:] = rng.integers(0, 60, n)                        # stagger ages so deaths don't bunch
w_start = wealth.copy()

def gini(w):
    w = np.sort(w); k = np.arange(1, len(w) + 1)
    return 2 * np.sum(k * w) / (len(w) * np.sum(w)) - (len(w) + 1) / len(w)

def lorenz(w):
    w = np.sort(w)
    return np.r_[0, np.arange(1, len(w) + 1) / len(w)], np.r_[0, np.cumsum(w) / w.sum()]

T = 500
g_path = [gini(wealth)]
for step in range(T):
    sugar = np.minimum(sugar + 1, cap)                 # growback G_1
    for a in rng.permutation(n):                       # random activation order
        i, j = pos[a]
        best = (sugar[i, j], 0, i, j)                  # (sugar, distance, row, col)
        for di, dj in ((-1, 0), (1, 0), (0, -1), (0, 1)):
            for d in range(1, vision[a] + 1):
                ni, nj = (i + di * d) % G, (j + dj * d) % G
                if occ[ni, nj] >= 0:
                    continue                           # occupied sites are off-limits
                s = sugar[ni, nj]
                if s > best[0] or (s == best[0] and d < best[1]):
                    best = (s, d, ni, nj)              # most sugar, nearest on ties
        _, _, bi, bj = best
        occ[i, j] = -1; occ[bi, bj] = a; pos[a] = (bi, bj)
        wealth[a] += sugar[bi, bj] - metab[a]; sugar[bi, bj] = 0
        age[a] += 1
        if wealth[a] <= 0 or age[a] >= maxage[a]:      # starvation or old age
            occ[bi, bj] = -1
            newborn(a)
    g_path.append(gini(wealth))

fig, (axL, axM, axR) = plt.subplots(1, 3, figsize=(11.2, 3.9),
                                    gridspec_kw={"width_ratios": [1, 1.25, 1]})
axL.imshow(sugar, cmap=LinearSegmentedColormap.from_list(
    "qss_seq", ["#f4f3ec", "#f2b48a", "#e8531d", "#b53c12"]), origin="lower")
axL.scatter(pos[:, 1], pos[:, 0], s=7, color="#161613")
axL.set_title("agents crowd the two peaks"); axL.axis("off")

axM.plot(g_path, lw=1.8, color="#e8531d")
for v in (0.230, 0.503):
    axM.axhline(v, ls="--", lw=1, color="#8a8a7e")
axM.text(T, 0.503, "book: 0.503", ha="right", va="bottom", fontsize=8, color="#5e5e54")
axM.text(T, 0.230, "book: 0.230", ha="right", va="bottom", fontsize=8, color="#5e5e54")
axM.set_ylim(0, 0.65)
axM.set_xlabel("time (periods)"); axM.set_ylabel("Gini coefficient of wealth")
axM.set_title(f"inequality emerges (last 100: mean {np.mean(g_path[-100:]):.2f})")

x0, y0 = lorenz(w_start); x1, y1 = lorenz(wealth)
axR.plot([0, 1], [0, 1], color="#b7b6a8", lw=1.2, label="perfect equality")
axR.plot(x0, y0, color="#161613", lw=1.6, label=f"start  (Gini {gini(w_start):.2f})")
axR.plot(x1, y1, color="#e8531d", lw=2.0, label=f"t = {T}  (Gini {gini(wealth):.2f})")
axR.set_xlabel("poorest fraction of agents"); axR.set_ylabel("share of total wealth")
axR.set_title("Lorenz curves"); axR.legend(frameon=False, fontsize=8, loc="upper left")
axR.set_aspect("equal")
fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

## Economics and demography are not separable

Switch on sexual reproduction (rule $S$: fertile agents with enough sugar mate with neighbours and the child
inherits genes from both) and population size becomes an output of the model. With the baseline fertility
ages the population holds roughly constant. Small changes to the rule produce very different histories
[@epstein_axtell]:

- End fertility **ten years earlier** and the population oscillates with a period of about **200 years**,
  never falling below about 250. The average agent lives about 80 years, so **no agent ever lives through a
  full swing**. The cycle is a property of the society, not of anyone's experience.
- Restore the fertility ages but lower the sugar needed to reproduce, and the oscillations become larger,
  starting above 1,000 in amplitude and settling near 800, with a period of about 125 years.
- Combine both changes and the swings become so violent that at one trough the population is too sparse or
  too uniform to recover. The society goes **extinct after three growth surges**.

The most arresting demonstration comes in their conclusion. Take 500 agents on a two-good (sugar and spice)
landscape, with movement and reproduction but **no trade**: the population crashes. Rerun everything
identically but switch on bilateral **trade**. For the first 75 years it looks like the same crash, and a
statistician extrapolating from those years would predict one. Then the society pulls out of the dive, grows
to more than twice its starting size, and settles into sustained oscillations with troughs near 700 and a
period of about 115 years, again longer than any agent lives [@epstein_axtell]. (How that bilateral trade
works, and why it never reaches the textbook equilibrium, is the subject of
[markets without an auctioneer](markets-without-an-auctioneer.qmd).)

Epstein and Axtell draw two conclusions. One is methodological: it questions "the very feasibility of a
separate science of demography" that ignores the economic regime. The other concerns policy: "economic
policy can be a kind of population policy" [@epstein_axtell]. For a social scientist the lesson is that
**subsystems you are used to modelling separately may be coupled**, and a model that cuts the coupling can
mispredict even the direction of change.

## Why ABM complements equation-based models

Traditional neoclassical / equation-based models lean on a **representative agent** (homogeneity) and assume
**equilibrium**. ABM relaxes both [@millerpage_cas; @epstein_axtell]:

- **Heterogeneity.** Agents can differ in traits, endowments, vision, metabolism — diversity that often makes
  mathematical models **intractable** but is trivial in a simulation [@epstein_axtell].
- **Adaptation.** Agents can **learn and change** their behaviour from feedback, so you can study how systems
  *evolve* [@millerpage_cas]. The [evolution of cooperation](evolution-of-cooperation.qmd) is the classic
  case: strategies that adapt to each other can reach outcomes a one-shot rational analysis rules out.
- **Out-of-equilibrium dynamics.** Where closed-form tools "purify" a model down to static rest points, ABM
  follows the **transient paths** and **perpetual motion** that real social life usually exhibits
  [@epstein_axtell].

## Common pitfalls

::: {.callout-warning title="Where this goes wrong"}
- **Confusing 'can grow it' with 'is true'.** Generating a pattern shows it's *sufficient*, not that it's the
  real mechanism — many rule-sets can grow the same macro fact. [@epstein_axtell]
- **Measuring a statistic that never settles.** If agents live forever, wealth accumulates without limit and
  there is no stationary distribution to describe. Sugarscape's Gini is meaningful only because finite lives
  and a replacement rule keep the population stationary. Check that a reported summary has actually
  converged. [@epstein_axtell]
- **Reading a fitted curve as a law.** A skewed distribution can be fitted by a Pareto curve, but that does
  not make it a law of nature. The laboratory question is whether the shape survives a change in the rules.
  [@epstein_axtell]
- **Cutting couplings for convenience.** Population dynamics modelled without the economy, or vice versa, can
  get the trajectory qualitatively wrong. The no-trade and trade societies look identical for 75 years and
  then diverge completely. [@epstein_axtell]
- **Parameter overload.** ABMs have many knobs; without disciplined calibration and **sensitivity analysis**
  they can be made to show almost anything. [@millerpage_cas]
- **Skipping validation.** *Verification* (the code does what you meant) is not *validation* (it matches the
  world); do both. [@millerpage_cas]
- **Over-reading a single run.** Stochastic ABMs need **many runs** and distributions of outcomes, not one
  pretty picture. Epstein and Axtell's carrying-capacity curves average ten runs per point.
  [@millerpage_cas; @epstein_axtell]
- **Mistaking richness for rigour.** A baroque model is harder to understand, not more credible; prefer the
  simplest model that grows the phenomenon. [@millerpage_cas]
:::
