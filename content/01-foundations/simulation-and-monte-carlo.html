Simulation & Monte Carlo

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source

When the integral is too hard, roll dice instead. Using random numbers to compute probabilities, expectations, and integrals no formula can reach — to see randomness work, and to test your own methods on data where you know the truth.

Most real probability questions have no closed-form answer. Monte Carlo methods sidestep the algebra: generate random draws, and let the Law of Large Numbers turn their average into the answer. Lambert calls it “abandoning brute force” — instead of grinding out an integral, you sample and count. It is how you estimate an intractable probability, integrate in a thousand dimensions, build a bootstrap sampling distribution, check that an estimator finds a truth you planted — and, not least, see the limit theorems happen on screen.

Why it matters

Simulation is both a computational tool (approximate what you can’t derive) and a way of thinking. Lambert stresses it’s the most direct route to internalising the “repeated sample and sampling distribution” concepts at the heart of statistics: when you can simulate a process a million times, the abstract idea of a sampling distribution becomes something you can literally plot. (Lambert 2018) His image: lost in dense fog, you take random steps and record where you are and how high — and from enough random samples you build a reliable map of the mountain. (Lambert 2018) Sampling is how you map a landscape you can’t see all at once.

For a working researcher it is also a laboratory. Moffatt’s Experimetrics leans on simulated data throughout, for a plain reason: when you generate the data yourself you know the true parameters, so you can check whether an estimator, a piece of code or a test actually does its job before you trust it with real data. (Moffatt 2016)

Generating random variables

Everything rests on turning a stream of uniform random numbers into draws from the distribution you want. Two workhorse methods: (Ross 2018)

  • Inverse-transform. The basic technique: to draw from a distribution with CDF F, generate U\sim \text{Uniform}(0,1) and set X = F^{-1}(U). (Ross 2018) (This is the probability integral transform run backwards.) An exponential variate, for instance, is X=-\ln(U)/\lambda. (Ross 2018) Clean when you can invert F. The same move turns uniforms into normals: Z=\Phi^{-1}(U), then \mu+\sigma Z for any normal and e^{\mu+\sigma Z} for a lognormal. (Moffatt 2016)
  • Rejection sampling. When F^{-1} is intractable, draw from an easy proposal g(x) and accept the draw with probability proportional to f(x)/g(x), rejecting the rest. (Lambert 2018) It always works for a density you can evaluate, but Lambert warns it can be “pretty wasteful” — many draws thrown away — which is exactly the inefficiency that motivates MCMC for hard, high-dimensional targets. (Lambert 2018)

Computer “random” numbers are pseudo-random: an algorithm produces them, and its only input is the seed. Set the same seed and you get the same sequence, which is how two researchers reproduce each other’s simulation exactly. (Moffatt 2016)

Monte Carlo integration

The central trick: an integral is an expectation, and an expectation is an average you can sample. To approximate \mathbb E[g(X)]=\int g(x)f(x)\,dx, draw X_1,\dots,X_n from f and take the sample mean: (Ross 2018) \mathbb E[g(X)] \approx \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n g(X_i). The Law of Large Numbers guarantees this converges to the true value, and the CLT tells you the error shrinks like 1/\sqrt{n}. (Ross 2018) That 1/\sqrt n rate is the catch and the magic: to halve the error you need four times the samples — but the rate doesn’t depend on the dimension, which is why Monte Carlo crushes ordinary numerical integration in high dimensions.

The textbook demonstration is estimating \pi: drop random points in the unit square, and the fraction landing inside the quarter-circle is \pi/4 (top-left panel of Figure 1).

Beating the √n barrier: variance reduction

That 1/\sqrt n rate is a floor on brute-force sampling, not on cleverness. Variance-reduction techniques buy accuracy not by drawing more, but by drawing smarter — engineering the samples so the same n estimates the answer with a smaller standard error. (Ross 2018) Three classics:

  • Antithetic variables. Instead of independent draws, use pairs that are negatively correlated: to estimate \mathbb E[g(U)], evaluate g at both U and 1-U. When g is monotone the two are negatively correlated, so their average has lower variance than two independent draws — an overshoot in one half of the pair is offset by an undershoot in the other. Almost free, and often a real win. (Ross 2018)
  • Conditioning (Rao–Blackwellisation). Replace the raw estimator by its conditional expectation given some variable you can integrate out by hand. Because \operatorname{Var}\!\big(\mathbb E[X\mid Y]\big) \le \operatorname{Var}(X) always (the law of total variance), conditioning can only shrink the variance — you do part of the averaging with a formula instead of by sampling. (Ross 2018)
  • Control variates. Subtract off a correlated quantity whose mean you already know. If C is correlated with the target and \mathbb E[C] is known, estimate \mathbb E[g(X)] with g(X) - \beta\,(C - \mathbb E[C]): the known part cancels the fluctuation it explains, leaving a tighter estimate. (Ross 2018)

All three exploit one idea — use structure you already know to remove noise you don’t need to simulate — and they pay off most when each draw is expensive (a slow model, a costly step), so squeezing more from every sample is worth the effort. (Ross 2018)

Drawing smarter still: Halton sequences

Push the antithetic idea to its limit and you stop drawing at random at all. Moffatt, following Train (2003), names two weaknesses of independent random draws when the goal is to approximate an integral: (Moffatt 2016)

  • Coverage. Independent draws can clump in some places and leave gaps in others. A sequence that is guaranteed to spread evenly should approximate the integral better.
  • Covariance. Independent draws have zero covariance, so the variance of their average is just one draw’s variance divided by R. If successive draws were negatively correlated, a draw that overshoots would tend to be followed by one that undershoots, and the average would sit closer to the truth.

A Halton sequence (Halton, 1960) delivers both. (Moffatt 2016) It is built from a prime number, which plays the role of a seed. With prime 2 the sequence starts \tfrac12,\ \tfrac14,\ \tfrac34,\ \tfrac18,\ \tfrac58,\ \tfrac38,\ \tfrac78,\ \tfrac1{16},\ \tfrac9{16},\ \dots Each new point drops into the biggest gap left so far: first split the interval in half, then fill the quarters, then the eighths. With prime 3 it goes \tfrac13, \tfrac23, \tfrac19, \tfrac49, \tfrac79, \tfrac29, \dots (Moffatt 2016) Pair a base-2 sequence with a base-3 one and you get points that tile the unit square far more evenly than random points do (top row of Figure 1, which reproduces the contrast in Moffatt’s Figure 10.2). (Moffatt 2016) The uniform points are then pushed through \Phi^{-1} to become normal draws, and Moffatt shows that 1,000 transformed Halton points trace the normal density much more closely than 1,000 random normals do. (Moffatt 2016)

The payoff in the \pi example is visible in the bottom-left panel. From a few hundred points on, the Halton estimate’s error is roughly 2 to 4 times smaller than the typical pseudo-random error at the same n. The gain is real but modest. Our reading of the figure (not a claim from the sources) is that the quarter-circle’s edge, a sharp jump in the function being averaged, limits how much even spacing can help.

Classic experiments

The method’s pedigree is full of vivid examples: (Ross 2018; Grinstead and Snell 1997)

  • Estimating \pi — random points in a square, count the fraction inside the circle (the figure below).
  • Buffon’s needle — drop needles on a ruled grid; the fraction crossing a line yields \pi. A physical Monte Carlo, two centuries before computers. (Grinstead and Snell 1997)
  • The bootstrap — “a simple Monte Carlo technique” that approximates a sampling distribution by resampling with replacement from the data you have. (Grinstead and Snell 1997)
  • Seeing the LLN — simulate trials and watch the running mean home in on the expectation, the law made visible. (Grinstead and Snell 1997)

This is also the engine of every executable figure in this book: each one is a small Monte Carlo, generating data to make an idea concrete.

Simulation as a research tool: plant the truth, then look for it

Moffatt gives three reasons a researcher simulates data. (Moffatt 2016)

  1. To demonstrate a method when no real data are to hand. The data should mimic real data as closely as possible, including known anomalies. But no conclusion about behaviour may ever be drawn from it.
  2. To test new estimation code. Code that crashes is easy to spot. Code that runs and returns wrong numbers is not. On simulated data the true parameters are known, so you can judge the code by whether it recovers them.
  3. To run a Monte Carlo study. Simulate many data sets, estimate on each, and study the distribution of the estimates. Is it centred on the truth (no bias)? How spread out is it (precision)? For a test, does its statistic follow the distribution the theory promises when the null is true?

The vocabulary is short. (Moffatt 2016) The data generating process (DGP) is the model you simulate from, with every random part fully specified and the sample size fixed. Each repetition is a replication: simulate the DGP once, fit a model once. The fitted model need not match the DGP. Fitting a deliberately wrong model is one of the main uses of the method, because it shows what misspecification costs. That is most useful where theory says little, for example in small samples.

A first study: OLS recovers its truth. Moffatt simulates y = 2 + 1\cdot x + 0.5\,u with x\sim U(0,1), u\sim N(0,1) and n=100, holding x fixed across 1,000 replications. (His printed equation 9.1 shows a slope of 0.5 and an error s.d. of 1, but his code and his discussion use slope 1 and s.d. 0.5.) The averages of the 1,000 estimates are 1.0008 for the slope, 1.9990 for the intercept and 0.4992 for the residual s.d. — all on target, so the estimators are unbiased. The spread of the 1,000 slopes (0.162) is also close to the standard error a single regression reported (0.153). The simulation therefore checks the standard errors as well as the point estimates. (Moffatt 2016) This is the sampling distribution made literal. Moffatt cites Kennedy (1998), who urges Monte Carlo in teaching for exactly this reason. (Moffatt 2016)

A second study: what ignoring people’s differences costs. Experiments produce panels, many decisions per subject, and subjects differ. Moffatt simulates 50 subjects making 20 binary choices each, with latent propensity y^*_{it} = -1 + 2x_{it} + u_i + \epsilon_{it}, where u_i is a subject effect with variance 1. He then fits two models to each of 1,000 data sets: a random-effects probit, which is the correct model, and a pooled probit, which treats all 1,000 choices as if they came from identical people. The random-effects slope is centred on the true 2.0. The pooled slope is biased down by around 30%. (Moffatt 2016) Ignoring heterogeneity in a probit does more than understate the standard errors, as it would in a linear model. It shrinks the coefficient itself. The bottom-right panel of Figure 1 re-runs this study, and the appendix shows where the 30% comes from.

A third study: testing a test. In his chapter on ordinal data, Moffatt proposes a Hausman test of whether subjects report their strength of preference truthfully. It compares a binary probit on the choice with an ordered probit that also uses the stated confidence. He then checks the test by simulation. Under the null (everyone truthful, 1,000 replications of 1,050 observations), the simulated statistic’s 95th percentile is 3.70 against the \chi^2(1) value of 3.84, and its 99th is 6.18 against 6.63. The size is therefore close to nominal, and a Q–Q plot against \chi^2(1) hugs the 45° line. Under an alternative in which 30% of subjects answer the confidence question at random, 71.4% of the statistics exceed 3.84. The test’s power against that alternative is 0.714. (Moffatt 2016) Repeat that over a grid of the share answering at random and you trace the test’s whole power function. (Moffatt 2016)

When the likelihood is itself an integral: maximum simulated likelihood

The random-effects probit raises a computing problem. Each subject has one unobserved u_i, shared by all of their decisions, so their contribution to the likelihood has to average over every value u_i could take. That is an integral, and it has no closed form. (Moffatt 2016) There are two ways to compute it: quadrature (a fixed grid of well-chosen points) or simulation. Moffatt uses simulation, which gives the method of maximum simulated likelihood (MSL). (Moffatt 2016)

The idea is the Monte Carlo integral again. An integral against a density is an expectation, so replace it by an average over R draws. (Moffatt 2016) For each subject:

  1. take R draws of the random effect (Halton draws, transformed to normal and scaled by \sigma_u);
  2. for each draw, compute the probability of the subject’s whole sequence of choices, the product over their T tasks;
  3. average those R probabilities. That average approximates the subject’s likelihood. Log the averages and sum them over subjects.

Two details matter in practice. (Moffatt 2016) First, the draws belong to the subject, not the row, so all T rows of a subject share the same R draws. Second, the draws are made once, at the start, and held fixed while the optimiser searches over \beta and \sigma_u. Only the transformation \sigma_u z changes from step to step. Moffatt also stores every non-integer variable in double precision (about 16 digits). Single precision carries only about 7, which can leave the simulated log-likelihood too rough for the optimiser to find the maximum. (Moffatt 2016)

As the check from reason 2 above, Moffatt simulates 60 subjects × 20 tasks with \beta_1=-1, \beta_2=2, \sigma_u=0.5. He fits the model with his own MSL code (125 Halton draws) and with Stata’s built-in random-effects probit (12-point adaptive quadrature): (Moffatt 2016)

\hat\beta_2 (slope) \hat\beta_1 (intercept) \hat\sigma_u log-likelihood

(Moffatt 2016) The same draws then give each subject’s posterior random effect: the average of u over the draws, weighted by how well each draw explains that subject’s choices. Plotted against the true u_i, the posteriors line up closely. That is a second check, and it lets you place individual subjects. (Moffatt 2016) MSL is the workhorse behind the rest of Moffatt’s book: the panel hurdle model, risky-choice models with continuous variation in risk attitude, and altruism models with heterogeneous utility parameters. (Moffatt 2016)

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def halton(n, base):                       # radical inverse: reverse the base-p digits of 1, 2, 3, ...
    out = np.empty(n)
    for k in range(n):
        f, r, i = 1.0, 0.0, k + 1
        while i > 0:
            f /= base; r += f * (i % base); i //= base
        out[k] = r
    return out

def Phi(z):                                # normal CDF (Abramowitz–Stegun 7.1.26, error < 1.5e-7)
    a = np.abs(z) / np.sqrt(2); t = 1 / (1 + 0.3275911 * a)
    poly = ((((1.061405429 * t - 1.453152027) * t + 1.421413741) * t - 0.284496736) * t + 0.254829592) * t
    return 0.5 * (1 + np.sign(z) * (1 - poly * np.exp(-a * a)))

    return x

grid = np.unique(np.round(np.logspace(1, np.log10(N), 25)).astype(int))
h_err = np.abs(4 * np.cumsum((hal**2).sum(1) <= 1)[grid - 1] / grid - np.pi)   # Halton: one fixed sequence
p_err = np.array([4 * np.cumsum((rng.random((N, 2))**2).sum(1) <= 1)[grid - 1] / grid - np.pi
                  for _ in range(200)])
p_rmse = np.sqrt((p_err**2).mean(axis=0))                                    # pseudo: typical error, 200 runs

# --- Monte Carlo study (Moffatt 9.4): pooled probit vs random-effects probit by MSL --
n, T, R, reps = 50, 20, 50, 300
x = rng.random(n * T)                      # x held fixed across replications, as in Moffatt
X = np.column_stack([np.ones(n * T), x])
z = Phi_inv(halton(n * R, 3)).reshape(n, R)  # R normal Halton draws per subject, fixed throughout

def pooled_probit(y):
    b = np.zeros(2); q = 2 * y - 1
    for _ in range(30):
        xb = X @ b
        lam = q * np.exp(-0.5 * xb**2) / np.sqrt(2 * np.pi) / np.clip(Phi(q * xb), 1e-12, 1)
        H = -(X * (lam * (lam + xb))[:, None]).T @ X
        step = np.linalg.solve(H, X.T @ lam); b -= step
        if np.abs(step).max() < 1e-8:
            break
    return b

def sim_loglik(th, y):                     # simulated log-likelihood: average over draws, per subject
    xb = (th[0] + th[1] * x).reshape(n, T); q = (2 * y - 1).reshape(n, T)
    P = Phi(q[:, :, None] * (xb[:, :, None] + np.exp(th[2]) * z[:, None, :]))
    return np.log(P.prod(axis=1).mean(axis=1)).sum()

def msl_probit(y, th):                     # Newton with numerical derivatives
    e, I = 1e-4, np.eye(3) * 1e-4
    for _ in range(40):
        g = np.array([(sim_loglik(th + I[i], y) - sim_loglik(th - I[i], y)) / (2 * e) for i in range(3)])
        H = np.array([[(sim_loglik(th + I[i] + I[j], y) - sim_loglik(th + I[i] - I[j], y)
                        - sim_loglik(th - I[i] + I[j], y) + sim_loglik(th - I[i] - I[j], y)) / (4 * e * e)
                       for j in range(3)] for i in range(3)])
        step = np.linalg.solve(H, g) if np.all(np.linalg.eigvalsh(H) < 0) else -0.1 * g
        th = th - step
        if np.abs(step).max() < 1e-6:
            break
    return th

pooled, re = [], []
for _ in range(reps):
    u = np.repeat(rng.standard_normal(n), T)
    y = ((-1 + 2 * x + u + rng.standard_normal(n * T)) > 0).astype(float)
    b = pooled_probit(y); pooled.append(b[1])
    re.append(msl_probit(y, np.array([b[0] * np.sqrt(2), b[1] * np.sqrt(2), 0.0]))[1])

Common pitfalls

WarningWhere this goes wrong

  • Expecting fast convergence. The error is only O(1/\sqrt n): 100× the samples buys 10× the accuracy. Plan the sample size for the precision you need. (Ross 2018)

  • Forgetting Monte Carlo error is itself random. A simulation estimate has a standard error; report it (or use enough draws that it’s negligible). (Ross 2018)

  • Wasteful rejection sampling. A poorly-matched proposal rejects almost everything; in high dimensions this fails badly — reach for MCMC. (Lambert 2018)

  • A bad random-number generator. Garbage pseudo-randomness (correlations, short period) poisons every estimate; use a vetted generator and seed it for reproducibility.

  • Reading behaviour off simulated data. Simulated data show how a method performs. They say nothing about how people behave, however realistic they are made to look. (Moffatt 2016)

  • Pooling heterogeneous subjects in a nonlinear model. In a linear model, ignoring subject effects mainly costs you honest standard errors. (Li 2023) In a probit it biases the coefficient itself, about 30% downward in Moffatt’s design. Model the heterogeneity, for example with a random effect. (Moffatt 2016)

  • Redrawing the simulation draws during estimation. In MSL the draws are made once and held fixed while the optimiser searches. If they change between steps, the objective function changes under the optimiser’s feet. Store everything in double precision too, or the simulated likelihood may be too rough to maximise. (Moffatt 2016)

  • Trusting a test’s nominal size and power. A test’s size and power are claims about its sampling distribution, and you can check them by simulation. Simulate under the null to check the size; simulate under a stated alternative to measure the power. (Moffatt 2016)

Appendix · the mathematics

Why negative correlation helps (Moffatt §10.3.1). For two draws, \operatorname{Var}\!\Big(\tfrac{t(\epsilon_1)+t(\epsilon_2)}{2}\Big) =\tfrac14\Big(\operatorname{Var}t(\epsilon_1)+\operatorname{Var}t(\epsilon_2)+2\operatorname{Cov}\big(t(\epsilon_1),t(\epsilon_2)\big)\Big). With independent draws the covariance is zero and the variance is \operatorname{Var}t(\epsilon)/2. A negative covariance pushes it below that. Antithetic pairs and Halton sequences both work through this term.

The Halton point. Write the index k in base p, k=\sum_j a_j p^j, and reflect the digits about the “decimal” point: h_p(k)=\sum_j a_j p^{-(j+1)}. For p=2: k=1=1_2\to0.1_2=\tfrac12; k=2=10_2\to0.01_2=\tfrac14; k=3=11_2\to0.11_2=\tfrac34. In two dimensions, use a different prime per coordinate.

Pooled probit under a neglected random effect. If y^*_{it}=x_{it}'\beta+u_i+\epsilon_{it} with u_i\sim N(0,\sigma_u^2) independent of \epsilon_{it}\sim N(0,1), then u_i+\epsilon_{it}\sim N(0,1+\sigma_u^2) and P(y_{it}=1\mid x_{it})=\Phi\!\Big(\frac{x_{it}'\beta}{\sqrt{1+\sigma_u^2}}\Big). The pooled probit consistently estimates \beta/\sqrt{1+\sigma_u^2}. With \sigma_u^2=1 that is \beta/\sqrt2, a factor of 0.707 and so about 29% below \beta, which is Moffatt’s “around 30%”. The coefficients shrink, but their ratios are unchanged.

Random-effects probit likelihood (Moffatt eqs. 10.11, 10.14). With yy_{it}=2y_{it}-1, \log L=\sum_{i=1}^n\ln\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\prod_{t=1}^T\Phi\big(yy_{it}(x_{it}'\beta+u)\big)\, \frac1{\sigma_u}\phi\!\Big(\frac u{\sigma_u}\Big)\,du \;\approx\;\sum_{i=1}^n\ln\Big[\frac1R\sum_{r=1}^R\prod_{t=1}^T\Phi\big(yy_{it}(x_{it}'\beta+\sigma_u z_{r,i})\big)\Big], where z_{1,i},\dots,z_{R,i} are subject i’s fixed, normal-transformed Halton draws. Maximise the right-hand side over (\beta,\sigma_u); Moffatt parameterises \ln\sigma_u to keep \sigma_u>0.

Posterior random effect (eq. 10.12). With w_{r,i}=\prod_t\Phi\big(yy_{it}(x_{it}'\hat\beta+\hat\sigma_u z_{r,i})\big), \hat u_i=E(u_i\mid y_{i1},\dots,y_{iT})\approx\frac{\sum_r \hat\sigma_u z_{r,i}\,w_{r,i}}{\sum_r w_{r,i}}.

The Hausman statistic in the strength-of-preference test (§9.4). For the single slope on w, H=(\hat b_{\text{probit}}-\hat b_{\text{oprobit}})^2/(\hat V_{\text{probit}}-\hat V_{\text{oprobit}}), which is \chi^2(1) under the null. The ordered probit is efficient under the null but inconsistent if the confidence reports are noise; the binary probit is consistent either way.

::: {.lead-kernel}
Most real probability questions have no closed-form answer. **Monte Carlo** methods sidestep the algebra:
generate random draws, and let the [Law of Large Numbers](law-of-large-numbers.qmd) turn their average into
the answer. Lambert calls it *"abandoning brute force"* — instead of grinding out an integral, you *sample*
and count. It is how you estimate an intractable probability, integrate in a thousand dimensions, build a
[bootstrap](../02-inference/resampling-and-nonparametric-tests.qmd) sampling distribution, check that an
estimator finds a truth you planted — and, not least, *see* the limit theorems happen on screen.
:::

## Why it matters

Simulation is both a **computational tool** (approximate what you can't derive) and a **way of thinking**.
Lambert stresses it's the most direct route to internalising the *"repeated sample and sampling
distribution"* concepts at the heart of statistics: when you can simulate a process a million times, the
abstract idea of a sampling distribution becomes something you can literally plot. [@lambert_bayes] His image:
**lost in dense fog, you take random steps and record where you are and how high — and from enough random
samples you build a reliable map of the mountain.** [@lambert_bayes] Sampling is how you map a landscape you
can't see all at once.

For a working researcher it is also a **laboratory**. Moffatt's *Experimetrics* leans on simulated data
throughout, for a plain reason: when you generate the data yourself you know the true parameters, so you can
check whether an estimator, a piece of code or a test actually does its job before you trust it with real
data. [@moffatt_experimetrics]

## Generating random variables

Everything rests on turning a stream of **uniform** random numbers into draws from the distribution you want.
Two workhorse methods: [@ross_first_course]

- **Inverse-transform.** The basic technique: to draw from a distribution with CDF $F$, generate $U\sim
  \text{Uniform}(0,1)$ and set $X = F^{-1}(U)$. [@ross_first_course] (This is the [probability integral
  transform](transformations-and-sums.qmd) run backwards.) An exponential variate, for instance, is
  $X=-\ln(U)/\lambda$. [@ross_first_course] Clean when you can invert $F$. The same move turns uniforms into
  normals: $Z=\Phi^{-1}(U)$, then $\mu+\sigma Z$ for any normal and $e^{\mu+\sigma Z}$ for a lognormal.
  [@moffatt_experimetrics]
- **Rejection sampling.** When $F^{-1}$ is intractable, draw from an easy **proposal** $g(x)$ and **accept**
  the draw with probability proportional to $f(x)/g(x)$, rejecting the rest. [@lambert_bayes] It always works
  for a density you can evaluate, but Lambert warns it can be *"pretty wasteful"* — many draws thrown away —
  which is exactly the inefficiency that motivates [MCMC](../02-inference/credible-intervals-and-computation.qmd)
  for hard, high-dimensional targets. [@lambert_bayes]

Computer "random" numbers are **pseudo-random**: an algorithm produces them, and its only input is the
**seed**. Set the same seed and you get the same sequence, which is how two researchers reproduce each other's
simulation exactly. [@moffatt_experimetrics]

## Monte Carlo integration

The central trick: **an integral is an expectation, and an expectation is an average you can sample.** To
approximate $\mathbb E[g(X)]=\int g(x)f(x)\,dx$, draw $X_1,\dots,X_n$ from $f$ and take the **sample mean**:
[@ross_first_course]
$$
\mathbb E[g(X)] \approx \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n g(X_i).
$$
The [Law of Large Numbers](law-of-large-numbers.qmd) guarantees this converges to the true value, and the
[CLT](../02-inference/sampling-distributions-clt.qmd) tells you the error shrinks like $1/\sqrt{n}$.
[@ross_first_course] That $1/\sqrt n$ rate is the catch *and* the magic: to halve the error you need *four
times* the samples — but the rate **doesn't depend on the dimension**, which is why Monte Carlo crushes
ordinary numerical integration in high dimensions.

The textbook demonstration is estimating $\pi$: drop random points in the unit square, and the fraction
landing inside the quarter-circle is $\pi/4$ (top-left panel of @fig-monte-carlo-pi).

## Beating the √n barrier: variance reduction

That $1/\sqrt n$ rate is a floor on *brute-force* sampling, not on cleverness. **Variance-reduction
techniques** buy accuracy not by drawing more, but by drawing *smarter* — engineering the samples so the same
$n$ estimates the answer with a smaller standard error. [@ross_first_course] Three classics:

- **Antithetic variables.** Instead of independent draws, use *pairs* that are negatively correlated: to
  estimate $\mathbb E[g(U)]$, evaluate $g$ at both $U$ and $1-U$. When $g$ is monotone the two are negatively
  correlated, so their average has *lower* variance than two independent draws — an overshoot in one half of
  the pair is offset by an undershoot in the other. Almost free, and often a real win. [@ross_first_course]
- **Conditioning (Rao–Blackwellisation).** Replace the raw estimator by its **conditional expectation** given
  some variable you can integrate out by hand. Because
  $\operatorname{Var}\!\big(\mathbb E[X\mid Y]\big) \le \operatorname{Var}(X)$ *always* (the law of total
  variance), conditioning can only shrink the variance — you do part of the averaging with a formula instead of
  by sampling. [@ross_first_course]
- **Control variates.** Subtract off a correlated quantity whose mean you already know. If $C$ is correlated
  with the target and $\mathbb E[C]$ is known, estimate $\mathbb E[g(X)]$ with
  $g(X) - \beta\,(C - \mathbb E[C])$: the known part cancels the fluctuation it explains, leaving a tighter
  estimate. [@ross_first_course]

All three exploit one idea — **use structure you already know to remove noise you don't need to simulate** —
and they pay off most when each draw is expensive (a slow model, a costly step), so squeezing more from every
sample is worth the effort. [@ross_first_course]

## Drawing smarter still: Halton sequences {#sec-halton}

Push the antithetic idea to its limit and you stop drawing at random at all. Moffatt, following Train (2003),
names two weaknesses of independent random draws when the goal is to approximate an integral:
[@moffatt_experimetrics]

- **Coverage.** Independent draws can clump in some places and leave gaps in others. A sequence that is
  *guaranteed* to spread evenly should approximate the integral better.
- **Covariance.** Independent draws have zero covariance, so the variance of their average is just one draw's
  variance divided by $R$. If successive draws were **negatively** correlated, a draw that overshoots would tend
  to be followed by one that undershoots, and the average would sit closer to the truth.

A **Halton sequence** (Halton, 1960) delivers both. [@moffatt_experimetrics] It is built from a prime number,
which plays the role of a seed. With prime 2 the sequence starts
$$
\tfrac12,\ \tfrac14,\ \tfrac34,\ \tfrac18,\ \tfrac58,\ \tfrac38,\ \tfrac78,\ \tfrac1{16},\ \tfrac9{16},\ \dots
$$
Each new point drops into the biggest gap left so far: first split the interval in half, then fill the
quarters, then the eighths. With prime 3 it goes $\tfrac13, \tfrac23, \tfrac19, \tfrac49, \tfrac79, \tfrac29,
\dots$ [@moffatt_experimetrics] Pair a base-2 sequence with a base-3 one and you get points that tile the unit
square far more evenly than random points do (top row of @fig-monte-carlo-pi, which reproduces the contrast in
Moffatt's Figure 10.2). [@moffatt_experimetrics] The uniform points are then pushed through $\Phi^{-1}$ to
become normal draws, and Moffatt shows that 1,000 transformed Halton points trace the normal density much more
closely than 1,000 random normals do. [@moffatt_experimetrics]

The payoff in the $\pi$ example is visible in the bottom-left panel. From a few hundred points on, the Halton
estimate's error is roughly 2 to 4 times smaller than the typical pseudo-random error at the same $n$. The gain
is real but modest. Our reading of the figure (not a claim from the sources) is that the quarter-circle's
edge, a sharp jump in the function being averaged, limits how much even spacing can help.

## Classic experiments

The method's pedigree is full of vivid examples: [@ross_first_course; @grinstead_snell]

- **Estimating $\pi$** — random points in a square, count the fraction inside the circle (the figure below).
- **Buffon's needle** — drop needles on a ruled grid; the fraction crossing a line yields $\pi$. A *physical*
  Monte Carlo, two centuries before computers. [@grinstead_snell]
- **The bootstrap** — *"a simple Monte Carlo technique"* that approximates a [sampling
  distribution](../02-inference/sampling-distributions-clt.qmd) by **resampling with replacement** from the
  data you have. [@grinstead_snell]
- **Seeing the LLN** — simulate trials and watch the running mean home in on the expectation, the law made
  visible. [@grinstead_snell]

This is also the engine of every executable figure in this book: each one is a small Monte Carlo, generating
data to make an idea concrete.

## Simulation as a research tool: plant the truth, then look for it {#sec-monte-carlo-study}

Moffatt gives three reasons a researcher simulates data. [@moffatt_experimetrics]

1. **To demonstrate a method when no real data are to hand.** The data should mimic real data as closely as
   possible, including known anomalies. But no conclusion about behaviour may ever be drawn from it.
2. **To test new estimation code.** Code that crashes is easy to spot. Code that runs and returns wrong numbers
   is not. On simulated data the true parameters are known, so you can judge the code by whether it recovers
   them.
3. **To run a Monte Carlo study.** Simulate many data sets, estimate on each, and study the *distribution* of
   the estimates. Is it centred on the truth (no bias)? How spread out is it (precision)? For a test, does its
   statistic follow the distribution the theory promises when the null is true?

The vocabulary is short. [@moffatt_experimetrics] The **data generating process (DGP)** is the model you
simulate from, with every random part fully specified and the sample size fixed. Each repetition is a
**replication**: simulate the DGP once, fit a model once. The fitted model need not match the DGP. Fitting a
deliberately wrong model is one of the main uses of the method, because it shows what misspecification costs.
That is most useful where theory says little, for example in small samples.

**A first study: OLS recovers its truth.** Moffatt simulates $y = 2 + 1\cdot x + 0.5\,u$ with
$x\sim U(0,1)$, $u\sim N(0,1)$ and $n=100$, holding $x$ fixed across 1,000 replications. (His printed
equation 9.1 shows a slope of 0.5 and an error s.d. of 1, but his code and his discussion use slope 1 and
s.d. 0.5.) The averages of the 1,000 estimates are 1.0008 for the slope, 1.9990 for the intercept and 0.4992
for the residual s.d. — all on target, so the estimators are unbiased. The spread of the 1,000 slopes (0.162)
is also close to the standard error a single regression reported (0.153). The simulation therefore checks the
standard errors as well as the point estimates. [@moffatt_experimetrics] This is the
[sampling distribution](../02-inference/sampling-distributions-clt.qmd) made literal. Moffatt cites Kennedy
(1998), who urges Monte Carlo in teaching for exactly this reason. [@moffatt_experimetrics]

**A second study: what ignoring people's differences costs.** Experiments produce panels, many decisions per
subject, and subjects differ. Moffatt simulates 50 subjects making 20 binary choices each, with latent
propensity $y^*_{it} = -1 + 2x_{it} + u_i + \epsilon_{it}$, where $u_i$ is a subject effect with variance 1.
He then fits two models to each of 1,000 data sets: a **random-effects probit**, which is the correct model,
and a **pooled probit**, which treats all 1,000 choices as if they came from identical people. The
random-effects slope is centred on the true 2.0. The pooled slope is biased down by **around 30%**.
[@moffatt_experimetrics] Ignoring heterogeneity in a [probit](../03-regression/limited-dependent-variables.qmd)
does more than understate the standard errors, as it would in a linear model. It shrinks the coefficient
itself. The bottom-right panel of @fig-monte-carlo-pi re-runs this study, and the appendix shows where the
30% comes from.

**A third study: testing a test.** In his chapter on ordinal data, Moffatt proposes a Hausman test of whether
subjects report their *strength of preference* truthfully. It compares a binary probit on the choice with an
ordered probit that also uses the stated confidence. He then checks the test by simulation. Under the null
(everyone truthful, 1,000 replications of 1,050 observations), the simulated statistic's 95th percentile is
3.70 against the $\chi^2(1)$ value of 3.84, and its 99th is 6.18 against 6.63. The **size** is therefore close
to nominal, and a Q–Q plot against $\chi^2(1)$ hugs the 45° line. Under an alternative in which 30% of
subjects answer the confidence question at random, 71.4% of the statistics exceed 3.84. The test's
[**power**](../02-inference/statistical-power.qmd) against that alternative is 0.714.
[@moffatt_experimetrics] Repeat that over a grid of the share answering at random and you trace the test's
whole **power function**. [@moffatt_experimetrics]

## When the likelihood is itself an integral: maximum simulated likelihood {#sec-msl}

The random-effects probit raises a computing problem. Each subject has one unobserved $u_i$, shared by all of
their decisions, so their contribution to the [likelihood](../02-inference/maximum-likelihood.qmd) has to
**average over every value $u_i$ could take**. That is an integral, and it has no closed form.
[@moffatt_experimetrics] There are two ways to compute it: **quadrature** (a fixed grid of well-chosen points)
or **simulation**. Moffatt uses simulation, which gives the **method of maximum simulated likelihood (MSL)**.
[@moffatt_experimetrics]

The idea is the Monte Carlo integral again. An integral against a density is an expectation, so replace it by
an average over $R$ draws. [@moffatt_experimetrics] For each subject:

1. take $R$ draws of the random effect (Halton draws, transformed to normal and scaled by $\sigma_u$);
2. for each draw, compute the probability of the subject's *whole* sequence of choices, the product over
   their $T$ tasks;
3. average those $R$ probabilities. That average approximates the subject's likelihood. Log the averages and
   sum them over subjects.

Two details matter in practice. [@moffatt_experimetrics] First, the draws belong to the **subject**, not the
row, so all $T$ rows of a subject share the same $R$ draws. Second, the draws are made **once**, at the start,
and held fixed while the optimiser searches over $\beta$ and $\sigma_u$. Only the transformation $\sigma_u z$
changes from step to step. Moffatt also stores every non-integer variable in **double precision** (about 16
digits). Single precision carries only about 7, which can leave the simulated log-likelihood too rough for the
optimiser to find the maximum. [@moffatt_experimetrics]

As the check from reason 2 above, Moffatt simulates 60 subjects × 20 tasks with $\beta_1=-1$, $\beta_2=2$,
$\sigma_u=0.5$. He fits the model with his own MSL code (125 Halton draws) and with Stata's built-in
random-effects probit (12-point adaptive quadrature): [@moffatt_experimetrics]

| | $\hat\beta_2$ (slope) | $\hat\beta_1$ (intercept) | $\hat\sigma_u$ | log-likelihood |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| MSL, 125 Halton draws | 1.848 (0.147) | −0.909 (0.109) | 0.542 (0.068) | −710.28 |
| Quadrature, 12 points | 1.848 (0.147) | −0.918 (0.109) | 0.555 (0.070) | −710.42 |
| *True value* | *2.0* | *−1.0* | *0.5* | |

: Moffatt's §10.4.7 results; standard errors in brackets. {.striped}

The two methods agree to the second or third decimal place, which is the evidence that the hand-written code
is right. [@moffatt_experimetrics] The same draws then give each subject's **posterior random effect**: the
average of $u$ over the draws, weighted by how well each draw explains that subject's choices. Plotted against
the true $u_i$, the posteriors line up closely. That is a second check, and it lets you place individual
subjects. [@moffatt_experimetrics] MSL is the workhorse behind the rest of Moffatt's book: the panel
[hurdle model](../03-regression/hurdle-models.qmd), risky-choice models with continuous variation in risk
attitude, and altruism models with heterogeneous utility parameters. [@moffatt_experimetrics]

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def halton(n, base):                       # radical inverse: reverse the base-p digits of 1, 2, 3, ...
    out = np.empty(n)
    for k in range(n):
        f, r, i = 1.0, 0.0, k + 1
        while i > 0:
            f /= base; r += f * (i % base); i //= base
        out[k] = r
    return out

def Phi(z):                                # normal CDF (Abramowitz–Stegun 7.1.26, error < 1.5e-7)
    a = np.abs(z) / np.sqrt(2); t = 1 / (1 + 0.3275911 * a)
    poly = ((((1.061405429 * t - 1.453152027) * t + 1.421413741) * t - 0.284496736) * t + 0.254829592) * t
    return 0.5 * (1 + np.sign(z) * (1 - poly * np.exp(-a * a)))

def Phi_inv(p):                            # inverse normal CDF (Acklam's rational approximation)
    a = [-3.969683028665376e1, 2.209460984245205e2, -2.759285104469687e2, 1.383577518672690e2, -3.066479806614716e1, 2.506628277459239]
    b = [-5.447609879822406e1, 1.615858368580409e2, -1.556989798598866e2, 6.680131188771972e1, -1.328068155288572e1]
    c = [-7.784894002430293e-3, -3.223964580411365e-1, -2.400758277161838, -2.549732539343734, 4.374664141464968, 2.938163982698783]
    d = [7.784695709041462e-3, 3.224671290700398e-1, 2.445134137142996, 3.754408661907416]
    p = np.asarray(p, float); x = np.empty_like(p)
    tail = lambda q: ((((c[0]*q + c[1])*q + c[2])*q + c[3])*q + c[4])*q + c[5]
    den = lambda q: (((d[0]*q + d[1])*q + d[2])*q + d[3])*q + 1
    lo, hi = p < 0.02425, p > 0.97575; mid = ~(lo | hi)
    q = np.sqrt(-2 * np.log(p[lo])); x[lo] = tail(q) / den(q)
    q = np.sqrt(-2 * np.log(1 - p[hi])); x[hi] = -tail(q) / den(q)
    q = p[mid] - 0.5; r = q * q
    x[mid] = (((((a[0]*r + a[1])*r + a[2])*r + a[3])*r + a[4])*r + a[5]) * q / \
             (((((b[0]*r + b[1])*r + b[2])*r + b[3])*r + b[4])*r + 1)
    return x

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)

# --- pi: pseudo-random vs Halton -------------------------------------------------
N = 20000
pseudo = rng.random((N, 2))
hal = np.column_stack([halton(N, 2), halton(N, 3)])
grid = np.unique(np.round(np.logspace(1, np.log10(N), 25)).astype(int))
h_err = np.abs(4 * np.cumsum((hal**2).sum(1) <= 1)[grid - 1] / grid - np.pi)   # Halton: one fixed sequence
p_err = np.array([4 * np.cumsum((rng.random((N, 2))**2).sum(1) <= 1)[grid - 1] / grid - np.pi
                  for _ in range(200)])
p_rmse = np.sqrt((p_err**2).mean(axis=0))                                    # pseudo: typical error, 200 runs

# --- Monte Carlo study (Moffatt 9.4): pooled probit vs random-effects probit by MSL --
n, T, R, reps = 50, 20, 50, 300
x = rng.random(n * T)                      # x held fixed across replications, as in Moffatt
X = np.column_stack([np.ones(n * T), x])
z = Phi_inv(halton(n * R, 3)).reshape(n, R)  # R normal Halton draws per subject, fixed throughout

def pooled_probit(y):
    b = np.zeros(2); q = 2 * y - 1
    for _ in range(30):
        xb = X @ b
        lam = q * np.exp(-0.5 * xb**2) / np.sqrt(2 * np.pi) / np.clip(Phi(q * xb), 1e-12, 1)
        H = -(X * (lam * (lam + xb))[:, None]).T @ X
        step = np.linalg.solve(H, X.T @ lam); b -= step
        if np.abs(step).max() < 1e-8:
            break
    return b

def sim_loglik(th, y):                     # simulated log-likelihood: average over draws, per subject
    xb = (th[0] + th[1] * x).reshape(n, T); q = (2 * y - 1).reshape(n, T)
    P = Phi(q[:, :, None] * (xb[:, :, None] + np.exp(th[2]) * z[:, None, :]))
    return np.log(P.prod(axis=1).mean(axis=1)).sum()

def msl_probit(y, th):                     # Newton with numerical derivatives
    e, I = 1e-4, np.eye(3) * 1e-4
    for _ in range(40):
        g = np.array([(sim_loglik(th + I[i], y) - sim_loglik(th - I[i], y)) / (2 * e) for i in range(3)])
        H = np.array([[(sim_loglik(th + I[i] + I[j], y) - sim_loglik(th + I[i] - I[j], y)
                        - sim_loglik(th - I[i] + I[j], y) + sim_loglik(th - I[i] - I[j], y)) / (4 * e * e)
                       for j in range(3)] for i in range(3)])
        step = np.linalg.solve(H, g) if np.all(np.linalg.eigvalsh(H) < 0) else -0.1 * g
        th = th - step
        if np.abs(step).max() < 1e-6:
            break
    return th

pooled, re = [], []
for _ in range(reps):
    u = np.repeat(rng.standard_normal(n), T)
    y = ((-1 + 2 * x + u + rng.standard_normal(n * T)) > 0).astype(float)
    b = pooled_probit(y); pooled.append(b[1])
    re.append(msl_probit(y, np.array([b[0] * np.sqrt(2), b[1] * np.sqrt(2), 0.0]))[1])
pooled, re = np.array(pooled), np.array(re)

# --- draw ---------------------------------------------------------------------
fig, ax = plt.subplots(2, 2, figsize=(9.2, 8.4))
tt = np.linspace(0, np.pi / 2, 100)
for a, P, col, ttl in [(ax[0, 0], pseudo, "#161613", "1,000 pseudo-random points"),
                       (ax[0, 1], hal, "#e8531d", "1,000 Halton points (bases 2, 3)")]:
    a.scatter(P[:1000, 0], P[:1000, 1], s=4, color=col, alpha=0.7)
    a.plot(np.cos(tt), np.sin(tt), color="#5e5e54", lw=1.2)
    a.set_aspect("equal"); a.set_xlim(0, 1); a.set_ylim(0, 1); a.set_title(ttl)

a = ax[1, 0]
a.loglog(grid, p_rmse, "o-", color="#161613", ms=3, lw=1, label=f"pseudo-random, RMS error of 200 runs ({p_rmse[-1]:.4f})")
a.loglog(grid, h_err, "s-", color="#e8531d", ms=3, lw=1, label=f"Halton, one sequence ({h_err[-1]:.4f})")
a.loglog(grid, np.sqrt(np.pi * (4 - np.pi) / grid), color="#8a8a7e", ls="--", lw=1.2,
         label="theoretical s.d. of the random estimate (1/√n rate)")
a.set_xlabel("number of points n"); a.set_ylabel("|estimate − π|")
a.set_title("error of the π estimate"); a.legend(fontsize=7.5, loc="lower left")

a = ax[1, 1]
bins = np.linspace(0.8, 3.0, 45)
a.hist(pooled, bins=bins, color="#b7b6a8", edgecolor="#f4f3ec", label=f"pooled probit (mean {pooled.mean():.2f})")
a.hist(re, bins=bins, histtype="step", color="#161613", lw=1.6, label=f"RE probit by MSL (mean {re.mean():.2f})")
a.axvline(2.0, color="#161613", ls="--", lw=1.2, label="true slope 2")
a.axvline(2 / np.sqrt(2), color="#5e5e54", ls=":", lw=1.6, label="2/√2 ≈ 1.41")
a.set_ylim(0, a.get_ylim()[1] * 1.45)
a.set_xlabel("estimated slope, 300 replications"); a.set_ylabel("count")
a.set_title("Monte Carlo study: ignoring subject effects"); a.legend(fontsize=7.5, loc="upper right")
fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()

## Common pitfalls

::: {.callout-warning title="Where this goes wrong"}

- **Expecting fast convergence.** The error is only $O(1/\sqrt n)$: 100× the samples buys 10× the accuracy.
  Plan the sample size for the precision you need. [@ross_first_course]

- **Forgetting Monte Carlo error is itself random.** A simulation estimate has a [standard
  error](../02-inference/sampling-distributions-clt.qmd); report it (or use enough draws that it's
  negligible). [@ross_first_course]

- **Wasteful rejection sampling.** A poorly-matched proposal rejects almost everything; in high dimensions
  this fails badly — reach for [MCMC](../02-inference/credible-intervals-and-computation.qmd). [@lambert_bayes]

- **A bad random-number generator.** Garbage pseudo-randomness (correlations, short period) poisons every
  estimate; use a vetted generator and seed it for reproducibility.

- **Reading behaviour off simulated data.** Simulated data show how a *method* performs. They say nothing
  about how *people* behave, however realistic they are made to look. [@moffatt_experimetrics]

- **Pooling heterogeneous subjects in a nonlinear model.** In a linear model, ignoring subject effects mainly
  costs you honest standard errors. [@dongli_panel] In a probit it biases the coefficient itself, about 30% downward in
  Moffatt's design. Model the heterogeneity, for example with a
  [random effect](../03-regression/mixed-effects-models.qmd). [@moffatt_experimetrics]

- **Redrawing the simulation draws during estimation.** In MSL the draws are made once and held fixed while the
  optimiser searches. If they change between steps, the objective function changes under the optimiser's feet.
  Store everything in double precision too, or the simulated likelihood may be too rough to maximise.
  [@moffatt_experimetrics]

- **Trusting a test's nominal size and power.** A test's size and power are claims about its sampling
  distribution, and you can check them by simulation. Simulate under the null to check the size; simulate
  under a stated alternative to measure the power. [@moffatt_experimetrics]

:::

## Appendix · the mathematics {.unnumbered}

::: {.math-appendix}

**Why negative correlation helps (Moffatt §10.3.1).** For two draws,
$$
\operatorname{Var}\!\Big(\tfrac{t(\epsilon_1)+t(\epsilon_2)}{2}\Big)
=\tfrac14\Big(\operatorname{Var}t(\epsilon_1)+\operatorname{Var}t(\epsilon_2)+2\operatorname{Cov}\big(t(\epsilon_1),t(\epsilon_2)\big)\Big).
$$
With independent draws the covariance is zero and the variance is $\operatorname{Var}t(\epsilon)/2$. A negative
covariance pushes it below that. Antithetic pairs and Halton sequences both work through this term.

**The Halton point.** Write the index $k$ in base $p$, $k=\sum_j a_j p^j$, and reflect the digits about the
"decimal" point: $h_p(k)=\sum_j a_j p^{-(j+1)}$. For $p=2$: $k=1=1_2\to0.1_2=\tfrac12$; $k=2=10_2\to0.01_2=\tfrac14$;
$k=3=11_2\to0.11_2=\tfrac34$. In two dimensions, use a different prime per coordinate.

**Pooled probit under a neglected random effect.** If $y^*_{it}=x_{it}'\beta+u_i+\epsilon_{it}$ with
$u_i\sim N(0,\sigma_u^2)$ independent of $\epsilon_{it}\sim N(0,1)$, then $u_i+\epsilon_{it}\sim N(0,1+\sigma_u^2)$
and
$$
P(y_{it}=1\mid x_{it})=\Phi\!\Big(\frac{x_{it}'\beta}{\sqrt{1+\sigma_u^2}}\Big).
$$
The pooled probit consistently estimates $\beta/\sqrt{1+\sigma_u^2}$. With $\sigma_u^2=1$ that is
$\beta/\sqrt2$, a factor of 0.707 and so about 29% below $\beta$, which is Moffatt's "around 30%". The
coefficients shrink, but their ratios are unchanged.

**Random-effects probit likelihood (Moffatt eqs. 10.11, 10.14).** With $yy_{it}=2y_{it}-1$,
$$
\log L=\sum_{i=1}^n\ln\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\prod_{t=1}^T\Phi\big(yy_{it}(x_{it}'\beta+u)\big)\,
\frac1{\sigma_u}\phi\!\Big(\frac u{\sigma_u}\Big)\,du
\;\approx\;\sum_{i=1}^n\ln\Big[\frac1R\sum_{r=1}^R\prod_{t=1}^T\Phi\big(yy_{it}(x_{it}'\beta+\sigma_u z_{r,i})\big)\Big],
$$
where $z_{1,i},\dots,z_{R,i}$ are subject $i$'s fixed, normal-transformed Halton draws. Maximise the right-hand
side over $(\beta,\sigma_u)$; Moffatt parameterises $\ln\sigma_u$ to keep $\sigma_u>0$.

**Posterior random effect (eq. 10.12).** With $w_{r,i}=\prod_t\Phi\big(yy_{it}(x_{it}'\hat\beta+\hat\sigma_u z_{r,i})\big)$,
$$
\hat u_i=E(u_i\mid y_{i1},\dots,y_{iT})\approx\frac{\sum_r \hat\sigma_u z_{r,i}\,w_{r,i}}{\sum_r w_{r,i}}.
$$

**The Hausman statistic in the strength-of-preference test (§9.4).** For the single slope on $w$,
$H=(\hat b_{\text{probit}}-\hat b_{\text{oprobit}})^2/(\hat V_{\text{probit}}-\hat V_{\text{oprobit}})$, which
is $\chi^2(1)$ under the null. The ordered probit is efficient under the null but inconsistent if the
confidence reports are noise; the binary probit is consistent either way.
