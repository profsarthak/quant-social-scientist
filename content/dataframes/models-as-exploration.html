Models as a Tool for Exploration: Patterns, Residuals & Many Models

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source

A model splits a dataset into a pattern and what is left over. Take away the loud pattern and the quiet ones come into view. Do this for one model, then build it up step by step, then repeat it across a hundred groups at once.

A model can be used to look at data as well as to confirm a claim about it. Fit the strongest, most obvious pattern, take it away, and study what is left over (the residuals). That is how a puzzling dataset (“why do the worst diamonds cost the most?”) turns into an obvious one, and how a hundred countries’ worth of trends can be read on a single page.

Why it matters

Plot diamond price against cut, colour, or clarity in the diamonds data and you get a paradox: low-quality diamonds cost more. The poorest cuts, the yellowest colours, and the stones with inclusions visible to the naked eye all have higher typical prices than their flawless counterparts (Wickham and Grolemund 2017). Nobody believes a worse diamond is worth more. Something else is going on, and a plot alone cannot show what.

The something else is weight. Carat is by far the most important driver of price, and lower-quality diamonds tend to be bigger. Quality and size are tangled together, so the raw comparison mixes them up. In the language of the regression chapters this is a confounder, and the cure is the same one multiple regression uses: hold carat fixed and compare like with like. R for Data Science teaches the exploratory version of that cure, and it needs no inference at all: fit a model for the loud pattern, subtract it, and plot what is left (Wickham and Grolemund 2017).

This entry covers the modelling part of the dataframe craft. The workflow diagram puts “model” inside the transform ⇄ visualise ⇄ model loop, next to the other tools of exploratory visualisation. This is that third tool. It does not belong to the inference spine of Part III.

Exploring is not confirming

Before any model is fitted, Wickham and Grolemund draw a line that this whole encyclopedia cares about. Models are usually taught as tools for confirmation: is this hypothesis true? Here they are used for exploration: what is going on in this data? Both are legitimate. Mixing them up is not (Wickham and Grolemund 2017):

  • Each observation can be used for exploration or for confirmation, not both.
  • You may use an observation as often as you like to explore, but only once to confirm. Use it twice and you have quietly switched back to exploring.

The reason is the one behind the replication crisis and multiple testing. A hypothesis checked against the same data that suggested it will always look better than it is. If you are serious about confirming, the book suggests splitting the data before you start. Put 60% in a training (exploration) set, where anything goes. Put 20% in a query set, for comparing candidate models or plots by hand but never inside an automated search. Hold back 20% as a test set, to be used once, on the final model (Wickham and Grolemund 2017). This is the by-hand cousin of the train/validation/test logic of the predictive chapter. Even confirmatory work still needs exploratory analysis first: without it you are blind to the data’s quality problems.

What a model is: a family and a fitted member

A model has two parts (Wickham and Grolemund 2017):

  1. A family, a precise but generic pattern with free numbers in it: a straight line y = a_1 + a_2 * x, or a curve y = a_1 * x ^ a_2.
  2. A fitted model, the one member of that family that comes closest to your data, such as y = 4.22 + 2.05 * x.

Notice what “closest” does not say. The fitted model is the best of its family, but that does not make it a good model, let alone a true one. The book leans on George Box’s aphorism, “all models are wrong, but some are useful”, and its fuller context: the only question worth asking of a model is whether it is “illuminating and useful”. Asking whether it is true misses the point (Wickham and Grolemund 2017).

“Closest” needs a distance. The book builds it by hand on a 30-point simulated dataset (sim1). Take the vertical gap between each point and the line, square it, average, and take the square root: the root-mean-squared distance. Then search for the line that makes it smallest. Of 250 random lines, most are terrible. A grid search over intercept and slope narrows them down, and a numerical optimiser (optim()) finishes the job at intercept 4.22, slope 2.05. lm() gets exactly the same numbers in a single step, because for linear models geometry and linear algebra give the minimum directly. No search is needed, and the answer is guaranteed to be the global minimum (Wickham and Grolemund 2017). Seeing the search done by hand makes it clear that lm() is not magic: it is “find the closest member of the family” with a very fast way of finding it. (The OLS-in-matrix-form appendix is that one step written out.)

model1 <- function(a, data) a[1] + data$x * a[2]          # the family, as a function
measure_distance <- function(mod, data) {
  diff <- data$y - model1(mod, data)
  sqrt(mean(diff ^ 2))                                    # root-mean-squared distance
}
best <- optim(c(0, 0), measure_distance, data = sim1)     # search for the closest member
best$par                                                  #> 4.22 2.05
coef(lm(y ~ x, data = sim1))                              #> 4.22 2.05  — same answer, one step

Seeing a model through its predictions and residuals

The book prefers to understand a model by looking at what it does rather than by staring at its coefficients. Every predictive model makes predictions, so the same handful of tools works for all of them (Wickham and Grolemund 2017):

  • Predictions show the pattern the model captured. data_grid() builds an evenly spaced grid over the predictors; add_predictions() adds the model’s prediction to each row; you then plot the grid over the data. This works for any model, from a straight line to a spline.
  • Residuals show what the model missed. add_residuals() adds observed − predicted to the original data (you need the real y values, so the grid will not do). A residual plot that looks like random noise around zero means the model has captured the pattern. One that still has shape means it has not. With an intercept in the model, the residuals always average to zero.
grid <- sim1 |> data_grid(x) |> add_predictions(sim1_mod)   # the pattern
sim1 <- sim1 |> add_residuals(sim1_mod)                      # what's left over
ggplot(sim1, aes(x, resid)) + geom_ref_line(h = 0) + geom_point()

The same lens explains the parts of model-writing that trip people up (Wickham and Grolemund 2017):

  • Formulas are shorthand for a model matrix. y ~ x becomes y = a_1 + a_2 * x. model_matrix() shows the columns R actually builds, including the intercept as a column of ones (drop it with - 1).
  • A categorical predictor predicts each group’s mean. sex becomes a 0/1 column sexmale. There is no sexfemale column as well, because it would equal 1 − sexmale exactly and the family would have infinitely many equally close members (the dummy-variable trap). Asking for a prediction at a level the model never saw is an error, not a guess.
  • + versus *. y ~ x1 + x2 gives each group the same slope with different intercepts. y ~ x1 * x2 adds the interaction, so slopes differ too, and it automatically includes both main effects. Faceting the residuals by model and group shows which one left pattern behind.
  • y ~ x^2 + x is not a quadratic. Inside a formula ^ means interaction, so it collapses to y ~ x. Wrap arithmetic in I(): y ~ I(x^2) + x. When in doubt, check model_matrix().
  • Flexible curves fail beyond the data. poly() and natural splines (splines::ns()) bend a linear model into a curve. Outside the range of the data, polynomials shoot off to ±∞, and every model is guessing. Only theory can tell you what happens there (see functional form and splines & GAMs).
  • Missing values are dropped silently. Modelling functions drop any row with an NA. Setting options(na.action = na.warn) makes R say so, and nobs() reports how many rows were actually used (see missing values).

Model building, case 1: the diamond paradox

Back to the paradox. The book first makes two adjustments: it keeps diamonds of 2.5 carats or less (99.7% of the data), and it takes base-2 logs of both price and carat, which turns the carat–price curve into a straight line (Wickham and Grolemund 2017). Then it fits the loud pattern and keeps what is left over:

diamonds2 <- diamonds |>
  filter(carat <= 2.5) |>
  mutate(lprice = log2(price), lcarat = log2(carat))

mod_diamond <- lm(lprice ~ lcarat, data = diamonds2)        # the loud pattern: weight
diamonds2   <- diamonds2 |> add_residuals(mod_diamond, "lresid")

ggplot(diamonds2, aes(cut, lresid)) + geom_boxplot()        # redo the paradox plot on residuals

Re-plotted on the residuals, the paradox disappears. As quality rises, so does relative price, for cut, colour, and clarity alike (Wickham and Grolemund 2017). The log-2 scale makes the y-axis easy to read. A residual of −1 means half the price you would predict from weight alone; +1 means double.

The back-transformed prediction line also teaches something. The largest diamonds sit below it, and the likely reason is not geology: no diamond in this dataset costs more than $19,000, so the top of the price range is cut off. Fitting a model is often how you notice a feature of how the data were collected.

The build continues: put color, cut, and clarity into the model to make their effects explicit, and look at the new residuals. data_grid(cut, .model = mod_diamond2) fills in the variables you did not name with “typical” values (the median for continuous variables, the most common level for categorical ones), so each predictor can be plotted on its own. The last step is to look at the extremes one by one. Sixteen diamonds have |residual| > 1, meaning they are priced at more than double or less than half the model’s prediction. Some are 0.25-carat stones at $1,013–$1,186 against predictions under $300. The book’s verdict is not a test statistic. It asks whether this is a problem with the model, an error in the data, or a mispriced diamond worth buying (Wickham and Grolemund 2017).

The figure rebuilds the core move on a simulated stand-in, since the encyclopedia does not ship the diamonds data:

Show the figure code (simulated diamonds, one residual model) 
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(19)
n = 5000
grade = rng.integers(0, 5, n)                          # 0 = worst … 4 = best
lcarat = np.clip(rng.normal(-0.2 - 0.25 * grade, 0.55), -2.3, 1.3)   # worse grades are bigger
lprice = 12.0 + 1.7 * lcarat + 0.12 * grade + rng.normal(0, 0.3, n)  # log2 price
price = 2 ** lprice

b = np.polyfit(lcarat, lprice, 1)                       # the loud pattern: weight only
resid = lprice - np.polyval(b, lcarat)                  # what is left over

labels = ["worst", "2", "3", "4", "best"]
groups_p = [price[grade == g] for g in range(5)]
groups_r = [resid[grade == g] for g in range(5)]
box = dict(widths=0.55, showfliers=False, patch_artist=True,
           boxprops=dict(facecolor="#e4e3d8", edgecolor="#161613"),
           medianprops=dict(color="#161613", lw=1.6),
           whiskerprops=dict(color="#5e5e54"), capprops=dict(color="#5e5e54"))

fig, (axL, axR) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(9.6, 4.2))
axL.boxplot(groups_p, tick_labels=labels, **box)
axL.set_yscale("log", base=2)
axL.set_xlabel("quality grade")
axL.set_ylabel("price (log scale)")
axL.set_title("Raw: better stones look cheaper")

axR.boxplot(groups_r, tick_labels=labels, **box)
med = [np.median(r) for r in groups_r]
axR.plot(range(1, 6), med, color="#e8531d", lw=2.2, marker="o", zorder=5)
axR.axhline(0, color="#8a8a7e", lw=1.0, ls=":")
for k, m in enumerate(med):
    axR.annotate(f"×{2 ** m:.2f}", (k + 1, m), textcoords="offset points", xytext=(0, 9),
                 ha="center", fontsize=8.5, color="#e8531d")
axR.set_xlabel("quality grade")
axR.set_ylabel("residual from log₂ price ~ log₂ carat")
axR.set_title("Residuals: better stones cost more")
fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()

Model building, case 2: what affects the number of daily flights?

The second case shows that even a two-column table can hide several layers. Count the flights leaving New York each day in 2013 and you get 365 rows. The book peels them back one at a time (Wickham and Grolemund 2017):

  1. The loud pattern is day of the week. There are far fewer flights at weekends, because most air travel is for business, and Saturday is the lowest of all: you might fly on Sunday for a Monday meeting, but rarely on a Saturday. Fit lm(n ~ wday) and plot the residuals over the year.
  2. The residuals show three quieter patterns. First, the model fails on Saturdays from June onward: there are more Saturday flights than expected in summer and fewer in autumn. Second, there is a cluster of days with far fewer flights than expected (residual below −100). They include New Year’s Day, 4 July, Thanksgiving and Christmas, plus a few days that need a calendar to explain. Third, there is a smooth seasonal swell: fewer flights in January and December, more from May to September.
  3. Turn the Saturday pattern into a variable. Raw Saturday counts line up with New York’s school terms (summer break in 2013 ran 26 June – 9 September). Spring Saturdays are busier than autumn ones, which the author’s American friends put down to families saving holidays for Thanksgiving and Christmas. The book offers that as “a plausible working hypothesis”, not a finding. A term() function cuts the year into spring, summer and autumn.
  4. Fit day-of-week × term, then make it robust. lm(n ~ wday * term) helps less than hoped, because outlier days (the holidays) pull each group mean away from its typical value. Refitting with a robust regression, MASS::rlm(), which down-weights points far from the line, removes the day-of-week pattern cleanly and leaves the long-term trend and the genuine outliers in view.
  5. Cross-check with a different approach. Instead of building in what you know about school terms, give the data room to speak with a natural spline over the year: rlm(n ~ wday * ns(date, 5)). It finds the same strong Saturday pattern. Getting the same signal from different approaches is a good sign.

One small piece of craft from this case: when you are trying many models, wrap variable creation in a function (compute_vars()) or put the transformation in the formula itself. That way the same transformation is applied in every model and every plot, and cannot drift between them. This is writing functions applied to modelling.

Knowing when to stop. There is no test that says a model is finished. The book borrows a seamstress analogy from a Reddit user: a poor seamstress makes many mistakes, a good one works hard to fix them, and a great one is not afraid to throw the garment away and start again (Wickham and Grolemund 2017). The model only has to reveal a little more about the data than you knew before.

The book also places this approach honestly against the alternative. A machine-learning workflow that only optimises predictive accuracy is “totally reasonable”, but it tends to produce a black box. That makes it hard to use what you know about the world, and hard to judge whether the model will keep working “as fundamentals change”. For most real problems the authors expect a mix of both approaches (Wickham and Grolemund 2017). This is the interpretability debate in miniature.

Many models: one per country

The last idea scales the residual move from one model to a model per group. The gapminder data (popularised by Hans Rosling) records life expectancy for 142 countries, every five years from 1952, 1,704 rows in all. Plot every country’s line together and the overall rise in life expectancy hides everything else (Wickham and Grolemund 2017).

The fix is to fit the strong signal, a linear trend, separately for each country and study what each one misses. For New Zealand alone this is easy: one lm(lifeExp ~ year) gives R^2 = 0.954. Doing it for all 142 countries needs three ideas the book puts together (Wickham and Grolemund 2017):

  • Nesting. group_by(country, continent) |> nest() turns the table into one row per country, with a column data whose every cell is that country’s own 12-row data frame. In an ordinary grouped data frame each row is an observation. In a nested one, each row is a meta-observation: a country’s whole time course.
  • List-columns. A data frame is a named list of equal-length vectors, and a list is a vector, so a column can hold anything: data frames, or fitted models. mutate(model = map(data, country_model)) stores 142 models next to the data that produced them. The payoff is bookkeeping. When you filter or sort, the models move with their countries. With separate lists you would have to keep them in step by hand, and if you forgot, the code would still run and give the wrong answer.
  • Unnest and tidy. map2(data, model, add_residuals) then unnest() turns everything back into one ordinary table of residuals you can plot and facet. broom turns models into data: glance() gives one row of fit statistics per model, tidy() one row per coefficient, augment() one row per observation (see reading regressions in R).
by_country <- gapminder |>
  group_by(country, continent) |>
  nest() |>                                                   # one row per country
  mutate(model = map(data, \(df) lm(lifeExp ~ year, data = df)))

glance <- by_country |>
  mutate(glance = map(model, broom::glance)) |>
  unnest(glance)                                              # one row of fit stats per country

glance |> arrange(r.squared)                                  # the countries a trend can't describe

Sort the 142 models by R^2 and the worst fits are all in Africa: Rwanda 0.017, Botswana 0.034, Zimbabwe 0.056, Zambia 0.060, Swaziland 0.068, Lesotho 0.085 (Wickham and Grolemund 2017). Plot those countries’ raw data and two tragedies stand out: the HIV/AIDS epidemic and the Rwandan genocide. Here a model statistic is used as a search tool. A low R^2 is not evidence of a weak effect. It flags a country whose story a straight line cannot tell, which tells you exactly where to look.

Common pitfalls

WarningWhere this goes wrong
  • Selling exploration as confirmation. A pattern found by fitting, peeling and refitting has been tuned to the very data it came from. Report it as a hypothesis, or confirm it on data you held back and use only once. (Wickham and Grolemund 2017)
  • Taking residual plots as effect sizes. Residualise on carat, then plot by quality, and the carat-only slope has already absorbed part of quality’s effect. The direction shows, but the size is understated (in the figure, ×0.88 to ×1.13 across grades, against a true premium of about ×1.40 from worst to best). To estimate an effect, put both variables in one model; exploration’s job was to tell you to.
  • Reading the fitted model as the true one. It is the closest member of the family you chose; a different family could be closer, and none is “true”. (Wickham and Grolemund 2017)
  • Writing y ~ x^2 + x and getting a straight line. Use I(x^2) or poly(), and check with model_matrix(). (Wickham and Grolemund 2017)
  • Extrapolating a flexible curve. Polynomials and splines are only trustworthy inside the data’s range; beyond it, you need theory. (Wickham and Grolemund 2017)
  • Letting outliers set the means. Holiday flights dragged the day-of-week means away from typical days; a robust fit (MASS::rlm()) fixed it. Look at a model’s predictions over the raw data before trusting it. (Wickham and Grolemund 2017)
  • Keeping models and data in separate lists. Re-sort one and not the other, and the code runs but gives the wrong answer. Keep them together in a list-column. (Wickham and Grolemund 2017)
  • Reading a low R² as “no effect”. In the many-models workflow, a bad fit is a flag that something happened there, such as an epidemic or a genocide. (Wickham and Grolemund 2017)

Appendix · the mathematics

The distance being minimised. For a family f(x;\mathbf a) and data (x_i,y_i), i=1,\dots,n, the root-mean-squared distance is d(\mathbf a)=\sqrt{\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\big(y_i-f(x_i;\mathbf a)\big)^2}. Minimising d is the same as minimising the sum of squared residuals, because the square root and the 1/n are increasing transformations. For a linear family f=\mathbf x_i'\mathbf a the minimiser has the closed form \hat{\mathbf a}=(X'X)^{-1}X'\mathbf y (see the matrix-form appendix). That is why lm() needs no search while optim() does.

In plain words: “closest line” and “least squares” are the same thing; lm() just knows the answer’s formula.

Residuals average to zero. With an intercept, the first-order condition for a_1 is \sum_i (y_i-\hat y_i)=0, so \bar e=0. A categorical predictor’s fitted value is its group mean, because the mean is the constant that minimises squared distance within each group.

Reading log₂ residuals. If e_i=\log_2 y_i-\log_2\hat y_i, then y_i/\hat y_i=2^{e_i}. A residual of -1 is half the predicted price and +1 is double. In a log–log model \log_2 p=a_0+a_1\log_2 c, the slope a_1 is an elasticity: a 1% heavier stone is about a_1% dearer.

Why residualising on one variable understates the other. Suppose the truth is y=\beta_0+\beta_1x_1+\beta_2x_2+u, but you fit y on x_1 alone and then plot the residuals against x_2. The short-regression slope is \tilde\beta_1=\beta_1+\beta_2\delta, where \delta is the slope from regressing x_2 on x_1 (the omitted-variable-bias formula). The residual therefore carries x_2’s effect only through r_2, the part of x_2 that x_1 does not predict. Regressing the residual on x_2 gives the slope \beta_2\,\frac{\operatorname{Var}(r_2)}{\operatorname{Var}(x_2)}=\beta_2\big(1-R^2_{x_2\mid x_1}\big), which is smaller than \beta_2 whenever the two predictors are correlated. (In the figure’s simulation, R^2_{x_2\mid x_1}\approx0.28, so the visible gradient is about 0.086 log₂ units per grade against a true 0.12; the sample slope is 0.087.) To recover \beta_2 itself, also residualise x_2 on x_1 and regress residual on residual (Frisch–Waugh), or simply fit both together.

In plain words: the first model takes credit for whatever the two variables share, so the leftover pattern shows only the second variable’s unshared part.

R^2 as a search statistic. For each country’s trend, R^2=1-\sum_t e_t^2/\sum_t(y_t-\bar y)^2, the share of that country’s variation the straight line explains. Ranking 142 values of R^2 is a sort, not a test. No multiple-comparison correction applies because nothing is being confirmed.

A model can be used to *look* at data as well as to *confirm* a claim about it. Fit the strongest, most obvious pattern, take it away, and study what is left over (the **residuals**). That is how a puzzling dataset
("why do the *worst* diamonds cost the most?") turns into an obvious one, and how a hundred countries' worth of
trends can be read on a single page.

## Why it matters

Plot diamond price against cut, colour, or clarity in the `diamonds` data and you get a paradox: **low-quality
diamonds cost more**. The poorest cuts, the yellowest colours, and the stones with inclusions visible to the naked
eye all have *higher* typical prices than their flawless counterparts [@wickham_grolemund_r4ds_1e]. Nobody
believes a worse diamond is worth more. Something else is going on, and a plot alone cannot show what.

The something else is **weight**. Carat is by far the most important driver of price, and lower-quality diamonds
tend to be bigger. Quality and size are tangled together, so the raw comparison mixes them up. In the language of
the regression chapters this is a **confounder**, and the cure is the same one
[multiple regression](../03-regression/multiple-regression.qmd) uses: hold carat fixed and compare like with like.
*R for Data Science* teaches the exploratory version of that cure, and it needs no inference at all: **fit a model
for the loud pattern, subtract it, and plot what is left** [@wickham_grolemund_r4ds_1e].

This entry covers the modelling part of the dataframe craft. The [workflow diagram](the-data-science-workflow.qmd)
puts "model" inside the transform ⇄ visualise ⇄ model loop, next to the other tools of
[exploratory visualisation](exploratory-visualization.qmd). This is that third tool. It does not belong to the
inference spine of [Part III](../03-regression/index.qmd).

## Exploring is not confirming {#sec-explore-vs-confirm}

Before any model is fitted, Wickham and Grolemund draw a line that this whole encyclopedia cares about. Models
are usually taught as tools for **confirmation**: is this hypothesis true? Here they are used for
**exploration**: what is going on in this data? Both are legitimate. Mixing them up is not
[@wickham_grolemund_r4ds_1e]:

- **Each observation can be used for exploration or for confirmation, not both.**
- You may use an observation **as often as you like to explore**, but **only once to confirm**. Use it twice and
  you have quietly switched back to exploring.

The reason is the one behind the [replication crisis](../02-inference/replication-crisis.qmd) and
[multiple testing](../02-inference/multiple-testing.qmd). A hypothesis checked against the same data that
suggested it will always look better than it is. If you are serious about confirming, the book suggests splitting
the data before you start. Put **60%** in a *training* (exploration) set, where anything goes. Put **20%** in a
*query* set, for comparing candidate models or plots by hand but never inside an automated search. Hold back
**20%** as a *test* set, to be used **once**, on the final model [@wickham_grolemund_r4ds_1e]. This is the
by-hand cousin of the [train/validation/test logic](../04-predictive/cross-validation.qmd) of the predictive chapter.
Even confirmatory work still needs exploratory analysis first: without it you are blind to the data's quality
problems.

## What a model is: a family and a fitted member

A model has two parts [@wickham_grolemund_r4ds_1e]:

1. A **family**, a precise but generic pattern with free numbers in it: a straight line `y = a_1 + a_2 * x`, or a
   curve `y = a_1 * x ^ a_2`.
2. A **fitted model**, the one member of that family that comes **closest** to your data, such as `y = 4.22 + 2.05 * x`.

Notice what "closest" does *not* say. The fitted model is the best of its family, but that does not make it a *good* model, let alone a *true* one. The
book leans on George Box's aphorism, *"all models are wrong, but some are useful"*, and its fuller context: the
only question worth asking of a model is whether it is "illuminating and useful". Asking whether it is true misses the point
[@wickham_grolemund_r4ds_1e].

**"Closest" needs a distance.** The book builds it by hand on a 30-point simulated dataset (`sim1`). Take the
vertical gap between each point and the line, square it, average, and take the square root: the
**root-mean-squared distance**. Then search for the line that makes it smallest. Of 250 random lines, most are
terrible. A grid search over intercept and slope narrows them down, and a numerical optimiser (`optim()`) finishes
the job at intercept **4.22**, slope **2.05**. `lm()` gets **exactly the same numbers** in a single step, because
for linear models geometry and linear algebra give the minimum directly. No search is needed, and the answer is
guaranteed to be the global minimum [@wickham_grolemund_r4ds_1e]. Seeing the search done by hand makes it clear
that `lm()` is not magic: it is "find the closest member of the family" with a very fast way of finding it. (The
[OLS-in-matrix-form appendix](../../appendices/math/ols-in-matrix-form.qmd) is that one step written out.)

```r
model1 <- function(a, data) a[1] + data$x * a[2]          # the family, as a function
measure_distance <- function(mod, data) {
  diff <- data$y - model1(mod, data)
  sqrt(mean(diff ^ 2))                                    # root-mean-squared distance
}
best <- optim(c(0, 0), measure_distance, data = sim1)     # search for the closest member
best$par                                                  #> 4.22 2.05
coef(lm(y ~ x, data = sim1))                              #> 4.22 2.05  — same answer, one step
```

## Seeing a model through its predictions and residuals

The book prefers to understand a model by **looking at what it does** rather than by staring at its coefficients.
Every predictive model makes predictions, so the same handful of tools works for all of them
[@wickham_grolemund_r4ds_1e]:

- **Predictions show the pattern the model captured.** `data_grid()` builds an evenly spaced grid over the
  predictors; `add_predictions()` adds the model's prediction to each row; you then plot the grid over the data.
  This works for any model, from a straight line to a spline.
- **Residuals show what the model missed.** `add_residuals()` adds *observed − predicted* to the original data
  (you need the real *y* values, so the grid will not do). A residual plot that looks like random noise around zero
  means the model has captured the pattern. One that still has shape means it has not. With an intercept in the
  model, **the residuals always average to zero**.

```r
grid <- sim1 |> data_grid(x) |> add_predictions(sim1_mod)   # the pattern
sim1 <- sim1 |> add_residuals(sim1_mod)                      # what's left over
ggplot(sim1, aes(x, resid)) + geom_ref_line(h = 0) + geom_point()
```

The same lens explains the parts of model-writing that trip people up [@wickham_grolemund_r4ds_1e]:

- **Formulas are shorthand for a model matrix.** `y ~ x` becomes `y = a_1 + a_2 * x`. `model_matrix()` shows the
  columns R actually builds, including the intercept as a column of ones (drop it with `- 1`).
- **A categorical predictor predicts each group's mean.** `sex` becomes a 0/1 column `sexmale`. There is no
  `sexfemale` column as well, because it would equal `1 − sexmale` exactly and the family would have infinitely
  many equally close members (the [dummy-variable trap](../03-regression/functional-form.qmd)). Asking for a
  prediction at a level the model never saw is an error, not a guess.
- **`+` versus `*`.** `y ~ x1 + x2` gives each group the same slope with different intercepts. `y ~ x1 * x2` adds
  the interaction, so slopes differ too, and it automatically includes both main effects. Faceting the residuals
  by model and group shows which one left pattern behind.
- **`y ~ x^2 + x` is not a quadratic.** Inside a formula `^` means interaction, so it collapses to `y ~ x`. Wrap
  arithmetic in `I()`: `y ~ I(x^2) + x`. When in doubt, check `model_matrix()`.
- **Flexible curves fail beyond the data.** `poly()` and natural splines (`splines::ns()`) bend a linear model
  into a curve. Outside the range of the data, polynomials shoot off to ±∞, and *every* model is guessing. Only
  theory can tell you what happens there (see [functional form](../03-regression/functional-form.qmd) and
  [splines & GAMs](../ml-track/beyond-linearity-splines-and-gams.qmd)).
- **Missing values are dropped silently.** Modelling functions drop any row with an `NA`. Setting
  `options(na.action = na.warn)` makes R say so, and `nobs()` reports how many rows were actually used (see
  [missing values](missing-values.qmd)).

## Model building, case 1: the diamond paradox {#sec-diamonds}

Back to the paradox. The book first makes two adjustments: it keeps diamonds of **2.5 carats or less** (99.7% of the
data), and it takes **base-2 logs** of both price and carat, which turns the carat–price curve into a straight
line [@wickham_grolemund_r4ds_1e]. Then it fits the loud pattern and keeps what is left over:

```r
diamonds2 <- diamonds |>
  filter(carat <= 2.5) |>
  mutate(lprice = log2(price), lcarat = log2(carat))

mod_diamond <- lm(lprice ~ lcarat, data = diamonds2)        # the loud pattern: weight
diamonds2   <- diamonds2 |> add_residuals(mod_diamond, "lresid")

ggplot(diamonds2, aes(cut, lresid)) + geom_boxplot()        # redo the paradox plot on residuals
```

Re-plotted on the residuals, the paradox disappears. **As quality rises, so does relative price**, for cut,
colour, and clarity alike [@wickham_grolemund_r4ds_1e]. The log-2 scale makes the y-axis easy to read. A residual of
**−1** means *half* the price you would predict from weight alone; **+1** means *double*.

The back-transformed prediction line also teaches something. The largest diamonds sit *below* it, and the likely
reason is not geology: **no diamond in this dataset costs more than \$19,000**, so the top of the price range is
cut off. Fitting a model is often how you notice a feature of how the data were collected.

The build continues: put `color`, `cut`, and `clarity` into the model to make their effects explicit, and look at
the new residuals. `data_grid(cut, .model = mod_diamond2)` fills in the variables you did not name with
"typical" values (the median for continuous variables, the most common level for categorical ones), so each
predictor can be plotted on its own. The last step is to **look at the extremes one by one**. Sixteen diamonds
have |residual| > 1, meaning they are priced at more than double or less than half the model's prediction. Some
are 0.25-carat stones at \$1,013–\$1,186 against predictions under \$300. The book's verdict is not a test
statistic. It asks whether this is a problem with the model, an error in the data, or a mispriced diamond worth
buying [@wickham_grolemund_r4ds_1e].

The figure rebuilds the core move on a simulated stand-in, since the encyclopedia does not ship the `diamonds` data:

```{python}
#| label: fig-residual-paradox
#| fig-cap: "The residual move, on a simulated stand-in for R for Data Science's diamonds paradox (5,000 stones, five quality grades; worse grades are bigger on average, while quality itself adds a modest premium). Left: raw prices fall as quality rises, the paradox. Right: fit log₂ price on log₂ carat alone, take the residuals, and plot those by grade. The orange line joins the median residuals, and the expected order appears: each step up in quality buys a few percent more than weight predicts. The residuals understate the true premium a little, because the carat-only slope (≈1.56) has absorbed part of the quality effect (true slope 1.70). Exploration shows the direction; a joint model is what estimates the size."
#| code-fold: true
#| code-summary: "Show the figure code (simulated diamonds, one residual model)"

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(19)
n = 5000
grade = rng.integers(0, 5, n)                          # 0 = worst … 4 = best
lcarat = np.clip(rng.normal(-0.2 - 0.25 * grade, 0.55), -2.3, 1.3)   # worse grades are bigger
lprice = 12.0 + 1.7 * lcarat + 0.12 * grade + rng.normal(0, 0.3, n)  # log2 price
price = 2 ** lprice

b = np.polyfit(lcarat, lprice, 1)                       # the loud pattern: weight only
resid = lprice - np.polyval(b, lcarat)                  # what is left over

labels = ["worst", "2", "3", "4", "best"]
groups_p = [price[grade == g] for g in range(5)]
groups_r = [resid[grade == g] for g in range(5)]
box = dict(widths=0.55, showfliers=False, patch_artist=True,
           boxprops=dict(facecolor="#e4e3d8", edgecolor="#161613"),
           medianprops=dict(color="#161613", lw=1.6),
           whiskerprops=dict(color="#5e5e54"), capprops=dict(color="#5e5e54"))

fig, (axL, axR) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(9.6, 4.2))
axL.boxplot(groups_p, tick_labels=labels, **box)
axL.set_yscale("log", base=2)
axL.set_xlabel("quality grade")
axL.set_ylabel("price (log scale)")
axL.set_title("Raw: better stones look cheaper")

axR.boxplot(groups_r, tick_labels=labels, **box)
med = [np.median(r) for r in groups_r]
axR.plot(range(1, 6), med, color="#e8531d", lw=2.2, marker="o", zorder=5)
axR.axhline(0, color="#8a8a7e", lw=1.0, ls=":")
for k, m in enumerate(med):
    axR.annotate(f"×{2 ** m:.2f}", (k + 1, m), textcoords="offset points", xytext=(0, 9),
                 ha="center", fontsize=8.5, color="#e8531d")
axR.set_xlabel("quality grade")
axR.set_ylabel("residual from log₂ price ~ log₂ carat")
axR.set_title("Residuals: better stones cost more")
fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

## Model building, case 2: what affects the number of daily flights?

The second case shows that even a two-column table can hide several layers. Count the flights leaving New York
each day in 2013 and you get **365 rows**. The book peels them back one at a time
[@wickham_grolemund_r4ds_1e]:

1. **The loud pattern is day of the week.** There are far fewer flights at weekends, because most air travel is
   for business, and Saturday is the lowest of all: you might fly on Sunday for a Monday meeting, but rarely on a
   Saturday. Fit `lm(n ~ wday)` and plot the residuals over the year.
2. **The residuals show three quieter patterns.** First, the model **fails on Saturdays from June onward**:
   there are more Saturday flights than expected in summer and fewer in autumn. Second, there is a cluster of days
   with **far fewer flights than expected** (residual below −100). They include New Year's Day, 4 July,
   Thanksgiving and Christmas, plus a few days that need a calendar to explain. Third, there is a **smooth
   seasonal swell**: fewer flights in January and December, more from May to September.
3. **Turn the Saturday pattern into a variable.** Raw Saturday counts line up with New York's **school terms**
   (summer break in 2013 ran 26 June – 9 September). Spring Saturdays are busier than autumn ones, which the
   author's American friends put down to families saving holidays for Thanksgiving and Christmas. The book offers
   that as *"a plausible working hypothesis"*, not a finding. A `term()` function cuts the year into spring,
   summer and autumn.
4. **Fit day-of-week × term, then make it robust.** `lm(n ~ wday * term)` helps less than hoped, because outlier
   days (the holidays) pull each group *mean* away from its typical value. Refitting with a robust regression,
   `MASS::rlm()`, which down-weights points far from the line, removes the day-of-week pattern cleanly and leaves
   the long-term trend and the genuine outliers in view.
5. **Cross-check with a different approach.** Instead of building in what you know about school terms, give the
   data room to speak with a natural spline over the year: `rlm(n ~ wday * ns(date, 5))`. It finds the same
   strong Saturday pattern. *Getting the same signal from different approaches is a good sign.*

One small piece of craft from this case: when you are trying many models, wrap variable creation in a function
(`compute_vars()`) or put the transformation in the formula itself. That way the same transformation is applied
in every model and every plot, and cannot drift between them. This is [writing functions](writing-functions.qmd)
applied to modelling.

**Knowing when to stop.** There is no test that says a model is finished. The book borrows a seamstress analogy
from a Reddit user: a poor seamstress makes many mistakes, a good one works hard to fix them, and a great one is
not afraid to throw the garment away and start again [@wickham_grolemund_r4ds_1e]. The model only has to reveal a
little more about the data than you knew before.

The book also places this approach honestly against the alternative. A machine-learning workflow that only
optimises predictive accuracy is "totally reasonable", but it tends to produce a black box. That makes it hard to
use what you know about the world, and hard to judge whether the model will keep working "as fundamentals
change". For most real problems the authors expect a mix of both approaches [@wickham_grolemund_r4ds_1e]. This is
the [interpretability debate](../04-predictive/interpretability-and-ml.qmd) in miniature.

## Many models: one per country {#sec-many-models}

The last idea scales the residual move from one model to **a model per group**. The `gapminder` data (popularised
by Hans Rosling) records life expectancy for **142 countries**, every five years from 1952, **1,704 rows** in all.
Plot every country's line together and the overall rise in life expectancy hides everything else
[@wickham_grolemund_r4ds_1e].

The fix is to fit the strong signal, a **linear trend**, separately for each country and study what each one
misses. For New Zealand alone this is easy: one `lm(lifeExp ~ year)` gives $R^2 = 0.954$. Doing it for all 142
countries needs three ideas the book puts together [@wickham_grolemund_r4ds_1e]:

- **Nesting.** `group_by(country, continent) |> nest()` turns the table into **one row per country**, with a
  column `data` whose every cell is that country's own 12-row data frame. In an ordinary grouped data frame each
  row is an observation. In a nested one, each row is a **meta-observation**: a country's whole time course.
- **List-columns.** A data frame is a named list of equal-length vectors, and a list is a vector, so a column can
  hold anything: data frames, or fitted models. `mutate(model = map(data, country_model))` stores 142 models
  *next to the data that produced them*. The payoff is bookkeeping. When you filter or sort, the models move
  with their countries. With separate lists you would have to keep them in step by hand, and if you forgot, the
  code would still run and give the wrong answer.
- **Unnest and tidy.** `map2(data, model, add_residuals)` then `unnest()` turns everything back into one ordinary
  table of residuals you can plot and facet. **broom** turns models into data: `glance()` gives one row of fit
  statistics per model, `tidy()` one row per coefficient, `augment()` one row per observation (see
  [reading regressions in R](../../appendices/computing/regressions-in-r.qmd#sec-summary)).

```r
by_country <- gapminder |>
  group_by(country, continent) |>
  nest() |>                                                   # one row per country
  mutate(model = map(data, \(df) lm(lifeExp ~ year, data = df)))

glance <- by_country |>
  mutate(glance = map(model, broom::glance)) |>
  unnest(glance)                                              # one row of fit stats per country

glance |> arrange(r.squared)                                  # the countries a trend can't describe
```

Sort the 142 models by $R^2$ and the worst fits are **all in Africa**: Rwanda **0.017**, Botswana **0.034**,
Zimbabwe **0.056**, Zambia **0.060**, Swaziland **0.068**, Lesotho **0.085** [@wickham_grolemund_r4ds_1e]. Plot
those countries' raw data and two tragedies stand out: the **HIV/AIDS epidemic** and the **Rwandan genocide**.
Here a model statistic is used as a *search tool*. A low $R^2$ is not evidence of a weak effect. It flags a
country whose story a straight line cannot tell, which tells you exactly where to look.

## Common pitfalls

::: {.callout-warning title="Where this goes wrong"}
- **Selling exploration as confirmation.** A pattern found by fitting, peeling and refitting has been
  tuned to the very data it came from. Report it as a hypothesis, or confirm it on data you held back and use only
  once. [@wickham_grolemund_r4ds_1e]
- **Taking residual plots as effect sizes.** Residualise on carat, then plot by quality, and the carat-only
  slope has already absorbed part of quality's effect. The direction shows, but the size is understated (in the figure,
  ×0.88 to ×1.13 across grades, against a true premium of about ×1.40 from worst to best). To estimate an effect,
  put both variables in [one model](../03-regression/multiple-regression.qmd); exploration's job was to tell you to.
- **Reading the fitted model as the true one.** It is the closest member of the family you chose; a different
  family could be closer, and none is "true". [@wickham_grolemund_r4ds_1e]
- **Writing `y ~ x^2 + x` and getting a straight line.** Use `I(x^2)` or `poly()`, and check with
  `model_matrix()`. [@wickham_grolemund_r4ds_1e]
- **Extrapolating a flexible curve.** Polynomials and splines are only trustworthy inside the data's range;
  beyond it, you need theory. [@wickham_grolemund_r4ds_1e]
- **Letting outliers set the means.** Holiday flights dragged the day-of-week means away from typical days;
  a robust fit (`MASS::rlm()`) fixed it. Look at a model's predictions over the raw data before trusting it.
  [@wickham_grolemund_r4ds_1e]
- **Keeping models and data in separate lists.** Re-sort one and not the other, and the code runs but gives the
  wrong answer. Keep them together in a list-column. [@wickham_grolemund_r4ds_1e]
- **Reading a low R² as "no effect".** In the many-models workflow, a bad fit is a flag that something
  happened there, such as an epidemic or a genocide. [@wickham_grolemund_r4ds_1e]
:::

## Appendix · the mathematics {.unnumbered}

::: {.math-appendix}

**The distance being minimised.** For a family $f(x;\mathbf a)$ and data $(x_i,y_i)$, $i=1,\dots,n$, the
root-mean-squared distance is
$$
d(\mathbf a)=\sqrt{\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\big(y_i-f(x_i;\mathbf a)\big)^2}.
$$
Minimising $d$ is the same as minimising the sum of squared residuals, because the square root and the $1/n$ are
increasing transformations. For a linear family $f=\mathbf x_i'\mathbf a$ the minimiser has the closed form
$\hat{\mathbf a}=(X'X)^{-1}X'\mathbf y$ (see the [matrix-form appendix](../../appendices/math/ols-in-matrix-form.qmd)).
That is why `lm()` needs no search while `optim()` does.

> **In plain words:** "closest line" and "least squares" are the same thing; `lm()` just knows the answer's formula.

**Residuals average to zero.** With an intercept, the first-order condition for $a_1$ is
$\sum_i (y_i-\hat y_i)=0$, so $\bar e=0$. A categorical predictor's fitted value is its group mean, because the
mean is the constant that minimises squared distance within each group.

**Reading log₂ residuals.** If $e_i=\log_2 y_i-\log_2\hat y_i$, then $y_i/\hat y_i=2^{e_i}$. A residual of $-1$ is
half the predicted price and $+1$ is double. In a log–log model $\log_2 p=a_0+a_1\log_2 c$, the slope $a_1$ is an
**elasticity**: a 1% heavier stone is about $a_1$% dearer.

**Why residualising on one variable understates the other.** Suppose the truth is
$y=\beta_0+\beta_1x_1+\beta_2x_2+u$, but you fit $y$ on $x_1$ alone and then plot the residuals against $x_2$.
The short-regression slope is $\tilde\beta_1=\beta_1+\beta_2\delta$, where $\delta$ is the slope from
regressing $x_2$ on $x_1$ (the [omitted-variable-bias](../03-regression/multiple-regression.qmd) formula).
The residual therefore carries $x_2$'s effect only through $r_2$, the part of $x_2$ that $x_1$ does not predict.
Regressing the residual on $x_2$ gives the slope
$$
\beta_2\,\frac{\operatorname{Var}(r_2)}{\operatorname{Var}(x_2)}=\beta_2\big(1-R^2_{x_2\mid x_1}\big),
$$
which is smaller than $\beta_2$ whenever the two predictors are correlated. (In the figure's simulation,
$R^2_{x_2\mid x_1}\approx0.28$, so the visible gradient is about 0.086 log₂ units per grade against a true 0.12;
the sample slope is 0.087.) To recover $\beta_2$ itself, also residualise $x_2$ on $x_1$ and regress residual on
residual (Frisch–Waugh), or simply fit both together.

> **In plain words:** the first model takes credit for whatever the two variables share, so the leftover pattern
> shows only the second variable's unshared part.

**$R^2$ as a search statistic.** For each country's trend, $R^2=1-\sum_t e_t^2/\sum_t(y_t-\bar y)^2$, the share of
that country's variation the straight line explains. Ranking 142 values of $R^2$ is a sort, not a test. No
multiple-comparison correction applies because nothing is being confirmed.

:::
