Machine Learning with Clustered Data

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source

Children in schools, waves within people, employees within teams. Data that come in groups break the assumption that every row is a fresh, independent case, and that assumption is built into both a standard tree and a standard cross-validation. This entry shows how trees and the lasso are combined with mixed-effects models, and how to cross-validate when rows share a person.

When observations come in clusters (repeated waves of the same child, pupils in the same school), a machine-learning method has to know about the grouping twice: in the model, by growing trees or running the lasso inside a mixed-effects model rather than on bare rows, and in the evaluation, by holding out whole clusters (grouped cross-validation) whenever the question is about new people rather than new rows from people the model has already seen.

Why it matters

Every method earlier in this track treats a dataset as a pile of independent rows. Trees split rows, the lasso weighs rows, and k-fold cross-validation shuffles rows into folds. Much social-science data does not look like that. Jacobucci, Grimm and Zhang list the usual cases: children within schools, repeated measures within people, employees within work units (Jacobucci et al. 2023). For a theory-driven model the answer is a mixed-effects (multilevel) model, which separates the between-cluster effect from the within-cluster one, gets the standard errors right and copes with clusters of different sizes (Jacobucci et al. 2023). The same dependence causes trouble for machine learning in two places. Predictions are harder to generate and models are harder to evaluate, and ignoring the clustering can give biased results, especially when the data mix person-level and occasion-level variables and cluster sizes differ (Jacobucci et al. 2023).

This entry is about the second wave of methods that fix this: mixed-effects trees and regularised mixed-effects models. It follows Jacobucci and colleagues’ longitudinal example: reading scores of kindergarten children from the ECLS-K, followed over the first two years of school (Jacobucci et al. 2023).

The model the methods are built around

A mixed-effects model for that kind of data gives each child their own line. Jacobucci’s reading model says that child i’s score at time t is a starting level plus a rate of growth, and both vary across children around a population average (Jacobucci et al. 2023):

  • Fixed effects are the population averages: the typical starting score and the typical yearly gain.
  • Random effects are each child’s departures from those averages, assumed to come from a normal distribution whose variances (and correlation) are estimated.

Fitted to the ECLS-K reading scores, the averages were a starting score of −0.89 in the autumn of kindergarten and a gain of 1.03 points a year. Children differed with a standard deviation of 0.52 in where they started and 0.16 in how fast they grew, and the two were negatively correlated (−0.56): children who started lower tended to grow faster. The occasion-to-occasion noise had a standard deviation of 0.24 (Jacobucci et al. 2023). The authors point out that the average yearly gain is about two standard deviations of the differences between children at the start, so a lot happens in two years (Jacobucci et al. 2023).

The question for machine learning is: which of the things we knew about each child at the start of kindergarten explain why some children start higher, or grow faster? There were many candidate predictors (parents’ education, poverty, gender, a parent rating of the child’s “approaches to learning”) and no strong theory saying which interactions to expect, which is exactly where trees and the lasso earn their keep. See ML for social science for that division of labour.

Trees that grow a mixed-effects model in every leaf

An ordinary regression tree splits the rows so that the outcome is as similar as possible within each leaf, and predicts with the leaf’s mean. A mixed-effects tree replaces “the leaf’s mean” with a whole mixed-effects model per leaf. The first such method, by Abdolell and colleagues in 2002, works like this (Jacobucci et al. 2023):

  1. Fit the mixed-effects model to everyone and record its fit, the deviance or -2\log L (lower is better).
  2. For every candidate predictor and every cut-point, split the children into two groups, fit the model separately in each and add the two deviances.
  3. Keep the split that lowers the deviance most, if the improvement beats a threshold k. Otherwise stop.
  4. Repeat inside each new group.

Because it splits on child-level variables, all of a child’s waves always stay together, and each leaf ends up as a group of children with its own growth curve. Jacobucci and colleagues suggest setting k either as a fixed number, as a fraction of the starting deviance (say 1%), or at the very least at the 95th percentile of a \chi^2 with as many degrees of freedom as the split adds parameters (Jacobucci et al. 2023).

Reading a mixed-effects tree

Run on the ECLS-K reading data with the longRPart2 package, each split had to cut the deviance by about 74 units. That is a demanding bar for one split, the authors note, since each split adds six parameters (Jacobucci et al. 2023). The tree split first on father’s education, which cut the deviance from 33,056.92 to 31,496.00 (15,662.16 + 15,833.84), an improvement of 1,560.92. Further splits used poverty status, mother’s education (twice), approaches to learning and gender, ending in seven leaves (Jacobucci et al. 2023). The left panel of the figure lays out how much each split bought.

What differs between the leaves is mostly where children start: the leaves’ average starting scores run from −1.31 to −0.60, while their average yearly gains stay between 0.97 and 1.10 (Jacobucci et al. 2023). The tree, in other words, mainly says that family background predicts the level children arrive at school with, not how fast they then learn to read. The leaves also differ in their random-effect structure. In two leaves (children of less-educated fathers who were below the poverty line or whose mothers had little education) a child’s starting level and growth were almost uncorrelated, while in the other five the correlation was clearly negative, from −0.38 to −0.69 (Jacobucci et al. 2023). The package’s variable-importance measure ranked mother’s and father’s education highest (about 20% each), well ahead of poverty (about 10%), gender and approaches to learning (about 5% each) (Jacobucci et al. 2023).

Two families of mixed-effects tree

The approaches differ in what is allowed to vary between leaves (Jacobucci et al. 2023):

Family What each leaf gets Packages Strength
Separate model per leaf (Abdolell et al. 2002) its own fixed effects and its own random-effect variances longRPart / longRPart2 flexible; finds groups whose variability differs
One model, leaves as dummies (RE-EM tree, Hajjem et al. 2011; Sela & Simonoff 2012; GLMM tree, Fokkema et al. 2018) its own fixed effects; one set of random-effect variances for everybody REEMtree, glmertree better for prediction; can split on occasion-level variables too

The RE-EM tree (random effects via the EM algorithm) alternates between fitting a tree to the outcome with the current random effects taken out, and re-estimating the random effects given the tree. Because the tree enters the mixed-effects model only as a set of indicator variables, there is a single set of random-effect parameters for all leaves, and the tree can split on occasion-level variables, so the same child’s waves can land in different leaves (Jacobucci et al. 2023). It has been shown to predict better than the separate-model approach, at the cost of less interpretable structure (Jacobucci et al. 2023). Later variants fix specific weaknesses (Jacobucci et al. 2023):

  • Fu and Simonoff (2015) replaced the CART splitting step with conditional inference trees, removing CART’s bias towards variables with many distinct values.
  • The MELT algorithm (Eo and Cho 2014) splits on child-level variables to find groups whose slopes differ, useful when the question is what moderates a within-person association.
  • The GLMM tree of Fokkema and colleagues allows non-normal outcomes (counts, binary) and model-based partitioning, holding the random-effect parameters the same across leaves like the RE-EM tree.
  • Stegmann and colleagues extended the separate-model approach to nonlinear mixed-effects models (such as exponential growth), and Serang and colleagues linked CART to Mplus (MplusTrees) for multilevel models with several outcomes.

On the ECLS-K data, glmertree’s first splits closely followed longRPart2’s (father’s education, poverty, mother’s education), again with seven leaves whose starting levels ran from −1.29 to −0.57 and whose growth rates barely moved (0.97 to 1.09) (Jacobucci et al. 2023). It used a tiny significance level (10^{-16}) to stop the very large sample producing endless splits. Its single set of random effects was smaller than the full-sample model’s (intercept SD 0.47, slope SD 0.15), as it should be once the leaves have absorbed part of the differences between children (Jacobucci et al. 2023). REEMtree, which was given time as a candidate splitting variable and fitted a random intercept only, split on time first: its first three splits simply separated the four assessment waves. Only then did it use father’s education, always at the same cut between “some college” and “a bachelor’s degree” (Jacobucci et al. 2023). The lesson is the authors’ own: these specifications produce different trees and different conclusions, the best one depends on the data, and it is wise to run more than one (Jacobucci et al. 2023).

The lasso inside a mixed-effects model

The second tool is regularisation. A lasso mixed-effects model minimises the model’s deviance plus a penalty \lambda times the sum of the absolute fixed effects, so that as \lambda grows, weak predictors are set exactly to zero (Jacobucci et al. 2023). Some versions penalise the random effects instead, to decide which slopes need to vary across people at all (Jacobucci et al. 2023).

The ECLS-K analysis used it in two stages, following Grimm and colleagues (2022) (Jacobucci et al. 2023). First the standardised predictors of children’s starting level were penalised, and \lambda chosen. Then, with the survivors kept in, the predictors of children’s growth were penalised. To choose \lambda the authors used a “BIC-500”: the Bayesian information criterion recomputed as if the sample had 500 children. The ordinary BIC’s penalty per parameter is too lenient with a sample as large as the ECLS-K (Jacobucci et al. 2023).

The result was blunt. For starting level, the chosen penalty kept only father’s education and poverty status. For growth, it kept nothing. The authors point out that this is common, because there is much less power to detect effects on random slopes than on random intercepts, so one may want a different criterion for each (Jacobucci et al. 2023). They then refitted an ordinary mixed-effects model with just those two predictors (the relaxed lasso, Meinshausen 2007), so that the estimates are on the variables’ natural scales and not shrunk. Each extra level of father’s education raised the expected starting score by 0.08, and children above the poverty line started 0.24 higher (Jacobucci et al. 2023). The tree and the lasso agree: in these data, family background shapes the starting line, not the pace.

Cross-validating when rows share a person

Model selection is the unsolved part. Jacobucci and colleagues note that cross-validation becomes very slow when every fit is a mixed-effects model, so researchers fall back on fit indices (BIC, a deviance threshold), and the choice of index and cut-off “remains fairly arbitrary” (Jacobucci et al. 2023). When you do cross-validate, there is a further decision that ordinary k-fold hides. Longitudinal data are stored in “long” form, one row per child per wave, and assigning random rows to folds leaves every child’s other waves in the training data. The alternatives they point to are stratified CV, which balances each person’s time points across folds, and grouped CV, which puts whole people into folds, so 20% of the participants are held out in each round of 5-fold CV (Jacobucci et al. 2023).

Which one is right depends on the question you will use the model for:

  • Predict a missed wave for a child we already follow → hold out rows.
  • Predict a child who is not yet in the data (a new cohort, a new school) → hold out children.

Use row-wise folds for the second question and the model gets credit for knowing the held-out child’s other waves, knowledge it will not have when it meets a new child. The right panel of the figure shows how large that flattery can be.

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# ---- left: deviance improvements of the longRPart2 tree (Jacobucci et al., Algorithm 3) ----
splits = [("father's educ.", 33056.92, 15662.16, 15833.84),
          ("poverty", 15662.16, 3114.981, 12125.79),
          ("mother's educ.\n(< 6)", 15833.84, 8345.322, 7113.559),
          ("mother's educ.\n(< 3)", 12125.79, 1533.931, 10419.97),
          ("gender", 10419.97, 5603.362, 4702.098),
          ("approaches\nto learning", 8345.322, 3675.407, 4579.168)]
labels = [s[0] for s in splits]
gain = np.array([parent - a - b for _, parent, a, b in splits])

# ---- right: row-wise vs grouped CV for a mixed-effects model (simulation, ours) ----
rng = np.random.default_rng(11)
T = np.array([0.0, 0.55, 1.0, 1.55])                      # four waves (years), ours
Psi = np.array([[0.47**2, -0.53*0.47*0.16], [-0.53*0.47*0.16, 0.16**2]])
sig = 0.24

def simulate(n):
    daded = rng.integers(1, 10, n); poor = rng.integers(1, 3, n)
    u = rng.multivariate_normal([0, 0], Psi, n)
    a = -1.39 + 0.08*(daded - 1) + 0.24*(poor - 1) + u[:, 0]
    b = 1.03 + u[:, 1]
    child = np.repeat(np.arange(n), 4); t = np.tile(T, n)
    y = a[child] + b[child]*t + rng.normal(0, sig, 4*n)
    X = np.column_stack([np.ones(4*n), daded[child] - 1, poor[child] - 1, t])
    return X, y, child

def fit(X, y, child):
    beta = np.linalg.lstsq(X, y, rcond=None)[0]           # fixed part
    r = y - X @ beta
    lines, covs, ss, df = [], [], 0.0, 0
    for c in np.unique(child):                            # moment estimates of the variance components
        m = child == c
        if m.sum() >= 3:
            Z = np.column_stack([np.ones(m.sum()), X[m, 3]])
            g = np.linalg.lstsq(Z, r[m], rcond=None)[0]
            e = r[m] - Z @ g; ss += e @ e; df += m.sum() - 2
            lines.append(g); covs.append(np.linalg.inv(Z.T @ Z))
    s2 = ss / df
    P = np.cov(np.array(lines).T) - s2 * np.mean(covs, axis=0)
    blup = {}
    for c in np.unique(child):                            # each child's shrunken intercept and slope
        m = child == c
        Z = np.column_stack([np.ones(m.sum()), X[m, 3]])
        blup[c] = P @ Z.T @ np.linalg.solve(Z @ P @ Z.T + s2*np.eye(m.sum()), r[m])
    return beta, blup

def predict(beta, blup, X, child, use_child):
    p = X @ beta
    if use_child:
        for i, c in enumerate(child):
            if c in blup:                                 # unseen children get no random effect
                p[i] += blup[c][0] + blup[c][1]*X[i, 3]
    return p

rmse = lambda e: np.sqrt(np.mean(e**2))
X, y, child = simulate(600)
res = {}
for scheme in ["row", "group"]:
    fold = rng.permutation(len(y)) % 5 if scheme == "row" else (rng.permutation(600) % 5)[child]
    errs = {False: [], True: []}
    for k in range(5):
        tr, te = fold != k, fold == k
        beta, blup = fit(X[tr], y[tr], child[tr])
        for uc in (False, True):
            errs[uc].append(y[te] - predict(beta, blup, X[te], child[te], uc))
    res[scheme] = {uc: rmse(np.concatenate(errs[uc])) for uc in errs}
beta, blup = fit(X, y, child)
Xn, yn, cn = simulate(3000)
new = {uc: rmse(yn - predict(beta, blup, Xn, cn + 10**6, uc)) for uc in (False, True)}

Beyond trees and the lasso

The chapter closes with the occasion-level side: methods that relax the assumption, made throughout the example, that reading grows in a straight line (Jacobucci et al. 2023).

  • Piecewise splines with random effects. Splines fit simple pieces between knots. MARS (multivariate adaptive regression splines, Friedman 1991) chooses the variables, knots and pieces automatically. Zhang and He’s MASAL algorithm combines MARS with a linear mixed-effects model to search for nonlinear associations between occasion-level variables (Jacobucci et al. 2023).
  • Generalised additive mixed models (GAMMs). A GAM adds up smooth functions of the predictors. GAMMs add random effects, and can let the smooth curves themselves differ across clusters. Whether smooth curves or sharp knots fit better should be a theoretical choice (Jacobucci et al. 2023).
  • Ensembles. Boosting and random forests for clustered data are less developed. Miller, McArtor and Lubke
    1. found that building the grouping into boosting improved both prediction and variable selection, and Hajjem and colleagues (2014) made a similar extension for random forests (Jacobucci et al. 2023).
  • Intensive longitudinal data. Experience sampling, ecological momentary assessment and passive smartphone data can give a thousand measurements per person, so the number of people can be small. The authors leave that field to its own literature, starting from dynamic network models (Jacobucci et al. 2023).

A practical warning comes with all of this: if the base mixed-effects model takes minutes, adding trees or the lasso can take hours. The authors advise a first run with a handful of predictors that have few distinct values, and rounding continuous predictors, because every distinct value is a cut-point the tree must try (Jacobucci et al. 2023).

Common pitfalls

WarningWhere this goes wrong
  • Running an ordinary tree or lasso on long-format rows. Each child’s four waves are then treated as four independent people, and biased results can follow, especially when person-level and occasion-level variables are mixed and cluster sizes differ. (Jacobucci et al. 2023)
  • Cross-validating by row when the question is about new people. The model gets credit for the held-out person’s other rows. In our simulation that cut the reported error from 0.50 to 0.30 for exactly the same model. Hold out whole clusters (grouped CV) unless the task really is to fill in rows for known people. (Simulation ours; grouped vs stratified CV from the source.) (Jacobucci et al. 2023)
  • Trusting one tree. Separate-model trees, RE-EM trees and GLMM trees make different assumptions, find different trees and support different conclusions. Run more than one. (Jacobucci et al. 2023)
  • Reading “no effects on the slope” as “nothing predicts growth”. Effects on random slopes are much harder to detect than effects on intercepts. A lasso that drops them all may be telling you about power, not about the world. (Jacobucci et al. 2023)
  • Treating the stopping rule as objective. The deviance threshold, the significance level or the BIC-500 decides the size of the tree or the set of predictors, and the choice “remains fairly arbitrary”. Report it and check how sensitive the result is. (Jacobucci et al. 2023)
  • Reporting shrunken lasso coefficients as effect sizes. Refit the selected model without the penalty (the relaxed lasso) to get estimates on the variables’ natural scales. (Jacobucci et al. 2023)

Appendix · the mathematics

The model. For observation i in cluster j, y_{ij}=x_{ij}^\top\beta+z_{ij}^\top u_j+e_{ij} with u_j\sim\mathcal N(0,G), e_{ij}\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma_e^2) and \operatorname{cov}(e,u)=0 (Jacobucci et al. 2023). The ECLS-K growth model is y_{ti}=(\beta_0+u_{0i})+(\beta_1+u_{1i})(x_{ti}-0.25)+e_{ti}, with time in years from 1 September 1998 centred at the first assessment. Writing the cluster’s rows together, y_j\sim\mathcal N(X_j\beta,\,V_j) with V_j=Z_jGZ_j^\top+\sigma_e^2I.

Splitting criterion (separate model per leaf). For a split s of node A into A_1, A_2, \Delta(s)=D(A)-\big[D(A_1)+D(A_2)\big],\qquad D=-2\log L, and the split is kept if \max_s\Delta(s)>k, with k at least \chi^2_{0.95}(p), p the number of added parameters. From the source’s deviances: 33{,}056.92-(15{,}662.16+15{,}833.84)=1{,}560.92; the other five splits give 421.39, 374.96, 171.89, 114.51 and 90.75.

Lasso mixed-effects model. With D(\beta,G,\sigma_e^2) the deviance, f_{\text{lasso}}=D(\beta,G,\sigma_e^2)+\lambda\sum_{p=1}^P|\beta_p|, which reduces to the ordinary fit at \lambda=0 (Jacobucci et al. 2023). The BIC-500 replaces \log n in \text{BIC}=D+q\log n by \log 500, so each parameter costs the same as in a study of 500.

Predictions for known and new clusters. The best linear unbiased predictor of a known cluster’s effects from its residuals r_j=y_j-X_j\hat\beta is \hat u_j=G Z_j^\top V_j^{-1} r_j , a shrunken version of that cluster’s own fitted line. A cluster absent from the training data has no rows to learn from, so \hat u_j=0 and the prediction is x^\top\hat\beta. Row-wise CV evaluates the first kind of prediction, grouped CV the second. In the simulation, the honest error for a new child is roughly \sqrt{z^\top Gz+\sigma_e^2} averaged over waves, about 0.49–0.50, against about 0.30 when the child’s other waves inform \hat u_j.

## Why it matters

Every method earlier in this track treats a dataset as a pile of independent rows. Trees split rows, the
lasso weighs rows, and $k$-fold [cross-validation](../04-predictive/cross-validation.qmd) shuffles rows into
folds. Much social-science data does not look like that. Jacobucci, Grimm and Zhang list the usual cases:
children within schools, repeated measures within people, employees within work units [@jacobucci_ml].
For a theory-driven model the answer is a
[mixed-effects (multilevel) model](../03-regression/mixed-effects-models.qmd), which separates the
between-cluster effect from the within-cluster one, gets the standard errors right and copes with clusters
of different sizes [@jacobucci_ml]. The same dependence causes trouble for machine learning in two places.
Predictions are harder to generate and models are harder to evaluate, and ignoring the clustering can give
biased results, especially when the data mix person-level and occasion-level variables and cluster sizes
differ [@jacobucci_ml].

This entry is about the second wave of methods that fix this: **mixed-effects trees** and **regularised
mixed-effects models**. It follows Jacobucci and colleagues' longitudinal example: reading scores of
kindergarten children from the ECLS-K, followed over the first two years of school [@jacobucci_ml].

## The model the methods are built around

A mixed-effects model for that kind of data gives each child their own line. Jacobucci's reading model
says that child $i$'s score at time $t$ is a starting level plus a rate of growth, and both vary across
children around a population average [@jacobucci_ml]:

- **Fixed effects** are the population averages: the typical starting score and the typical yearly gain.
- **Random effects** are each child's departures from those averages, assumed to come from a normal
  distribution whose variances (and correlation) are estimated.

Fitted to the ECLS-K reading scores, the averages were a starting score of −0.89 in the autumn of
kindergarten and a gain of 1.03 points a year. Children differed with a standard deviation of 0.52 in
where they started and 0.16 in how fast they grew, and the two were negatively correlated (−0.56): children
who started lower tended to grow faster. The occasion-to-occasion noise had a standard deviation of 0.24
[@jacobucci_ml]. The authors point out that the average yearly gain is about two standard deviations of the
differences between children at the start, so a lot happens in two years [@jacobucci_ml].

The question for machine learning is: **which of the things we knew about each child at the start of
kindergarten explain why some children start higher, or grow faster?** There were many candidate
predictors (parents' education, poverty, gender, a parent rating of the child's "approaches to learning")
and no strong theory saying which interactions to expect, which is exactly where trees and the lasso earn
their keep. See [ML for social science](ml-for-social-science.qmd) for that division of labour.

## Trees that grow a mixed-effects model in every leaf

An ordinary [regression tree](../04-predictive/trees-and-forests.qmd) splits the rows so that the outcome
is as similar as possible within each leaf, and predicts with the leaf's mean. A **mixed-effects tree**
replaces "the leaf's mean" with **a whole mixed-effects model per leaf**. The first such method, by
Abdolell and colleagues in 2002, works like this [@jacobucci_ml]:

1. Fit the mixed-effects model to everyone and record its fit, the deviance or $-2\log L$
   (lower is better).
2. For every candidate predictor and every cut-point, split the **children** into two groups, fit the
   model separately in each and add the two deviances.
3. Keep the split that lowers the deviance most, **if** the improvement beats a threshold $k$. Otherwise
   stop.
4. Repeat inside each new group.

Because it splits on child-level variables, all of a child's waves always stay together, and each leaf ends
up as a group of children with its own growth curve. Jacobucci and colleagues suggest setting $k$ either as
a fixed number, as a fraction of the starting deviance (say 1%), or at the very least at the 95th
percentile of a $\chi^2$ with as many degrees of freedom as the split adds parameters [@jacobucci_ml].

### Reading a mixed-effects tree

Run on the ECLS-K reading data with the **longRPart2** package, each split had to cut the deviance by
about 74 units. That is a demanding bar for one split, the authors note, since each split adds six
parameters [@jacobucci_ml]. The tree split first on **father's education**, which cut the deviance from
33,056.92 to 31,496.00 (15,662.16 + 15,833.84), an improvement of 1,560.92. Further splits used poverty
status, mother's education (twice), approaches to learning and gender, ending in seven leaves
[@jacobucci_ml]. The left panel of the figure lays out how much each split bought.

What differs between the leaves is mostly **where children start**: the leaves' average starting scores run
from −1.31 to −0.60, while their average yearly gains stay between 0.97 and 1.10 [@jacobucci_ml]. The tree,
in other words, mainly says that family background predicts the level children arrive at school with,
not how fast they then learn to read. The leaves also differ in their random-effect structure. In two
leaves (children of less-educated fathers who were below the poverty line or whose mothers had little
education) a child's starting level and growth were almost uncorrelated, while in the other five the
correlation was clearly negative, from −0.38 to −0.69 [@jacobucci_ml]. The package's variable-importance
measure ranked mother's and father's education highest (about 20% each), well ahead of poverty (about 10%),
gender and approaches to learning (about 5% each) [@jacobucci_ml].

### Two families of mixed-effects tree

The approaches differ in **what is allowed to vary between leaves** [@jacobucci_ml]:

| Family | What each leaf gets | Packages | Strength |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Separate model per leaf** (Abdolell et al. 2002) | its own fixed effects *and* its own random-effect variances | longRPart / longRPart2 | flexible; finds groups whose *variability* differs |
| **One model, leaves as dummies** (RE-EM tree, Hajjem et al. 2011; Sela & Simonoff 2012; GLMM tree, Fokkema et al. 2018) | its own fixed effects; one set of random-effect variances for everybody | REEMtree, glmertree | better for prediction; can split on occasion-level variables too |

The **RE-EM tree** (random effects via the EM algorithm) alternates between fitting a tree to the outcome
with the current random effects taken out, and re-estimating the random effects given the tree. Because the
tree enters the mixed-effects model only as a set of indicator variables, there is a **single set of
random-effect parameters** for all leaves, and the tree can split on occasion-level variables, so the same
child's waves can land in different leaves [@jacobucci_ml]. It has been shown to predict better than the
separate-model approach, at the cost of less interpretable structure [@jacobucci_ml]. Later variants fix
specific weaknesses [@jacobucci_ml]:

- Fu and Simonoff (2015) replaced the CART splitting step with **conditional inference trees**, removing
  CART's bias towards variables with many distinct values.
- The **MELT** algorithm (Eo and Cho 2014) splits on child-level variables to find groups whose *slopes*
  differ, useful when the question is what moderates a within-person association.
- The **GLMM tree** of Fokkema and colleagues allows non-normal outcomes (counts, binary) and model-based
  partitioning, holding the random-effect parameters the same across leaves like the RE-EM tree.
- Stegmann and colleagues extended the separate-model approach to **nonlinear** mixed-effects models (such
  as exponential growth), and Serang and colleagues linked CART to Mplus (**MplusTrees**) for multilevel
  models with several outcomes.

On the ECLS-K data, glmertree's first splits closely followed longRPart2's (father's education, poverty,
mother's education), again with seven leaves whose starting levels ran from −1.29 to −0.57 and whose growth
rates barely moved (0.97 to 1.09) [@jacobucci_ml]. It used a tiny significance level ($10^{-16}$) to stop
the very large sample producing endless splits. Its single set of random effects was smaller than the
full-sample model's (intercept SD 0.47, slope SD 0.15), as it should be once the leaves have absorbed part
of the differences between children [@jacobucci_ml]. REEMtree, which was given time as a candidate
splitting variable and fitted a random intercept only, **split on time first**: its first three splits simply
separated the four assessment waves. Only then did it use father's education, always at the same cut
between "some college" and "a bachelor's degree" [@jacobucci_ml]. The lesson is the authors' own: these
specifications **produce different trees and different conclusions**, the best one depends on the data,
and it is wise to run more than one [@jacobucci_ml].

## The lasso inside a mixed-effects model

The second tool is [regularisation](../04-predictive/regularization.qmd). A **lasso mixed-effects model**
minimises the model's deviance plus a penalty $\lambda$ times the sum of the absolute fixed effects, so that
as $\lambda$ grows, weak predictors are set exactly to zero [@jacobucci_ml]. Some versions penalise the
random effects instead, to decide which slopes need to vary across people at all [@jacobucci_ml].

The ECLS-K analysis used it in two stages, following Grimm and colleagues (2022) [@jacobucci_ml]. First the
standardised predictors of children's **starting level** were penalised, and $\lambda$ chosen. Then, with
the survivors kept in, the predictors of children's **growth** were penalised. To choose $\lambda$ the
authors used a **"BIC-500"**: the Bayesian information criterion recomputed as if the sample had 500
children. The ordinary BIC's penalty per parameter is too lenient with a sample as large as the ECLS-K
[@jacobucci_ml].

The result was blunt. For starting level, the chosen penalty kept only **father's education and poverty
status**. For growth, it kept **nothing**. The authors point out that this is common, because there is much
less power to detect effects on random slopes than on random intercepts, so one may want a different
criterion for each [@jacobucci_ml]. They then refitted an ordinary mixed-effects model with just those two
predictors (the **relaxed lasso**, Meinshausen 2007), so that the estimates are on the variables' natural
scales and not shrunk. Each extra level of father's education raised the expected starting score by 0.08,
and children above the poverty line started 0.24 higher [@jacobucci_ml]. The tree and the lasso agree: in
these data, family background shapes the starting line, not the pace.

## Cross-validating when rows share a person

Model selection is the unsolved part. Jacobucci and colleagues note that cross-validation becomes very slow
when every fit is a mixed-effects model, so researchers fall back on fit indices (BIC, a deviance
threshold), and the choice of index and cut-off "remains fairly arbitrary" [@jacobucci_ml]. When you do
cross-validate, there is a further decision that ordinary $k$-fold hides. Longitudinal data are stored in
"long" form, one row per child per wave, and assigning random rows to folds leaves every child's other
waves in the training data. The alternatives they point to are **stratified** CV, which balances each
person's time points across folds, and **grouped** CV, which puts whole people into folds, so 20% of the
participants are held out in each round of 5-fold CV [@jacobucci_ml].

Which one is right depends on the question you will use the model for:

- *Predict a missed wave for a child we already follow* → hold out **rows**.
- *Predict a child who is not yet in the data* (a new cohort, a new school) → hold out **children**.

Use row-wise folds for the second question and the model gets credit for knowing the held-out child's other
waves, knowledge it will not have when it meets a new child. The right panel of the figure shows how large
that flattery can be.

```{python}
#| label: fig-ml-clustered
#| fig-cap: "Left: how a mixed-effects tree earns its splits. Row-wise 5-fold CV credits the model with each held-out child's other waves and reports an error of 0.30 (orange). Grouped CV, holding out whole children, reports 0.50, which matches the error on 3,000 genuinely new children (0.49). A model with the fixed part only is the same (about 0.50) under every scheme."

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# ---- left: deviance improvements of the longRPart2 tree (Jacobucci et al., Algorithm 3) ----
splits = [("father's educ.", 33056.92, 15662.16, 15833.84),
          ("poverty", 15662.16, 3114.981, 12125.79),
          ("mother's educ.\n(< 6)", 15833.84, 8345.322, 7113.559),
          ("mother's educ.\n(< 3)", 12125.79, 1533.931, 10419.97),
          ("gender", 10419.97, 5603.362, 4702.098),
          ("approaches\nto learning", 8345.322, 3675.407, 4579.168)]
labels = [s[0] for s in splits]
gain = np.array([parent - a - b for _, parent, a, b in splits])

# ---- right: row-wise vs grouped CV for a mixed-effects model (simulation, ours) ----
rng = np.random.default_rng(11)
T = np.array([0.0, 0.55, 1.0, 1.55])                      # four waves (years), ours
Psi = np.array([[0.47**2, -0.53*0.47*0.16], [-0.53*0.47*0.16, 0.16**2]])
sig = 0.24

def simulate(n):
    daded = rng.integers(1, 10, n); poor = rng.integers(1, 3, n)
    u = rng.multivariate_normal([0, 0], Psi, n)
    a = -1.39 + 0.08*(daded - 1) + 0.24*(poor - 1) + u[:, 0]
    b = 1.03 + u[:, 1]
    child = np.repeat(np.arange(n), 4); t = np.tile(T, n)
    y = a[child] + b[child]*t + rng.normal(0, sig, 4*n)
    X = np.column_stack([np.ones(4*n), daded[child] - 1, poor[child] - 1, t])
    return X, y, child

def fit(X, y, child):
    beta = np.linalg.lstsq(X, y, rcond=None)[0]           # fixed part
    r = y - X @ beta
    lines, covs, ss, df = [], [], 0.0, 0
    for c in np.unique(child):                            # moment estimates of the variance components
        m = child == c
        if m.sum() >= 3:
            Z = np.column_stack([np.ones(m.sum()), X[m, 3]])
            g = np.linalg.lstsq(Z, r[m], rcond=None)[0]
            e = r[m] - Z @ g; ss += e @ e; df += m.sum() - 2
            lines.append(g); covs.append(np.linalg.inv(Z.T @ Z))
    s2 = ss / df
    P = np.cov(np.array(lines).T) - s2 * np.mean(covs, axis=0)
    blup = {}
    for c in np.unique(child):                            # each child's shrunken intercept and slope
        m = child == c
        Z = np.column_stack([np.ones(m.sum()), X[m, 3]])
        blup[c] = P @ Z.T @ np.linalg.solve(Z @ P @ Z.T + s2*np.eye(m.sum()), r[m])
    return beta, blup

def predict(beta, blup, X, child, use_child):
    p = X @ beta
    if use_child:
        for i, c in enumerate(child):
            if c in blup:                                 # unseen children get no random effect
                p[i] += blup[c][0] + blup[c][1]*X[i, 3]
    return p

rmse = lambda e: np.sqrt(np.mean(e**2))
X, y, child = simulate(600)
res = {}
for scheme in ["row", "group"]:
    fold = rng.permutation(len(y)) % 5 if scheme == "row" else (rng.permutation(600) % 5)[child]
```

The later splits each clear the bar by less, and the last (approaches to learning, 91 units) only just
makes it. A slightly stricter threshold would have given a smaller tree, which is why the authors call the
choice of cut-off arbitrary.

The right panel is a simulation of our own, with the ECLS-K estimates as the true values. The model that
uses each child's own estimated effects (their best linear unbiased predictions, the
[partially pooled](../03-regression/mixed-effects-models.qmd) child-level terms) looks far better under
row-wise CV: 0.30 against 0.50. That number is correct for its question. If you want to fill in a missing
wave for a child already in the study, the child's other waves really do help. It is wrong for the other
question. For a child the model has never seen there are no child-level effects to estimate, the model
falls back on its fixed part, and the honest error is 0.49, which grouped CV reports (0.50). The fixed-part
model, which never uses the child's identity, scores the same under every scheme. The problem is not
cross-validation itself. The folds have to match the question you will use the model for.

## Beyond trees and the lasso

The chapter closes with the occasion-level side: methods that relax the assumption, made throughout the
example, that reading grows in a straight line [@jacobucci_ml].

- **Piecewise splines with random effects.** Splines fit simple pieces between **knots**. **MARS**
  (multivariate adaptive regression splines, Friedman 1991) chooses the variables, knots and pieces
  automatically. Zhang and He's **MASAL** algorithm combines MARS with a linear mixed-effects model to
  search for nonlinear associations between occasion-level variables [@jacobucci_ml].
- **Generalised additive mixed models (GAMMs).** A [GAM](beyond-linearity-splines-and-gams.qmd) adds up
  smooth functions of the predictors. GAMMs add random effects, and can let the smooth curves themselves
  differ across clusters. Whether smooth curves or sharp knots fit better should be a theoretical choice
  [@jacobucci_ml].
- **Ensembles.** Boosting and random forests for clustered data are less developed. Miller, McArtor and Lubke
  (2017) found that building the grouping into boosting improved both prediction and variable selection, and
  Hajjem and colleagues (2014) made a similar extension for random forests [@jacobucci_ml].
- **Intensive longitudinal data.** Experience sampling, ecological momentary assessment and passive
  smartphone data can give a thousand measurements per person, so the number of people can be small. The
  authors leave that field to its own literature, starting from dynamic network models [@jacobucci_ml].

A practical warning comes with all of this: if the base mixed-effects model takes minutes, adding trees or
the lasso can take hours. The authors advise a first run with a handful of predictors that have few distinct
values, and rounding continuous predictors, because every distinct value is a cut-point the tree must try
[@jacobucci_ml].

## Common pitfalls

::: {.callout-warning title="Where this goes wrong"}
- **Running an ordinary tree or lasso on long-format rows.** Each child's four waves are then treated as four
  independent people, and biased results can follow, especially when person-level and occasion-level
  variables are mixed and cluster sizes differ. [@jacobucci_ml]
- **Cross-validating by row when the question is about new people.** The model gets credit for the held-out
  person's other rows. In our simulation that cut the reported error from 0.50 to 0.30 for exactly the same
  model. Hold out whole clusters (grouped CV) unless the task really is to fill in rows for known people.
  *(Simulation ours; grouped vs stratified CV from the source.)* [@jacobucci_ml]
- **Trusting one tree.** Separate-model trees, RE-EM trees and GLMM trees make different assumptions, find
  different trees and support different conclusions. Run more than one. [@jacobucci_ml]
- **Reading "no effects on the slope" as "nothing predicts growth".** Effects on random slopes are much harder
  to detect than effects on intercepts. A lasso that drops them all may be telling you about power, not
  about the world. [@jacobucci_ml]
- **Treating the stopping rule as objective.** The deviance threshold, the significance level or the
  BIC-500 decides the size of the tree or the set of predictors, and the choice "remains fairly arbitrary".
  Report it and check how sensitive the result is. [@jacobucci_ml]
- **Reporting shrunken lasso coefficients as effect sizes.** Refit the selected model without the penalty
  (the relaxed lasso) to get estimates on the variables' natural scales. [@jacobucci_ml]
:::

## Appendix · the mathematics {#sec-clustered-math .unnumbered}

::: {.math-appendix}

**The model.** For observation $i$ in cluster $j$, $y_{ij}=x_{ij}^\top\beta+z_{ij}^\top u_j+e_{ij}$ with
$u_j\sim\mathcal N(0,G)$, $e_{ij}\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma_e^2)$ and $\operatorname{cov}(e,u)=0$
[@jacobucci_ml]. The ECLS-K growth model is
$y_{ti}=(\beta_0+u_{0i})+(\beta_1+u_{1i})(x_{ti}-0.25)+e_{ti}$, with time in years from 1 September 1998
centred at the first assessment. Writing the cluster's rows together, $y_j\sim\mathcal N(X_j\beta,\,V_j)$
with $V_j=Z_jGZ_j^\top+\sigma_e^2I$.

**Splitting criterion (separate model per leaf).** For a split $s$ of node $A$ into $A_1, A_2$,
$$
\Delta(s)=D(A)-\big[D(A_1)+D(A_2)\big],\qquad D=-2\log L,
$$
and the split is kept if $\max_s\Delta(s)>k$, with $k$ at least $\chi^2_{0.95}(p)$, $p$ the number of
added parameters. From the source's deviances: $33{,}056.92-(15{,}662.16+15{,}833.84)=1{,}560.92$;
the other five splits give 421.39, 374.96, 171.89, 114.51 and 90.75.

**Lasso mixed-effects model.** With $D(\beta,G,\sigma_e^2)$ the deviance,
$$
f_{\text{lasso}}=D(\beta,G,\sigma_e^2)+\lambda\sum_{p=1}^P|\beta_p|,
$$
which reduces to the ordinary fit at $\lambda=0$ [@jacobucci_ml]. The BIC-500 replaces $\log n$ in
$\text{BIC}=D+q\log n$ by $\log 500$, so each parameter costs the same as in a study of 500.

**Predictions for known and new clusters.** The best linear unbiased predictor of a known cluster's effects
from its residuals $r_j=y_j-X_j\hat\beta$ is
$$
\hat u_j=G Z_j^\top V_j^{-1} r_j ,
$$
a shrunken version of that cluster's own fitted line. A cluster absent from the training data has no rows
to learn from, so $\hat u_j=0$ and the prediction is $x^\top\hat\beta$. Row-wise CV evaluates the first kind
of prediction, grouped CV the second. In the simulation, the honest error for a new child is roughly
$\sqrt{z^\top Gz+\sigma_e^2}$ averaged over waves, about 0.49–0.50, against about 0.30 when the child's
other waves inform $\hat u_j$.

:::
