Hidden Markov Models

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source

A mixture model whose hidden label has a memory. You never see the state, only what it emits, and three short recursions recover it: filter it as you go, smooth it in hindsight, or decode the single best story.

A hidden Markov model (HMM) says the world moves through a few unseen states that persist and switch according to a Markov chain, and each state emits observations with its own distribution. You see only the emissions. The model’s value lies in its algorithms: the forward pass (what state am I in now?), forward–backward (what state was I in, given everything?), Viterbi (what is the single most probable path?) and Baum–Welch (EM for learning the model when the states are never labelled).

Why it matters

Many social and behavioural sequences are driven by a regime you cannot observe directly: an economy in expansion or recession, a speaker taking or yielding the floor, a respondent who is engaged or just clicking through. Read one observation at a time and each one is noisy. Read the sequence and the regime shows, because regimes persist. A run of unusual values is more likely to be a regime than a run of coincidences.

The mixture model already gives each observation a soft membership in a hidden cluster. An HMM is the same idea with one addition: cluster membership has Markovian dynamics, which is why Murphy notes HMMs are also called Markov switching models (Murphy 2012). Economists know the continuous-time-series version as Hamilton’s Markov-switching regression; the Kalman filter is the twin model for a continuous hidden state. This entry gives the discrete-state machinery that both of those lean on.

First, a visible Markov chain

Start without anything hidden. A Markov chain assumes the current state carries everything relevant for predicting the next one, so the joint probability of a sequence factorises into a starting probability times one-step transitions (Murphy 2012). With K discrete states the transitions form a K\times K transition matrix A, where A_{ij} is the probability of moving from state i to state j; each row sums to one (Murphy 2012). Multiplying the matrix by itself gives multi-step transitions (“powering up” the matrix), the Chapman–Kolmogorov result the Markov-chains appendix develops.

When the states are observed, estimation is just counting. The maximum-likelihood estimate of A_{jk} is the number of observed j\to k transitions divided by the number of transitions out of j (Murphy 2012). The trap is sparsity. Murphy’s example is a word-level language model (an n-gram model): with about 50,000 words, a bigram model already has about 2.5 billion free parameters, almost all of them unseen in any training text, and setting an unseen pair’s probability to zero is “a severe form of overfitting” (Murphy 2012). The fixes are smoothing: add-one smoothing, which has a Bayesian justification (a uniform Dirichlet prior on each row), or deleted interpolation, a weighted blend of the bigram and unigram frequencies, which Murphy shows is close to the posterior mean under a shared hierarchical Dirichlet prior (Murphy 2012). That is hierarchical shrinkage applied to the rows of a transition matrix.

Now hide the state

An HMM keeps the chain but hides it. There is a discrete hidden state z_t\in\{1,\dots,K\} following a Markov chain, plus an observation model p(x_t\mid z_t): a table of symbol probabilities if the emissions are discrete, or a state-specific Gaussian if they are continuous (Murphy 2012). Murphy’s 2-D Gaussian example shows the typical look of HMM data: several observations land near the same place, then there is a sudden jump to a new cluster (Murphy 2012).

Two things follow. First, the observations themselves are not Markov: the hidden chain carries information across long stretches, so an HMM can represent long-range dependence that a visible Markov model cannot (Murphy 2012). Second, the states can be given meaning (the spoken word behind an acoustic signal, the part of speech behind a word, whether a stretch of DNA codes for a gene), and the job becomes inferring them (Murphy 2012).

The occasionally dishonest casino

Murphy’s running example, taken from Durbin et al., is a casino that mostly rolls a fair die but occasionally switches to a loaded one that favours six, for a short stretch (Murphy 2012). You see only the faces. The state (which die) is hidden; the faces are the emissions; the switching is the Markov chain.

Here is the logic of the forward pass done by hand, with illustrative numbers of our own: the casino stays with the fair die with probability 0.95 per roll and with the loaded die with probability 0.90; the loaded die shows a six half the time. In the long run the loaded die is in use one third of the time, so that is the starting belief.

Step Belief “loaded” before the roll Roll Belief “loaded” after the roll
1 0.333 6 0.600
2 0.560 6 0.792
3 0.724 6 0.887
4 0.804 1 0.711

Each row does two things. Predict: push yesterday’s belief through the transition matrix (0.600 becomes 0.560, because the die might have switched). Update: weigh the new roll by how likely each die is to produce it, using Bayes’ rule (a six is three times likelier from the loaded die, so belief jumps). Three sixes in a row make “loaded” likely; one ordinary roll pulls it back only partly, because states persist. That two-step cycle is the whole forward algorithm.

Five questions you can ask

With the parameters known, an HMM answers several distinct questions, and confusing them is a common source of error (Murphy 2012):

  • Filtering: p(z_t\mid x_{1:t}), the belief about the current state using only data up to now. This is the online, real-time answer. It reduces noise compared with judging each observation alone.
  • Smoothing: p(z_t\mid x_{1:T}), the belief about state t using all the data, past and future. Murphy’s analogy is a detective: confusion lasts until the key clue turns up, after which the earlier observations make sense in hindsight (Murphy 2012). Fixed-lag smoothing sits between the two: wait \ell steps, then commit, trading delay for accuracy.
  • Prediction: p(z_{t+h}\mid x_{1:t}), obtained by applying the transition matrix h more times to the current belief; summing over the emission model turns it into a forecast of future observations (Murphy 2012).
  • MAP decoding (Viterbi): the single most probable whole path z_{1:T}.
  • Probability of the evidence: p(x_{1:T}), summed over all hidden paths, which lets you classify sequences, cluster them, or flag anomalies (Murphy 2012).
Show the HMM code (simulate, forward, forward-backward, Viterbi) 
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
A = np.array([[0.95, 0.05],          # fair   -> fair, loaded
              [0.10, 0.90]])         # loaded -> fair, loaded
B = np.vstack([np.full(6, 1/6),                    # fair die
               np.r_[np.full(5, 0.1), 0.5]])       # loaded die: six half the time
pi0 = np.array([1.0, 0.0])
T = 300

# simulate the hidden die and the observed faces
z = np.zeros(T, int); x = np.zeros(T, int)
for t in range(T):
    if t > 0:
        z[t] = rng.choice(2, p=A[z[t-1]])
    x[t] = rng.choice(6, p=B[z[t]])

# forward pass (filtering): predict, then update, normalising each step
alpha = np.zeros((T, 2))
u = pi0 * B[:, x[0]]; alpha[0] = u / u.sum()
for t in range(1, T):
    u = B[:, x[t]] * (A.T @ alpha[t-1]); alpha[t] = u / u.sum()

# backward pass, then combine (smoothing)
beta = np.ones((T, 2))
for t in range(T-1, 0, -1):
    v = A @ (B[:, x[t]] * beta[t]); beta[t-1] = v / v.sum()
gamma = alpha * beta; gamma /= gamma.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)

# Viterbi in logs: max instead of sum, then trace back
logd = np.log(pi0 + 1e-300) + np.log(B[:, x[0]])
back = np.zeros((T, 2), int)
for t in range(1, T):
    s = logd[:, None] + np.log(A)
    back[t] = s.argmax(axis=0)
    logd = s.max(axis=0) + np.log(B[:, x[t]])
path = np.zeros(T, int); path[-1] = logd.argmax()
for t in range(T-1, 0, -1):
    path[t-1] = back[t, path[t]]

err_f = int(((alpha[:, 1] > 0.5) != z).sum())
err_s = int(((gamma[:, 1] > 0.5) != z).sum())
err_v = int((path != z).sum())

fig, axes = plt.subplots(3, 1, figsize=(8.6, 6.2), sharex=True)
rolls = np.arange(1, T + 1)
for ax in axes:
    ax.fill_between(rolls, 0, 1, where=z == 1, step="mid", color="#e4e3d8", lw=0)
    ax.set_ylim(-0.05, 1.05)
axes[0].plot(rolls, alpha[:, 1], color="#5e5e54", lw=1.3,
             label=f"filtered: {err_f}/{T} wrong")
axes[0].set_ylabel("p(loaded)")
axes[1].plot(rolls, gamma[:, 1], color="#e8531d", lw=1.6,
             label=f"smoothed: {err_s}/{T} wrong")
axes[1].set_ylabel("p(loaded)")
axes[2].step(rolls, path, where="mid", color="#161613", lw=1.4,
             label=f"Viterbi path: {err_v}/{T} wrong")
axes[2].set_yticks([0, 1]); axes[2].set_yticklabels(["fair", "loaded"])
axes[2].set_xlabel("roll number")
for ax in axes:
    ax.legend(loc="upper right", fontsize=8, frameon=False)
fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()

The forward algorithm: Bayes’ rule on a loop

The table above is the forward algorithm. Each step predicts with the transition matrix and updates with the new observation’s likelihood, then normalises; the normalising constants, logged and summed, give the log-likelihood of the whole sequence as a free by-product (Murphy 2012). Murphy calls this the predict–update cycle, and the vector of K filtered probabilities the belief state (Murphy 2012). It is the discrete twin of the Kalman filter’s predict-then-correct step, and like it, it runs online in one pass.

Two practical notes from Murphy. Multiply enough probabilities and the numbers underflow to zero, so real code normalises at every step or works in logs (Murphy 2012). And the cost is O(K^2T): linear in the length of the sequence, quadratic in the number of states (Murphy 2012). Summing over the K^T possible hidden paths directly would be hopeless; the recursion makes it cheap.

Forward–backward: hindsight

To smooth, run a second recursion from the end of the sequence backwards. It computes, for each state at time t, how well that state explains the future observations. Multiply that by the filtered (past) belief and normalise, and you have p(z_t\mid x_{1:T}) (Murphy 2012). Murphy describes it as passing messages left to right, then right to left, and combining them at each node, the special case of the general belief propagation algorithm for graphical models (Murphy 2012).

In the figure the smoothed curve (middle) switches more cleanly than the filtered one (top). The filter has to wait for evidence to pile up before it believes a switch has happened, which is the right behaviour for a decision made in real time. The smoother can see the run of sixes that followed and revise the earlier rolls.

Viterbi: the single best story

Sometimes you need one coherent answer rather than a probability at each step: the transcript of a speech signal, or a single segmentation of a sequence into regimes. The Viterbi algorithm finds the jointly most probable path. It uses the same recursion as the forward pass with “sum” replaced by “max”, and it remembers, for each state, which predecessor got it there, so the best path can be traced back from the end (Murphy 2012). Murphy’s speech example traces it on a three-state model: \delta_1(1)=0.5; then 0.5\times0.3\times0.3=0.045 for staying in state 1 against 0.5\times0.7\times0.2=0.07 for moving to state 2, and so on, ending in the path 1, 2, 2, 3 (Murphy 2012).

The most probable path is not the same as the sequence of individually most probable states. Murphy’s two-step example (Murphy 2012):

X_1=0 X_1=1 total
X_2=0 0.04 0.30 0.34
X_2=1 0.36 0.30 0.66
total 0.40 0.60

The most probable joint cell is (0,1) at 0.36. But the marginally most probable value of X_1 is 1 (0.60) and of X_2 is 1 (0.66), giving (1,1), a combination with probability only 0.30. Viterbi’s answer is always globally consistent: Murphy’s example is “recognize speech” misheard as “wreck a nice beach”, where “beach” might win locally but loses once the whole utterance has to make linguistic sense (Murphy 2012). Thresholding the smoothed marginals is more robust per step, because each state averages over its neighbours instead of conditioning on one guess about them; it is also the rule that minimises the expected number of wrongly labelled steps (Murphy 2012).

In Murphy’s casino run, thresholding gave 71 errors in 300 rolls for filtering, 49 for smoothing and 60 for Viterbi (Murphy 2012). In our simulated sequence above, Viterbi happens to edge out smoothing. That is not a contradiction: smoothing minimises errors in expectation, and any single sequence can fall either way. Filtering is the worst of the three in both, because it uses the least information.

Learning an HMM: Baum–Welch

If you have training sequences where the hidden states were labelled, fitting is counting again: transitions from the state labels, emission probabilities from the times each state produced each symbol (Murphy 2012). Usually the states are never seen, and you are in the same position as with a mixture model. The standard answer is EM, known for HMMs as the Baum–Welch algorithm (Murphy 2012):

  • E-step: run forward–backward with the current parameters to get, for every time step, the probability of being in each state and the probability of each transition between consecutive steps. Summed over time these are expected counts.
  • M-step: re-estimate exactly as if those expected counts were real counts. The new A_{jk} is the expected number of j\to k transitions divided by the expected number of transitions out of j; Gaussian emission means are posterior-weighted averages (Murphy 2012).

Everything you know about EM for mixtures carries over. It climbs to a local optimum, so initialisation matters: use some labelled data, or fit a plain mixture first and ignore the dynamics, or use several random restarts (Murphy 2012). Murphy warns against starting with Viterbi training (using only the single best path in the E-step): early on the parameters are poor and the best path is fairly arbitrary, so it is safer to start with forward–backward and switch near convergence (Murphy 2012). A Bayesian alternative samples whole hidden paths (forwards filtering, backwards sampling) inside a Gibbs sampler, and must then deal with label switching just as mixtures do (Murphy 2012).

How many states? As with the number of mixture components: grid-search K and compare cross-validated likelihood or BIC, or use Bayesian methods that switch off unneeded states (Murphy 2012). The training likelihood alone will always prefer more states.

Stretching the model

The basic HMM makes two strong assumptions, and Murphy surveys the standard ways to relax them (Murphy 2012):

  • How long a regime lasts. With a self-transition probability A_{ii}, the time spent in a state is geometric: the single most likely stay is one step, and long stays become exponentially rarer (Murphy 2012). Real regimes often do not behave like that. A hidden semi-Markov model gives each state an explicit duration distribution. A cheaper trick chains n copies of a state with the same emissions, which turns the geometric duration into a negative binomial that can have a hump (Murphy 2012).
  • Observations that depend on each other. The basic HMM assumes the observations are independent given the state. The auto-regressive HMM, also called a regime-switching Markov model, lets x_t depend on x_{t-1} through a regression whose coefficients change with the hidden state. Murphy notes these are widely used in econometrics, citing Hamilton (1990) (Murphy 2012); fitting them is weighted least squares, one regression per regime, weighted by the smoothed state probabilities. This is the model behind the Markov-switching section of the time-series track.
  • Inputs. An input-output HMM lets covariates drive the switching: the transition probabilities become a logistic regression on the inputs, with coefficients that depend on the previous state (Murphy 2012).
  • Several interacting sequences. A coupled HMM links chains so that each one’s next state depends on its neighbours’ current states. The leaner influence model writes each chain’s transition as a weighted blend of pairwise influences, where each weight says how much one chain sways another. It has been used to model conversational interaction between people (Murphy 2012).

Common pitfalls

WarningWhere this goes wrong
  • Treating states as discovered facts. The model returns states 1, 2, 3; the labels “recession”, “engaged” or “loaded” are your reading of their emission distributions. Labels are arbitrary across runs (label switching), and a different K can carve the sequence differently. (Murphy 2012)
  • Using smoothed states for a real-time decision. Smoothing conditions on future observations (Murphy 2012). A backtest that acts on p(z_t\mid x_{1:T}) at time t is using information it would not have had; the honest real-time quantity is the filtered belief.
  • Reading the Viterbi path as a list of individually likely states. It is the most probable joint path; a step on it can be less probable, taken alone, than the alternative. For per-step accuracy, threshold the smoothed marginals. (Murphy 2012)
  • Ignoring the geometric-duration assumption. A plain HMM thinks the most likely regime length is one step. If your regimes have typical lengths, use a semi-Markov or expanded-state model. (Murphy 2012)
  • One EM run, or K picked by training likelihood. Baum–Welch finds local optima and more states always fit better in-sample; restart several times and choose K on held-out likelihood or BIC. (Murphy 2012)
  • Multiplying raw probabilities. Over a long sequence they underflow to zero; normalise at each step or work in logs. (Murphy 2012)

Appendix · the mathematics

The model. With initial distribution \pi, transition matrix A_{ij}=p(z_t=j\mid z_{t-1}=i) and emission model \phi_t(j)=p(x_t\mid z_t=j), p(z_{1:T},x_{1:T}) = p(z_1)\prod_{t=2}^{T}p(z_t\mid z_{t-1})\prod_{t=1}^{T}p(x_t\mid z_t).

Forward (filtering). Let \alpha_t(j)=p(z_t=j\mid x_{1:t}). Predict, then update: \alpha_t \;\propto\; \phi_t \odot \big(A^\top \alpha_{t-1}\big),\qquad Z_t = \sum_j \phi_t(j)\,[A^\top\alpha_{t-1}]_j = p(x_t\mid x_{1:t-1}), with \alpha_1\propto \phi_1\odot\pi. The log-likelihood is \log p(x_{1:T})=\sum_t \log Z_t.

Backward and smoothing. Let \beta_t(i)=p(x_{t+1:T}\mid z_t=i), with \beta_T=\mathbf 1 and \beta_{t-1} = A\,\big(\phi_t\odot\beta_t\big). Conditioning on z_t splits the chain into past and future, so \gamma_t(j)=p(z_t=j\mid x_{1:T})\propto \alpha_t(j)\,\beta_t(j). The two-slice marginal needed by EM is \xi_{t,t+1}(i,j)\propto \alpha_t(i)\,A_{ij}\,\phi_{t+1}(j)\,\beta_{t+1}(j).

Viterbi. Let \delta_t(j)=\max_{z_{1:t-1}}p(z_{1:t-1},z_t=j\mid x_{1:t}). The best path to j at t extends the best path to some i at t-1: \delta_t(j)=\max_i\,\delta_{t-1}(i)\,A_{ij}\,\phi_t(j),\qquad a_t(j)=\arg\max_i\,\delta_{t-1}(i)\,A_{ij}\,\phi_t(j), with \delta_1(j)=\pi_j\phi_1(j), z_T^*=\arg\max_i\delta_T(i) and traceback z_t^*=a_{t+1}(z_{t+1}^*). Because \log\max=\max\log, the recursion runs exactly in logs.

Baum–Welch M-step. With expected counts from the E-step, \hat A_{jk}=\frac{\sum_t \xi_{t-1,t}(j,k)}{\sum_{k'}\sum_t \xi_{t-1,t}(j,k')},\qquad \hat\pi_k=\gamma_1(k),\qquad \hat\mu_k=\frac{\sum_t\gamma_t(k)\,x_t}{\sum_t\gamma_t(k)} (summed over sequences when there are several); the expected complete-data log-likelihood separates into a start term, a transition term and an emission term, each maximised on its own.

Durations. Staying exactly d steps in state i has probability (1-A_{ii})A_{ii}^{\,d-1}, a geometric law with mean 1/(1-A_{ii}); in the casino above that is 20 rolls for the fair die and 10 for the loaded one. Chaining n copies with self-loop p gives p(d)=\binom{d-1}{n-1}p^{\,d-n}(1-p)^n, a negative binomial.

The worked table, checked. With A=\begin{pmatrix}0.95&0.05\\0.10&0.90\end{pmatrix} the stationary share of the loaded die solves p=0.05(1-p)+0.9p, so p=1/3. After a six: \frac{(1/3)(0.5)}{(2/3)(1/6)+(1/3)(0.5)}=0.600; predict 0.6(0.9)+0.4(0.05)=0.560; and so on down the table.

::: {.lead-kernel}
A **hidden Markov model (HMM)** says the world moves through a few unseen **states** that persist and switch
according to a Markov chain, and each state **emits** observations with its own distribution. You see only
the emissions. The model's value lies in its algorithms: the **forward** pass (what state am I in *now*?),
**forward–backward** (what state was I in, *given everything*?), **Viterbi** (what is the *single most
probable* path?) and **Baum–Welch** (EM for learning the model when the states are never labelled).
:::

## Why it matters

Many social and behavioural sequences are driven by a regime you cannot observe directly: an economy in
expansion or recession, a speaker taking or yielding the floor, a respondent who is engaged or just
clicking through. Read one observation at a time and each one is noisy. Read the sequence and the regime
shows, because regimes **persist**. A run of unusual values is more likely to be a regime than a run of
coincidences.

The [mixture model](mixture-models-and-em.qmd) already gives each observation a soft membership in a hidden
cluster. An HMM is the same idea with one addition: **cluster membership has Markovian dynamics**, which is
why Murphy notes HMMs are also called **Markov switching models** [@murphy_ml]. Economists know the
continuous-time-series version as Hamilton's
[Markov-switching regression](../04-predictive/nonlinear-time-series.qmd#markov-switching-regimes-you-cannot-see);
the [Kalman filter](../04-predictive/state-space-and-kalman-filter.qmd) is the twin model for a *continuous*
hidden state. This entry gives the discrete-state machinery that both of those lean on.

## First, a visible Markov chain

Start without anything hidden. A **Markov chain** assumes the current state carries everything relevant for
predicting the next one, so the joint probability of a sequence factorises into a starting probability times
one-step **transitions** [@murphy_ml]. With $K$ discrete states the transitions form a $K\times K$
**transition matrix** $A$, where $A_{ij}$ is the probability of moving from state $i$ to state $j$; each row
sums to one [@murphy_ml]. Multiplying the matrix by itself gives multi-step transitions ("powering up" the
matrix), the Chapman–Kolmogorov result the
[Markov-chains appendix](../../appendices/advanced-probability/markov-chains.qmd) develops.

When the states are observed, estimation is just counting. The maximum-likelihood estimate of $A_{jk}$ is
the number of observed $j\to k$ transitions divided by the number of transitions out of $j$ [@murphy_ml].
The trap is sparsity. Murphy's example is a word-level language model (an **$n$-gram** model): with about
50,000 words, a bigram model already has about 2.5 billion free parameters, almost all of them unseen in any
training text, and setting an unseen pair's probability to zero is "a severe form of overfitting"
[@murphy_ml]. The fixes are smoothing: **add-one smoothing**, which has a Bayesian justification (a uniform
Dirichlet prior on each row), or **deleted interpolation**, a weighted blend of the bigram and unigram
frequencies, which Murphy shows is close to the posterior mean under a shared hierarchical Dirichlet prior
[@murphy_ml]. That is [hierarchical shrinkage](../02-inference/hierarchical-bayesian-models.qmd) applied to
the rows of a transition matrix.

## Now hide the state

An HMM keeps the chain but hides it. There is a discrete hidden state $z_t\in\{1,\dots,K\}$ following a
Markov chain, plus an **observation model** $p(x_t\mid z_t)$: a table of symbol probabilities if the
emissions are discrete, or a state-specific Gaussian if they are continuous [@murphy_ml]. Murphy's 2-D
Gaussian example shows the typical look of HMM data: several observations land near the same place, then
there is a sudden jump to a new cluster [@murphy_ml].

Two things follow. First, the observations themselves are **not** Markov: the hidden chain carries
information across long stretches, so an HMM can represent long-range dependence that a visible Markov
model cannot [@murphy_ml]. Second, the states can be given meaning (the spoken word behind an acoustic
signal, the part of speech behind a word, whether a stretch of DNA codes for a gene), and the job becomes
*inferring* them [@murphy_ml].

### The occasionally dishonest casino

Murphy's running example, taken from Durbin et al., is a casino that mostly rolls a **fair** die but
occasionally switches to a **loaded** one that favours six, for a short stretch [@murphy_ml]. You see only
the faces. The state (which die) is hidden; the faces are the emissions; the switching is the Markov chain.

Here is the logic of the forward pass done by hand, with illustrative numbers of our own: the casino stays
with the fair die with probability 0.95 per roll and with the loaded die with probability 0.90; the loaded
die shows a six half the time. In the long run the loaded die is in use one third of the time, so that is
the starting belief.

| Step | Belief "loaded" before the roll | Roll | Belief "loaded" after the roll |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0.333 | 6 | **0.600** |
| 2 | 0.560 | 6 | **0.792** |
| 3 | 0.724 | 6 | **0.887** |
| 4 | 0.804 | 1 | **0.711** |

Each row does two things. **Predict**: push yesterday's belief through the transition matrix (0.600 becomes
0.560, because the die might have switched). **Update**: weigh the new roll by how likely each die is to
produce it, using Bayes' rule (a six is three times likelier from the loaded die, so belief jumps). Three
sixes in a row make "loaded" likely; one ordinary roll pulls it back only partly, because states persist.
That two-step cycle is the whole forward algorithm.

## Five questions you can ask

With the parameters known, an HMM answers several distinct questions, and confusing them is a common source
of error [@murphy_ml]:

- **Filtering**: $p(z_t\mid x_{1:t})$, the belief about the current state using only data up to now. This
  is the online, real-time answer. It reduces noise compared with judging each observation alone.
- **Smoothing**: $p(z_t\mid x_{1:T})$, the belief about state $t$ using *all* the data, past and future.
  Murphy's analogy is a detective: confusion lasts until the key clue turns up, after which the earlier
  observations make sense in hindsight [@murphy_ml]. **Fixed-lag smoothing** sits between the two: wait
  $\ell$ steps, then commit, trading delay for accuracy.
- **Prediction**: $p(z_{t+h}\mid x_{1:t})$, obtained by applying the transition matrix $h$ more times to the
  current belief; summing over the emission model turns it into a forecast of future observations
  [@murphy_ml].
- **MAP decoding (Viterbi)**: the single most probable *whole path* $z_{1:T}$.
- **Probability of the evidence**: $p(x_{1:T})$, summed over all hidden paths, which lets you classify
  sequences, cluster them, or flag anomalies [@murphy_ml].

```{python}
#| label: fig-hmm-casino
#| fig-cap: "The dishonest casino, simulated and decoded by hand (300 rolls; switching and loaded-die probabilities are our illustrative choices). Grey bands mark the stretches when the loaded die was really in use. Top: the filtered belief p(loaded | rolls so far), which reacts late and jitters. Middle: the smoothed belief p(loaded | all rolls), which uses the future too and so has cleaner edges. Bottom: the single most probable path from Viterbi. The legend counts misclassified rolls (threshold 0.5) against the true die for this one simulated sequence."
#| code-fold: true
#| code-summary: "Show the HMM code (simulate, forward, forward-backward, Viterbi)"

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
A = np.array([[0.95, 0.05],          # fair   -> fair, loaded
              [0.10, 0.90]])         # loaded -> fair, loaded
B = np.vstack([np.full(6, 1/6),                    # fair die
               np.r_[np.full(5, 0.1), 0.5]])       # loaded die: six half the time
pi0 = np.array([1.0, 0.0])
T = 300

# simulate the hidden die and the observed faces
z = np.zeros(T, int); x = np.zeros(T, int)
for t in range(T):
    if t > 0:
        z[t] = rng.choice(2, p=A[z[t-1]])
    x[t] = rng.choice(6, p=B[z[t]])

# forward pass (filtering): predict, then update, normalising each step
alpha = np.zeros((T, 2))
u = pi0 * B[:, x[0]]; alpha[0] = u / u.sum()
for t in range(1, T):
    u = B[:, x[t]] * (A.T @ alpha[t-1]); alpha[t] = u / u.sum()

# backward pass, then combine (smoothing)
beta = np.ones((T, 2))
for t in range(T-1, 0, -1):
    v = A @ (B[:, x[t]] * beta[t]); beta[t-1] = v / v.sum()
gamma = alpha * beta; gamma /= gamma.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)

# Viterbi in logs: max instead of sum, then trace back
logd = np.log(pi0 + 1e-300) + np.log(B[:, x[0]])
back = np.zeros((T, 2), int)
for t in range(1, T):
    s = logd[:, None] + np.log(A)
    back[t] = s.argmax(axis=0)
    logd = s.max(axis=0) + np.log(B[:, x[t]])
path = np.zeros(T, int); path[-1] = logd.argmax()
for t in range(T-1, 0, -1):
    path[t-1] = back[t, path[t]]

err_f = int(((alpha[:, 1] > 0.5) != z).sum())
err_s = int(((gamma[:, 1] > 0.5) != z).sum())
err_v = int((path != z).sum())

fig, axes = plt.subplots(3, 1, figsize=(8.6, 6.2), sharex=True)
rolls = np.arange(1, T + 1)
for ax in axes:
    ax.fill_between(rolls, 0, 1, where=z == 1, step="mid", color="#e4e3d8", lw=0)
    ax.set_ylim(-0.05, 1.05)
axes[0].plot(rolls, alpha[:, 1], color="#5e5e54", lw=1.3,
             label=f"filtered: {err_f}/{T} wrong")
axes[0].set_ylabel("p(loaded)")
axes[1].plot(rolls, gamma[:, 1], color="#e8531d", lw=1.6,
             label=f"smoothed: {err_s}/{T} wrong")
axes[1].set_ylabel("p(loaded)")
axes[2].step(rolls, path, where="mid", color="#161613", lw=1.4,
             label=f"Viterbi path: {err_v}/{T} wrong")
axes[2].set_yticks([0, 1]); axes[2].set_yticklabels(["fair", "loaded"])
axes[2].set_xlabel("roll number")
for ax in axes:
    ax.legend(loc="upper right", fontsize=8, frameon=False)
fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

## The forward algorithm: Bayes' rule on a loop

The table above is the [forward algorithm](#sec-hmm-math). Each step **predicts** with the transition matrix
and **updates** with the new observation's likelihood, then normalises; the normalising constants, logged
and summed, give the log-likelihood of the whole sequence as a free by-product [@murphy_ml]. Murphy calls
this the **predict–update cycle**, and the vector of $K$ filtered probabilities the **belief state**
[@murphy_ml]. It is the discrete twin of the Kalman filter's predict-then-correct step, and like it, it runs
online in one pass.

Two practical notes from Murphy. Multiply enough probabilities and the numbers underflow to zero, so real
code normalises at every step or works in logs [@murphy_ml]. And the cost is $O(K^2T)$: linear in the length
of the sequence, quadratic in the number of states [@murphy_ml]. Summing over the $K^T$ possible hidden
paths directly would be hopeless; the recursion makes it cheap.

## Forward–backward: hindsight

To smooth, run a second recursion from the end of the sequence backwards. It computes, for each state at time
$t$, how well that state explains the *future* observations. Multiply that by the filtered (past) belief and
normalise, and you have $p(z_t\mid x_{1:T})$ [@murphy_ml]. Murphy describes it as passing messages left to
right, then right to left, and combining them at each node, the special case of the general **belief
propagation** algorithm for graphical models [@murphy_ml].

In the figure the smoothed curve (middle) switches more cleanly than the filtered one (top). The filter has
to wait for evidence to pile up before it believes a switch has happened, which is the right behaviour for
a decision made in real time. The smoother can see the run of sixes that followed and revise the earlier
rolls.

## Viterbi: the single best story

Sometimes you need one coherent answer rather than a probability at each step: the transcript of a speech
signal, or a single segmentation of a sequence into regimes. The **Viterbi algorithm** finds the jointly most
probable path. It uses the same recursion as the forward pass with "sum" replaced by "max", and it remembers,
for each state, which predecessor got it there, so the best path can be traced back from the end
[@murphy_ml]. Murphy's speech example traces it on a three-state model: $\delta_1(1)=0.5$; then
$0.5\times0.3\times0.3=0.045$ for staying in state 1 against $0.5\times0.7\times0.2=0.07$ for moving to
state 2, and so on, ending in the path 1, 2, 2, 3 [@murphy_ml].

The most probable *path* is not the same as the sequence of individually most probable *states*. Murphy's
two-step example [@murphy_ml]:

|  | $X_1=0$ | $X_1=1$ | total |
|---|---|---|---|
| $X_2=0$ | 0.04 | 0.30 | 0.34 |
| $X_2=1$ | **0.36** | 0.30 | 0.66 |
| total | 0.40 | 0.60 | |

The most probable joint cell is $(0,1)$ at 0.36. But the marginally most probable value of $X_1$ is 1 (0.60)
and of $X_2$ is 1 (0.66), giving $(1,1)$, a combination with probability only 0.30. Viterbi's answer is
always **globally consistent**: Murphy's example is "recognize speech" misheard as "wreck a nice beach",
where "beach" might win locally but loses once the whole utterance has to make linguistic sense
[@murphy_ml]. Thresholding the smoothed marginals is **more robust** per step, because each state averages
over its neighbours instead of conditioning on one guess about them; it is also the rule that minimises the
expected number of wrongly labelled steps [@murphy_ml].

In Murphy's casino run, thresholding gave 71 errors in 300 rolls for filtering, 49 for smoothing and 60 for
Viterbi [@murphy_ml]. In our simulated sequence above, Viterbi happens to edge out smoothing. That is not a
contradiction: smoothing minimises errors *in expectation*, and any single sequence can fall either way.
Filtering is the worst of the three in both, because it uses the least information.

## Learning an HMM: Baum–Welch

If you have training sequences where the hidden states were labelled, fitting is counting again: transitions
from the state labels, emission probabilities from the times each state produced each symbol [@murphy_ml].
Usually the states are never seen, and you are in the same position as with a mixture model. The standard
answer is EM, known for HMMs as the **Baum–Welch algorithm** [@murphy_ml]:

- **E-step**: run forward–backward with the current parameters to get, for every time step, the probability
  of being in each state and the probability of each **transition** between consecutive steps. Summed over
  time these are *expected counts*.
- **M-step**: re-estimate exactly as if those expected counts were real counts. The new $A_{jk}$ is the
  expected number of $j\to k$ transitions divided by the expected number of transitions out of $j$; Gaussian
  emission means are posterior-weighted averages [@murphy_ml].

Everything you know about [EM for mixtures](mixture-models-and-em.qmd) carries over. It climbs to a **local**
optimum, so initialisation matters: use some labelled data, or fit a plain mixture first and ignore the
dynamics, or use several random restarts [@murphy_ml]. Murphy warns against starting with **Viterbi
training** (using only the single best path in the E-step): early on the parameters are poor and the best
path is fairly arbitrary, so it is safer to start with forward–backward and switch near convergence
[@murphy_ml]. A Bayesian alternative samples whole hidden paths (forwards filtering, backwards sampling)
inside a Gibbs sampler, and must then deal with **label switching** just as mixtures do [@murphy_ml].

**How many states?** As with the number of mixture components: grid-search $K$ and compare cross-validated
likelihood or BIC, or use Bayesian methods that switch off unneeded states [@murphy_ml]. The training
likelihood alone will always prefer more states.

## Stretching the model

The basic HMM makes two strong assumptions, and Murphy surveys the standard ways to relax them
[@murphy_ml]:

- **How long a regime lasts.** With a self-transition probability $A_{ii}$, the time spent in a state is
  **geometric**: the single most likely stay is one step, and long stays become exponentially rarer
  [@murphy_ml]. Real regimes often do not behave like that. A **hidden semi-Markov model** gives each state an
  explicit duration distribution. A cheaper trick chains $n$ copies of a state with the same emissions,
  which turns the geometric duration into a **negative binomial** that can have a hump [@murphy_ml].
- **Observations that depend on each other.** The basic HMM assumes the observations are independent given
  the state. The **auto-regressive HMM**, also called a **regime-switching Markov model**, lets $x_t$ depend
  on $x_{t-1}$ through a regression whose coefficients change with the hidden state. Murphy notes these are
  widely used in econometrics, citing Hamilton (1990) [@murphy_ml]; fitting them is weighted least squares,
  one regression per regime, weighted by the smoothed state probabilities. This is the model behind the
  [Markov-switching section](../04-predictive/nonlinear-time-series.qmd#markov-switching-regimes-you-cannot-see)
  of the time-series track.
- **Inputs.** An **input-output HMM** lets covariates drive the switching: the transition probabilities
  become a logistic regression on the inputs, with coefficients that depend on the previous state
  [@murphy_ml].
- **Several interacting sequences.** A **coupled HMM** links chains so that each one's next state depends on
  its neighbours' current states. The leaner **influence model** writes each chain's transition as a weighted
  blend of pairwise influences, where each weight says how much one chain sways another. It has been used to
  model **conversational interaction between people** [@murphy_ml].

## Common pitfalls

::: {.callout-warning title="Where this goes wrong"}
- **Treating states as discovered facts.** The model returns states 1, 2, 3; the labels "recession",
  "engaged" or "loaded" are your reading of their emission distributions. Labels are arbitrary across runs
  (label switching), and a different $K$ can carve the sequence differently. [@murphy_ml]
- **Using smoothed states for a real-time decision.** Smoothing conditions on future observations
  [@murphy_ml]. A backtest that acts on $p(z_t\mid x_{1:T})$ at time $t$ is using information it would not
  have had; the honest real-time quantity is the filtered belief.
- **Reading the Viterbi path as a list of individually likely states.** It is the most probable *joint*
  path; a step on it can be less probable, taken alone, than the alternative. For per-step accuracy,
  threshold the smoothed marginals. [@murphy_ml]
- **Ignoring the geometric-duration assumption.** A plain HMM thinks the most likely regime length is one
  step. If your regimes have typical lengths, use a semi-Markov or expanded-state model. [@murphy_ml]
- **One EM run, or K picked by training likelihood.** Baum–Welch finds local optima and more states always
  fit better in-sample; restart several times and choose $K$ on held-out likelihood or BIC. [@murphy_ml]
- **Multiplying raw probabilities.** Over a long sequence they underflow to zero; normalise at each step or
  work in logs. [@murphy_ml]
:::

## Appendix · the mathematics {#sec-hmm-math .unnumbered}

::: {.math-appendix}

**The model.** With initial distribution $\pi$, transition matrix $A_{ij}=p(z_t=j\mid z_{t-1}=i)$ and
emission model $\phi_t(j)=p(x_t\mid z_t=j)$,
$$
p(z_{1:T},x_{1:T}) = p(z_1)\prod_{t=2}^{T}p(z_t\mid z_{t-1})\prod_{t=1}^{T}p(x_t\mid z_t).
$$

**Forward (filtering).** Let $\alpha_t(j)=p(z_t=j\mid x_{1:t})$. Predict, then update:
$$
\alpha_t \;\propto\; \phi_t \odot \big(A^\top \alpha_{t-1}\big),\qquad
Z_t = \sum_j \phi_t(j)\,[A^\top\alpha_{t-1}]_j = p(x_t\mid x_{1:t-1}),
$$
with $\alpha_1\propto \phi_1\odot\pi$. The log-likelihood is $\log p(x_{1:T})=\sum_t \log Z_t$.

**Backward and smoothing.** Let $\beta_t(i)=p(x_{t+1:T}\mid z_t=i)$, with $\beta_T=\mathbf 1$ and
$$
\beta_{t-1} = A\,\big(\phi_t\odot\beta_t\big).
$$
Conditioning on $z_t$ splits the chain into past and future, so
$\gamma_t(j)=p(z_t=j\mid x_{1:T})\propto \alpha_t(j)\,\beta_t(j)$. The two-slice marginal needed by EM is
$\xi_{t,t+1}(i,j)\propto \alpha_t(i)\,A_{ij}\,\phi_{t+1}(j)\,\beta_{t+1}(j)$.

**Viterbi.** Let $\delta_t(j)=\max_{z_{1:t-1}}p(z_{1:t-1},z_t=j\mid x_{1:t})$. The best path to $j$ at $t$
extends the best path to some $i$ at $t-1$:
$$
\delta_t(j)=\max_i\,\delta_{t-1}(i)\,A_{ij}\,\phi_t(j),\qquad a_t(j)=\arg\max_i\,\delta_{t-1}(i)\,A_{ij}\,\phi_t(j),
$$
with $\delta_1(j)=\pi_j\phi_1(j)$, $z_T^*=\arg\max_i\delta_T(i)$ and traceback $z_t^*=a_{t+1}(z_{t+1}^*)$.
Because $\log\max=\max\log$, the recursion runs exactly in logs.

**Baum–Welch M-step.** With expected counts from the E-step,
$$
\hat A_{jk}=\frac{\sum_t \xi_{t-1,t}(j,k)}{\sum_{k'}\sum_t \xi_{t-1,t}(j,k')},\qquad
\hat\pi_k=\gamma_1(k),\qquad
\hat\mu_k=\frac{\sum_t\gamma_t(k)\,x_t}{\sum_t\gamma_t(k)}
$$
(summed over sequences when there are several); the expected complete-data log-likelihood separates into a
start term, a transition term and an emission term, each maximised on its own.

**Durations.** Staying exactly $d$ steps in state $i$ has probability $(1-A_{ii})A_{ii}^{\,d-1}$, a geometric
law with mean $1/(1-A_{ii})$; in the casino above that is 20 rolls for the fair die and 10 for the loaded
one. Chaining $n$ copies with self-loop $p$ gives $p(d)=\binom{d-1}{n-1}p^{\,d-n}(1-p)^n$, a negative
binomial.

**The worked table, checked.** With $A=\begin{pmatrix}0.95&0.05\\0.10&0.90\end{pmatrix}$ the
stationary share of the loaded die solves $p=0.05(1-p)+0.9p$, so $p=1/3$. After a six:
$\frac{(1/3)(0.5)}{(2/3)(1/6)+(1/3)(0.5)}=0.600$; predict $0.6(0.9)+0.4(0.05)=0.560$; and so on down the
table.

:::
