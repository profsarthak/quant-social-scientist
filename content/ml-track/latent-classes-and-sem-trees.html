Searching for Groups: Latent Classes, Mixtures & SEM Trees

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source

Is your sample one population or several? Mixture models look for groups nobody measured; SEM trees look for groups defined by things you did measure. This entry covers both, how to decide how many groups there are, and what each answer can and cannot tell you.

There are two ways to ask whether a sample hides subgroups. A mixture model (latent class analysis, latent profile analysis, factor and growth mixtures) assumes the groups are unobserved and infers each person’s probability of belonging to each one. A SEM tree assumes the groups are defined by observed covariates and searches for the splits (“conscientiousness item ≥ 2”) that most improve the fit of a structural equation model. Mixtures are more flexible and will usually fit better; trees are easier to describe. Neither tells you the groups are real.

Why it matters

Social and behavioural researchers sort people into groups all the time. Jacobucci, Grimm and Zhang’s example is the CES-D depression scale: sum the items, call everyone above a cutoff (say, more than 16) “depressed”, and use that label as an outcome or a predictor (Jacobucci et al. 2023). The cutoff can be tuned with ROC curves against a clinical interview, but the procedure still has three problems. It treats every item as equally related to depression. It ignores measurement error. And it assigns people to groups deterministically, with no allowance for misclassification, which changes the associations you then estimate with the label (Jacobucci et al. 2023).

Underneath is a more general question: did the sample come from one population (homogeneity) or from several (heterogeneity) (Jacobucci et al. 2023)? Jacobucci and colleagues treat two families of methods as the most useful answers for social and behavioural research, finite mixture models and SEM trees, and set them side by side in a chapter they call Searching for Groups (Jacobucci et al. 2023). This entry follows that chapter.

Two ways to look for groups

The key question is where you think the heterogeneity comes from (Jacobucci et al. 2023):

Groups come from… Membership is… Example question
Mixture model an unobserved categorical variable probabilistic: each person has a probability for each class “Are there distinct response types in this grit scale?”
SEM tree values of observed covariates deterministic, by covariate value “Which measured traits split people into groups whose factor model differs?”
Multiple-group SEM a grouping you specify in advance deterministic “Does the model differ between men and women?” (a test, not a search)

Jacobucci and colleagues label mixtures and trees atheoretical (they search) and multiple-group SEM theoretical (it tests a grouping you chose), and describe multiple-group SEM as a generalisation of ANOVA: a theory-given grouping, with any SEM in place of a single continuous outcome (Jacobucci et al. 2023). The vocabulary differs as well: mixture models produce classes, trees produce groups (Jacobucci et al. 2023).

Cluster analysis (such as k-means) is the third relative. It is used much like a mixture, but it assigns people deterministically, whereas a mixture assigns probabilities and can handle binary, ordinal, categorical, count and continuous data in one model (Jacobucci et al. 2023). Bruce and Bruce make the same contrast from the data-science side: k-means and hierarchical clustering are heuristics built on distance, with “no probability model involved”, while model-based clustering is “grounded in statistical theory” and gives “more rigorous ways to determine the nature and number of clusters” (Bruce et al. 2020).

Mixture models, named by what is inside each class

A finite mixture says each person’s data come from one of K hidden classes, each with its own distribution, blended in proportions \phi_k (Jacobucci et al. 2023):

f(y_i) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \phi_k\, f_k(y_i\mid\theta_k).

In plain words: the overall distribution is a weighted average of the class distributions, and the weights are the class sizes.

Jacobucci and colleagues illustrate it with two Normal curves weighted 0.9 and 0.1. With two classes and a Normal in each, the model estimates five parameters: a mean and variance per class plus one mixing proportion, usually estimated as an intercept with one class as the baseline (Jacobucci et al. 2023). Once it is fitted, each person gets posterior probabilities of membership in each class (Jacobucci et al. 2023). The EM machinery that does the fitting is in mixture models & EM.

The flexibility is in what model sits inside each class, and each choice has its own name (Jacobucci et al. 2023):

  • Latent class analysis (LCA) has binary or ordinal items and a single class variable, with conditional independence: within a class the items are uncorrelated, so the class variable is the only reason items correlate in the population. The parameters are item thresholds per class plus the class intercepts.
  • Latent profile analysis (LPA) applies the same idea to continuous items, estimating means and variances per class. Modern software lets you relax conditional independence and allow correlations within class, equal or free across classes, and even equal for classes 1 and 2 but free for class 3.
  • A factor mixture model (Lubke & Muthén) puts a factor model inside each class.
  • A growth mixture model (Muthén & Shedden) puts a growth curve inside each class.

These used to be separate techniques with separate software. Folding them into one general latent-variable framework (Mplus, Latent GOLD, OpenMx in R) made them variations on one model (Jacobucci et al. 2023).

Murphy’s mixture of Bernoullis is LCA under another name: binary data, a class variable, and pixels treated as independent within a class. Fitted with K=10 to unlabelled handwritten digits, it recovers some of the digits but splits others into several clusters and misses some entirely. Murphy’s reasons transfer directly to survey items: the within-class model is too simple (“each pixel is treated independently”), and some “true” categories really contain several styles, such as sevens with and without the crossbar (Murphy 2012).

A worked example: Gaussian clusters in stock returns

Bruce and Bruce fit model-based clustering (R’s mclust, a mixture of multivariate Normals) to daily returns of two oil stocks, ExxonMobil and Chevron, from 2011 on (1,131 days) (Bruce et al. 2020). It chose two components, with 963 days in one and 168 in the other. The surprise is what the components are. They do not sit side by side like k-means clusters. Their means are about the same, and the second component is a wide cloud surrounding the first. Stock returns have longer tails than a Normal, so the mixture covers the bulk of the days with one Normal and the extreme days with a second, more spread-out one (Bruce et al. 2020).

The model mclust picked is called VEE, “ellipsoidal, equal shape and orientation”, and you can check what that means from the printed covariances. Every entry of the second matrix is 3.49 times the corresponding entry of the first, so both components have the same correlation (0.75). They differ only in size (our arithmetic on Bruce and Bruce’s output). mclust gets there by fitting 14 different covariance structures at every K and choosing among all of them by BIC (Bruce et al. 2020). A two-component answer here is a statement about tail shape. It does not say there are two kinds of trading day.

How many classes?

In a mixture, both the number of classes and the parameters within each class are unknown. The usual approach is to fix the type of within-class model and search over the rest (Jacobucci et al. 2023). Following Ram and Grimm, Jacobucci and colleagues recommend choosing a few theoretically motivated specifications in advance, fitting each with more and more classes until the model stops converging or stops improving, and then comparing the whole set on fit, sensitivity, substantive meaning and classification quality (Jacobucci et al. 2023).

Information criteria. With deviance -2LL and p parameters, \text{AIC}=-2LL+2p and \text{BIC}=-2LL+\ln(N)\,p; lower is better (Jacobucci et al. 2023). BIC charges \ln N per parameter instead of 2, so it favours smaller models once N exceeds seven. Both can compare non-nested models, so they work across different numbers of classes and different constraints (Jacobucci et al. 2023). Murphy explains why BIC is the default. The principled Bayesian choice is the K with the largest marginal likelihood, but for latent-variable models that is hard to compute, and BIC is the simple approximation (citing Fraley & Raftery) (Murphy 2012). A trap with software: mclust reports BIC with the sign flipped and picks the largest value (Bruce et al. 2020).

Likelihood-ratio tests. A K-class and a (K{-}1)-class model are nested, but their deviance difference is not \chi^2-distributed under the null. The corrected versions are the Vuong–Lo–Mendell–Rubin and Lo–Mendell–Rubin adjusted tests, and the bootstrap LRT simulates from the smaller model to build the null distribution directly (Jacobucci et al. 2023).

Cross-validation. Fit on k-1 folds and score fit on the held-out fold (Grimm, Mazza & Davoudzadeh) (Jacobucci et al. 2023). Murphy explains why this works for mixtures but not for k-means. Reconstruction error keeps falling as K grows, while a mixture’s held-out likelihood eventually turns back up (Murphy 2012).

Simulation studies have not found one index that wins across latent class, latent profile and growth mixture models, sample sizes and class separations. Jacobucci and colleagues therefore read the evidence in sequence: information criteria, then approximate LRTs, then substantive interpretation and class separation, with cross-validation as a sensitivity check (Jacobucci et al. 2023).

Classification quality: entropy. Entropy summarises how decisively the posterior probabilities sort people (Jacobucci et al. 2023):

\text{Entropy} = 1 + \frac{1}{N\ln K}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{k=1}^{K} P(C_i=k\mid y_i)\,\ln P(C_i=k\mid y_i).

In plain words: 1 means everyone is assigned with certainty; 0 means the class labels are no better than coin flips.

Take a toy two-class model with four people (our numbers). If their probabilities of class 1 are 0.95, 0.90, 0.20 and 0.55, entropy is 0.38. The person at 0.55 is nearly a coin flip, and even 0.90 and 0.80 leave real doubt. If the four are 0.99, 0.98, 0.01 and 0.97, entropy is 0.88. Some researchers want at least 0.80 before taking a solution seriously, but Jacobucci and colleagues note “no strong theoretical grounds” for that number (Jacobucci et al. 2023). High entropy also does not show there are classes. It only shows the fitted classes are well separated.

Finally, fit every model from several sets of starting values. Mixture likelihoods have local optima, and restarts both help find the global maximum and show whether different starts give the same answer (Jacobucci et al. 2023).

The grit example

Jacobucci and colleagues ran a latent class analysis on the 12 items of the Grit scale for a random 500 respondents, treating items as ordinal with five categories, estimating 1–5 classes with 10 starts each, and leaving covariates out while choosing K (Jacobucci et al. 2023):

Classes Deviance Parameters AIC BIC Entropy
1 17,636 48 17,732 17,934 –
2 16,525 97 16,719 17,128 0.85
3 16,184 146 16,476 17,091 0.87
4 15,925* 195 16,315 17,137 0.87
5 15,721 244 16,209 17,238 0.86

The parameter counts follow directly. One class needs 4 thresholds × 12 items = 48, and each extra class adds another 48 plus one class intercept (Jacobucci et al. 2023). *The book prints the 4-class deviance as 16,925, but its own AIC and BIC both imply 15,925 (16,315 − 2 × 195 = 15,925, and adding ln(500) × 195 gives the printed BIC of 17,137). We have corrected the typo. The AIC keeps falling through five classes, while the BIC bottoms out at three. Jacobucci and colleagues side with BIC, because in their experience AIC does better in small samples and BIC in larger ones, and they report three classes of about 50%, 20% and 30% (Jacobucci et al. 2023).

The classes are read from item-response probabilities. Class 1 (half the sample) scores high on nearly every item. Class 2 reports interests and projects changing from year to year, which the authors read as easily distracted, with average commitment. Class 3 scores low on diligence and overcoming setbacks, but its interests change less than class 2’s (Jacobucci et al. 2023). Adding the Big Five as predictors of class membership barely moved the classes. More conscientious (t=7.25), agreeable (t=4.18) and less neurotic (t=3.09) people were more likely to be in class 1 than class 2, and more open people (t=3.68) were more likely to be in class 2 than class 3 (Jacobucci et al. 2023).

Factor mixtures: classes defined by a measurement model

When the indicators come from the same scale, or are the same measure over time, it often makes sense to model their covariance with latent factors and look for classes with respect to that factor model. A factor mixture model therefore combines two kinds of latent variable: a categorical one for the classes and a continuous one for the common factor (Jacobucci et al. 2023). The analyst decides which parameters may differ across classes. Freeing everything is the most flexible option and also the hardest to estimate. A common compromise lets only the factor means differ, fixing them at zero in a reference class (Jacobucci et al. 2023).

The example Jacobucci and colleagues recommend is Arias et al.’s detection of careless responders. In the majority class every loading was fixed at 1. In the minority class the loadings were +1 or −1 according to item wording, which is what you would see from someone answering every item the same way even though the negatively worded items had been reverse-scored. About 4–10% of cases fell in that inattentive class (Jacobucci et al. 2023).

Applied to the two-factor Grit model (N ≈ 1,000), a two-class means-only model beat one class on AIC and BIC, though entropy was low (0.62). Freeing the factor variances and covariance improved every index but produced a negative variance, an improper solution. A three-class means-only model improved again (Jacobucci et al. 2023). In the two-class model the class intercept was −1.05 on the log-odds scale. That puts about 26% of people in class 1 (our conversion: 1/(1+e^{1.05})). Class 1’s factor means were −0.32 (SE 0.10) and −0.98 (0.07) relative to class 2, so it is lower on both grit facets (Jacobucci et al. 2023).

Covariates: let them form the classes, or only describe them?

Adding covariates is easy. Deciding what they should do is the hard part (Jacobucci et al. 2023). If covariates predict the factors (making the CFA a MIMIC model), they help form the classes. If you want the classes fixed first and the covariates only to describe them, use the three-step approach (Asparouhov & Muthén): estimate the classes, compute classification-uncertainty rates, then run a multinomial logistic regression of class on the covariates that accounts for that uncertainty (Jacobucci et al. 2023). With all 62 Grit-data variables as covariates the model was inadmissible. Using five Big Five factor scores instead, every trait except neuroticism predicted class membership, and conscientiousness had the largest effect (−2.143, SE 0.239) (Jacobucci et al. 2023). As an odds ratio, a 1-SD increase in conscientiousness multiplies the odds of being in the low-grit class by about 0.12 (our conversion).

SEM trees: groups defined by observed covariates

A decision tree does two things. It fits nonlinear relationships, and it puts every observation into a terminal node, so each node is a subgroup (Jacobucci et al. 2023). SEM trees (Brandmaier et al., 2013) use the second feature. The outcome is a whole structural equation model, and the tree looks for covariate splits that improve that model’s fit, scored by deviance. Jacobucci and colleagues call it exploratory multiple-group modelling (Jacobucci et al. 2023). Repeated splits can capture interactions among covariates and highly nonlinear step-function structure. The cost is the usual cost of a single tree: instability and less-than-optimal prediction (Jacobucci et al. 2023). The same idea, with a mixed-effects model in place of an SEM, appears in ML with clustered data.

Demonstration. With the two-factor Grit CFA as the outcome model and every Grit-data covariate as a candidate splitter, a one-split SEM tree on N=1{,}000 splits on C8, “I shirk my duties”, between responses 1 and 2. The likelihood-ratio gain is 379.7 on 40 df, because all 40 parameters of the CFA may differ across the two groups (13 free loadings, 12 residual variances, 3 factor variances and covariance, 12 intercepts) (Jacobucci et al. 2023). The authors read the right-hand group as higher in conscientiousness. It has higher loadings on the first grit factor and lower ones on the second. You then compare the two groups’ parameters to see what drives the split (Jacobucci et al. 2023).

Tuning. Jacobucci and colleagues stress that these settings should follow best practice and theory, not be tuned by trial and error (Jacobucci et al. 2023):

  • Split criterion. The naive method tries every cutpoint and keeps the best likelihood ratio. Cross-validation scores each cutpoint on held-out folds and is very slow. The fair criterion, which they recommend, uses half the sample to choose each covariate’s best cutpoint and the other half to compare covariates. This reduces the usual tree bias towards predictors with many possible cutpoints.
  • Minimum node size. An SEM is estimated in every node, so set a minimum (100 is a reasonable default). SEM trees are a large-sample technique.
  • Maximum depth. With very large samples (over about 5,000) trees grow too big to read, so cap the depth.

Asking about the latent variables only: invariance first

If the question is “which covariates explain differences in the constructs?”, the groups must measure the same constructs. That requires measurement invariance: equality constraints on loadings, intercepts and residual variances, so that only the factor means, variances and covariance can differ across groups (Jacobucci et al. 2023). One complication is that in a mixture you fix a reference class’s factor means at zero, but an SEM tree has no reference group. The workaround is to centre every observed variable, fix the intercepts at zero (which is roughly where they would be estimated anyway), and read each group’s factor means against the whole-sample mean of zero (Jacobucci et al. 2023).

Under strict invariance only 5 parameters per group are free (two factor variances, their covariance and two factor means). The Grit tree again splits first on C8 (LR 153.3, 5 df), then on C1, “I am always prepared”, then on N10, “I often feel blue” (Jacobucci et al. 2023):

SEM forests

Single trees are unstable, so as with random forests the remedy is to grow hundreds of SEM trees on random subsets of covariates (mtry) and summarise variable importance: the permutation-based drop in log-likelihood, averaged across trees (Jacobucci et al. 2023). Variables that formed the single tree should rank high, and here they did. With mtry = 20 and 100 trees, a few conscientiousness items dominated, with C8 the most important, and most variables scored near zero. With mtry = 2 and 500 trees the importances spread out much more evenly, because each tree sees only two candidates and weaker variables get chosen (Jacobucci et al. 2023). Jacobucci and colleagues explain the trade-off: low mtry gives a more marginal reading of importance, and high mtry a more conditional one, favouring variables that still matter when others compete (Jacobucci et al. 2023). Runtimes are long. SEM trees take minutes to hours, and SEM forests take hours (run them in parallel) (Jacobucci et al. 2023).

import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(10)

# ---------- Left: latent class analysis by EM (mixture of Bernoullis) ----------
N, J = 500, 8
pi_true = np.array([0.5, 0.2, 0.3])
theta_true = np.array([[0.85]*8,                       # "high on everything"
                       [0.80]*4 + [0.25]*4,            # "high on half"
                       [0.20]*8])                      # "low on everything"
z = rng.choice(3, size=N, p=pi_true)
Y = (rng.random((N, J)) < theta_true[z]).astype(float)

def fit_lca(Y, K, rng, iters=300):
    n = len(Y)
    pi = rng.dirichlet(np.ones(K))
    th = rng.uniform(0.2, 0.8, size=(K, Y.shape[1]))
    for _ in range(iters):
        logp = np.log(pi) + Y @ np.log(th).T + (1 - Y) @ np.log(1 - th).T   # n x K
        m = logp.max(axis=1, keepdims=True)
        ll = float((m.ravel() + np.log(np.exp(logp - m).sum(axis=1))).sum())
        r = np.exp(logp - m); r /= r.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)              # E-step
        nk = r.sum(axis=0)
        pi = nk / n                                                          # M-step
        th = np.clip((r.T @ Y) / nk[:, None], 1e-4, 1 - 1e-4)
    return ll, r

Ks = range(1, 6)
aic, bic, ent = [], [], []
for K in Ks:
    ll, r = max((fit_lca(Y, K, rng) for _ in range(10)), key=lambda t: t[0])  # 10 restarts
    p = K * J + (K - 1)                      # item probabilities + class intercepts
    aic.append(-2 * ll + 2 * p); bic.append(-2 * ll + np.log(N) * p)
    rr = np.clip(r, 1e-12, 1)
    ent.append(np.nan if K == 1 else 1 + (rr * np.log(rr)).sum() / (N * np.log(K)))
aic, bic = np.array(aic), np.array(bic)
kb = int(np.argmin(bic))

# Outcome "model": two correlated scores (5 parameters: 2 means, 2 variances, 1 covariance)

def gauss_ll(X):
    m = X.mean(axis=0); D = X - m
    Sh = D.T @ D / len(X)                  # ML covariance
    return -0.5 * len(X) * (np.log(np.linalg.det(2 * np.pi * Sh)) + 2)

ll0 = gauss_ll(X)
def scan(v, min_n=100):
    vals = np.unique(v)

Choosing between them

Jacobucci and colleagues frame the choice by asking whether you believe the heterogeneity comes from unobserved or observed variables (Jacobucci et al. 2023). Their trade-offs:

  • Multivariate (SEM) trees work best when there are meaningful covariates to split on. It is not known how non-Normal outcomes affect the groups they form. They get expensive with many covariates. With a complex outcome model it can be hard to tell which parameters really differ. And they inherit the instability of trees (Jacobucci et al. 2023).
  • Mixtures suit heterogeneity from unobserved variables and will fit better than trees. Non-Normality can cause trouble (they point to Bauer & Curran, 2004). Classes are harder to describe than tree nodes, and the constraints on class variances and covariances are hard to choose (Jacobucci et al. 2023).

You cannot settle the choice with fit statistics. The two models are not nested, and since a mixture is limited only by the number of classes while a tree is limited to splits on covariate values, the mixture will almost always win on information criteria (Jacobucci et al. 2023). When unsure, run both and decide mainly on theoretical grounds. Methods that combine the two (Grimm et al. 2022, the MplusTrees package) and the use of mixtures and trees to detect non-invariance and differential item functioning are active research areas (Jacobucci et al. 2023).

Common pitfalls

WarningWhere this goes wrong
  • Reifying classes. A mixture with K classes can describe a population that is really continuous but non-Normal. Bruce and Bruce’s two “clusters” of stock returns are one heavy-tailed cloud, and Jacobucci and colleagues flag non-Normality (Bauer & Curran) as a source of trouble. Treat classes as a description of the data, not a discovery of kinds of people. (Bruce et al. 2020; Jacobucci et al. 2023)
  • Treating entropy as evidence for classes. Entropy measures separation of the fitted classes. The 0.80 rule of thumb has “no strong theoretical grounds”, and a well-separated solution can still have the wrong K. (Jacobucci et al. 2023)
  • Picking K from one index, or from the naive LRT. The deviance difference between K and K-1 classes is not \chi^2, and simulation studies disagree about which index is best. Read ICs, adjusted or bootstrap LRTs, interpretation and stability together. (Jacobucci et al. 2023)
  • One start. Mixture likelihoods have local optima. Use many starting values and check that the best solutions agree. (Jacobucci et al. 2023)
  • Ignoring improper solutions. A negative variance (as in the free-variance grit FMM) is a warning about the model, not a result to report. (Jacobucci et al. 2023)
  • Letting covariates silently reshape the classes. Covariates entered directly can change which classes form. Check the classes before and after, or use a three-step approach. (Jacobucci et al. 2023)
  • Using hard class labels downstream as if they were known. Assignment is uncertain, and ignoring the posterior probabilities brings back the cutoff-score problem the mixture was meant to fix. (Jacobucci et al. 2023)
  • Reading SEM-tree groups before checking invariance. If loadings may differ, “this group has higher grit” may only mean grit was measured differently there. Constrain the measurement model first. (Jacobucci et al. 2023)
  • Trusting one tree. Splits are unstable and the naive criterion favours covariates with many cutpoints. Use the fair criterion and check the splits against SEM-forest importance. (Jacobucci et al. 2023)
  • Mixing BIC sign conventions. Mplus-style output wants the lowest BIC; mclust reports BIC so that the highest wins. (Jacobucci et al. 2023; Bruce et al. 2020)

Appendix · the mathematics

The latent class likelihood. For person i with J categorical items y_{ij}, conditional independence within class gives

P(y_i) = \sum_{k=1}^{K}\phi_k\prod_{j=1}^{J}P(y_{ij}\mid C_i=k), \qquad P(C_i=k\mid y_i)=\frac{\phi_k\prod_j P(y_{ij}\mid C_i=k)}{P(y_i)}.

The second expression (Bayes’ rule) gives the posterior probabilities that feed entropy and the three-step approach. For binary items, P(y_{ij}\mid C_i=k)=\theta_{kj}^{y_{ij}}(1-\theta_{kj})^{1-y_{ij}}, which is Murphy’s mixture of Bernoullis, and EM updates \theta_{kj} as the responsibility-weighted share of 1s (the figure’s M-step).

Counting parameters. With ordinal items of c categories, each class needs J(c-1) thresholds and the class variable needs K-1 intercepts: p=K\,J(c-1)+K-1. For Grit (J=12, c=5): p=48K+K-1 = 48, 97, 146, 195, 244 for K=1,\dots,5, matching Table 10.1. In the figure (J=8 binary items), p=8K+K-1.

Information criteria. \text{AIC}=-2\ell+2p and \text{BIC}=-2\ell+p\ln N. BIC’s per-parameter penalty exceeds AIC’s when \ln N>2, that is N>e^2\approx7.4. mclust reports 2\ell-p\ln N (the negative), so for Bruce and Bruce’s model 2(-2255.134)-9\ln 1131=-4573.55.

Entropy. Each person contributes \sum_k P_{ik}\ln P_{ik}\in[-\ln K,0]. It is 0 for a certain assignment and -\ln K for a uniform one. Dividing the sum by N\ln K and adding 1 maps the average onto [0,1]. In the toy example, the four people contribute −0.199, −0.325, −0.500 and −0.688, so 1-1.712/(4\ln2)=0.38.

The bootstrap LRT (McLachlan, as described by Jacobucci et al.). (1) Fit the K-1 and K class models and record LR_{obs}=2(\ell_K-\ell_{K-1}). (2) Simulate many datasets from the fitted (K-1)-class model. (3) Fit both models to each and record LR^{*}. (4) The p-value is the share of LR^{*}\ge LR_{obs}. The simulation stands in for the \chi^2 reference distribution, which does not hold for the class-number comparison.

The SEM-tree split statistic. For a node with data D and a candidate split into D_L,D_R,

LR=2\big[\ell(\hat\theta_L;D_L)+\ell(\hat\theta_R;D_R)-\ell(\hat\theta;D)\big],

where each \hat\theta is the SEM’s ML estimate on that subset. The degrees of freedom equal the number of parameters allowed to differ: 40 for the unconstrained grit CFA, 5 under strict invariance (two factor variances, one covariance, two factor means). For the figure’s bivariate Normal, \ell(\hat\theta;D)=-\tfrac n2\big[\ln\det(2\pi\hat\Sigma)+2\big] with \hat\Sigma the ML covariance, so the LR has 5 df. The naive criterion takes the maximum over all cutpoints and covariates. A covariate with many cutpoints gets many chances, so its maximum is inflated. The fair criterion picks the cutpoint on one half of the data and compares covariates on the other half.

The centring trick. If every indicator is centred, \bar y_j=0, the ML intercepts of the pooled model are 0. Fixing them at 0 in every group then identifies each group’s factor means as deviations from the pooled mean. That is why the size-weighted average of the Fig 10.5 group means is 0 on both factors (238(-0.485)+218(0.397)+251(-0.107)+293(0.19)\approx 0).

::: {.lead-kernel}
There are two ways to ask whether a sample hides subgroups. A **mixture model** (latent class analysis,
latent profile analysis, factor and growth mixtures) assumes the groups are **unobserved** and infers each
person's *probability* of belonging to each one. A **SEM tree** assumes the groups are defined by
**observed** covariates and searches for the splits ("conscientiousness item ≥ 2") that most improve the fit
of a structural equation model. Mixtures are more flexible and will usually fit better; trees are easier to
describe. Neither tells you the groups are real.
:::

## Why it matters

Social and behavioural researchers sort people into groups all the time. Jacobucci, Grimm and Zhang's
example is the CES-D depression scale: sum the items, call everyone above a cutoff (say, more than 16)
"depressed", and use that label as an outcome or a predictor [@jacobucci_ml]. The cutoff can be tuned with
ROC curves against a clinical interview, but the procedure still has three problems. It treats every item as
equally related to depression. It ignores measurement error. And it assigns people to groups
**deterministically**, with no allowance for misclassification, which changes the associations you then
estimate with the label [@jacobucci_ml].

Underneath is a more general question: did the sample come from **one population (homogeneity)** or from
**several (heterogeneity)** [@jacobucci_ml]? Jacobucci and colleagues treat two families of methods as the
most useful answers for social and behavioural research, **finite mixture models** and **SEM trees**, and
set them side by side in a chapter they call *Searching for Groups* [@jacobucci_ml]. This entry follows that
chapter.

## Two ways to look for groups {#sec-two-ways}

The key question is **where you think the heterogeneity comes from** [@jacobucci_ml]:

| | Groups come from… | Membership is… | Example question |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Mixture model** | an **unobserved** categorical variable | **probabilistic**: each person has a probability for each class | "Are there distinct response *types* in this grit scale?" |
| **SEM tree** | values of **observed** covariates | deterministic, by covariate value | "Which measured traits split people into groups whose factor model differs?" |
| **Multiple-group SEM** | a grouping **you specify in advance** | deterministic | "Does the model differ between men and women?" (a test, not a search) |

Jacobucci and colleagues label mixtures and trees **atheoretical** (they search) and multiple-group SEM
**theoretical** (it tests a grouping you chose), and describe multiple-group SEM as a generalisation of
ANOVA: a theory-given grouping, with any SEM in place of a single continuous outcome [@jacobucci_ml]. The
vocabulary differs as well: mixture models produce **classes**, trees produce **groups** [@jacobucci_ml].

Cluster analysis (such as [k-means](unsupervised-learning.qmd)) is the third relative. It is used much like a
mixture, but it assigns people deterministically, whereas a mixture assigns probabilities and can handle
binary, ordinal, categorical, count and continuous data in one model [@jacobucci_ml]. Bruce and Bruce make
the same contrast from the data-science side: k-means and hierarchical clustering are heuristics built on
distance, with "no probability model involved", while model-based clustering is "grounded in statistical
theory" and gives "more rigorous ways to determine the nature and number of clusters" [@bruce_practical].

## Mixture models, named by what is inside each class {#sec-mixtures}

A finite mixture says each person's data come from one of $K$ hidden classes, each with its own
distribution, blended in proportions $\phi_k$ [@jacobucci_ml]:

$$
f(y_i) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \phi_k\, f_k(y_i\mid\theta_k).
$$

> **In plain words:** the overall distribution is a weighted average of the class distributions, and the
> weights are the class sizes.

Jacobucci and colleagues illustrate it with two Normal curves weighted 0.9 and 0.1. With two classes and a
Normal in each, the model estimates **five** parameters: a mean and variance per class plus one mixing
proportion, usually estimated as an intercept with one class as the baseline [@jacobucci_ml]. Once it is
fitted, each person gets **posterior probabilities** of membership in each class [@jacobucci_ml]. The EM
machinery that does the fitting is in [mixture models & EM](mixture-models-and-em.qmd).

The flexibility is in **what model sits inside each class**, and each choice has its own name
[@jacobucci_ml]:

- **Latent class analysis (LCA)** has binary or ordinal items and a single class variable, with
  **conditional independence**: within a class the items are uncorrelated, so the class variable is the only
  reason items correlate in the population. The parameters are item thresholds per class plus the class
  intercepts.
- **Latent profile analysis (LPA)** applies the same idea to continuous items, estimating means and variances
  per class. Modern software lets you relax conditional independence and allow correlations within class,
  equal or free across classes, and even equal for classes 1 and 2 but free for class 3.
- A **factor mixture model** (Lubke & Muthén) puts a factor model inside each class.
- A **growth mixture model** (Muthén & Shedden) puts a growth curve inside each class.

These used to be separate techniques with separate software. Folding them into one general latent-variable
framework (Mplus, Latent GOLD, OpenMx in R) made them variations on one model [@jacobucci_ml].

Murphy's **mixture of Bernoullis** is LCA under another name: binary data, a class variable, and pixels
treated as independent within a class. Fitted with $K=10$ to unlabelled handwritten digits, it recovers some
of the digits but splits others into several clusters and misses some entirely. Murphy's reasons transfer
directly to survey items: the within-class model is too simple ("each pixel is treated independently"), and
some "true" categories really contain several styles, such as sevens with and without the crossbar
[@murphy_ml].

### A worked example: Gaussian clusters in stock returns

Bruce and Bruce fit model-based clustering (R's `mclust`, a mixture of multivariate Normals) to daily returns
of two oil stocks, ExxonMobil and Chevron, from 2011 on (1,131 days) [@bruce_practical]. It chose **two
components**, with 963 days in one and 168 in the other. The surprise is what the components are. They do not
sit side by side like k-means clusters. Their means are about the same, and the second component is a wide
cloud **surrounding** the first. Stock returns have longer tails than a Normal, so the mixture covers the bulk
of the days with one Normal and the extreme days with a second, more spread-out one [@bruce_practical].

The model `mclust` picked is called VEE, "ellipsoidal, equal shape and orientation", and you can check what
that means from the printed covariances. Every entry of the second matrix is **3.49 times** the
corresponding entry of the first, so both components have the same correlation (0.75). They differ only
in size (our arithmetic on Bruce and Bruce's output). `mclust` gets there by fitting 14 different covariance
structures at every $K$ and choosing among all of them by BIC [@bruce_practical]. A two-component answer here
is a statement about **tail shape**. It does not say there are two kinds of trading day.

## How many classes? {#sec-enumeration}

In a mixture, both the number of classes and the parameters within each class are unknown. The usual
approach is to fix the type of within-class model and search over the rest [@jacobucci_ml]. Following Ram
and Grimm, Jacobucci and colleagues recommend choosing a few theoretically motivated specifications in
advance, fitting each with more and more classes until the model stops converging or stops improving, and
then comparing the whole set on **fit, sensitivity, substantive meaning and classification quality**
[@jacobucci_ml].

**Information criteria.** With deviance $-2LL$ and $p$ parameters, $\text{AIC}=-2LL+2p$ and
$\text{BIC}=-2LL+\ln(N)\,p$; lower is better [@jacobucci_ml]. BIC charges $\ln N$ per parameter instead of 2,
so it favours smaller models once $N$ exceeds seven. Both can compare non-nested models, so they work across
different numbers of classes *and* different constraints [@jacobucci_ml]. Murphy explains why BIC is the
default. The principled Bayesian choice is the $K$ with the largest marginal likelihood, but for
latent-variable models that is hard to compute, and BIC is the simple approximation (citing Fraley & Raftery)
[@murphy_ml]. A trap with software: `mclust` reports BIC **with the sign flipped** and picks the
*largest* value [@bruce_practical].

**Likelihood-ratio tests.** A $K$-class and a $(K{-}1)$-class model are nested, but their deviance difference
is **not** $\chi^2$-distributed under the null. The corrected versions are the Vuong–Lo–Mendell–Rubin and
Lo–Mendell–Rubin adjusted tests, and the **bootstrap LRT** simulates from the smaller model to build the null
distribution directly [@jacobucci_ml].

**Cross-validation.** Fit on $k-1$ folds and score fit on the held-out fold (Grimm, Mazza & Davoudzadeh)
[@jacobucci_ml]. Murphy explains why this works for mixtures but not for k-means. Reconstruction error keeps
falling as $K$ grows, while a mixture's held-out likelihood eventually turns back up [@murphy_ml].

Simulation studies have **not** found one index that wins across latent class, latent profile and growth
mixture models, sample sizes and class separations. Jacobucci and colleagues therefore read the evidence in
sequence: information criteria, then approximate LRTs, then substantive interpretation and class separation,
with cross-validation as a sensitivity check [@jacobucci_ml].

**Classification quality: entropy.** Entropy summarises how decisively the posterior probabilities sort
people [@jacobucci_ml]:

$$
\text{Entropy} = 1 + \frac{1}{N\ln K}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{k=1}^{K} P(C_i=k\mid y_i)\,\ln P(C_i=k\mid y_i).
$$

> **In plain words:** 1 means everyone is assigned with certainty; 0 means the class labels are no better
> than coin flips.

Take a toy two-class model with four people (our numbers). If their probabilities of class 1 are 0.95, 0.90,
0.20 and 0.55, entropy is **0.38**. The person at 0.55 is nearly a coin flip, and even 0.90 and 0.80 leave
real doubt. If the four are 0.99, 0.98, 0.01 and 0.97, entropy is **0.88**. Some researchers want at least
0.80 before taking a solution seriously, but Jacobucci and colleagues note "no strong theoretical grounds"
for that number [@jacobucci_ml]. High entropy also does **not** show there are classes. It only shows the
fitted classes are well separated.

Finally, fit every model **from several sets of starting values**. Mixture likelihoods have local optima,
and restarts both help find the global maximum and show whether different starts give the same answer
[@jacobucci_ml].

### The grit example {#sec-grit}

Jacobucci and colleagues ran a latent class analysis on the 12 items of the Grit scale for a random 500
respondents, treating items as ordinal with five categories, estimating 1–5 classes with 10 starts each, and
leaving covariates out while choosing $K$ [@jacobucci_ml]:

| Classes | Deviance | Parameters | AIC | BIC | Entropy |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 17,636 | 48 | 17,732 | 17,934 | – |
| 2 | 16,525 | 97 | 16,719 | 17,128 | 0.85 |
| 3 | 16,184 | 146 | 16,476 | **17,091** | 0.87 |
| 4 | 15,925\* | 195 | 16,315 | 17,137 | 0.87 |
| 5 | 15,721 | 244 | **16,209** | 17,238 | 0.86 |

The parameter counts follow directly. One class needs 4 thresholds × 12 items = 48, and each extra class adds
another 48 plus one class intercept [@jacobucci_ml]. \*The book prints the 4-class deviance as 16,925, but
its own AIC and BIC both imply **15,925** (16,315 − 2 × 195 = 15,925, and adding ln(500) × 195 gives the
printed BIC of 17,137). We have corrected the typo. The AIC keeps falling through five classes, while the BIC
bottoms out at three. Jacobucci and colleagues side with BIC, because in their experience AIC does better in
small samples and BIC in larger ones, and they report three classes of about 50%, 20% and 30% [@jacobucci_ml].

The classes are read from item-response probabilities. Class 1 (half the sample) scores high on nearly every
item. Class 2 reports interests and projects changing from year to year, which the authors read as easily
distracted, with average commitment. Class 3 scores low on diligence and overcoming setbacks, but its
interests change less than class 2's [@jacobucci_ml]. Adding the Big Five as predictors of class membership
barely moved the classes. More conscientious ($t=7.25$), agreeable ($t=4.18$) and less neurotic ($t=3.09$)
people were more likely to be in class 1 than class 2, and more open people ($t=3.68$) were more likely to be
in class 2 than class 3 [@jacobucci_ml].

## Factor mixtures: classes defined by a measurement model {#sec-fmm}

When the indicators come from the same scale, or are the same measure over time, it often makes sense to
model their covariance with **latent factors** and look for classes *with respect to that factor model*. A
factor mixture model therefore combines two kinds of latent variable: a categorical one for the classes and a
continuous one for the common factor [@jacobucci_ml]. The analyst decides which parameters may differ across
classes. Freeing everything is the most flexible option and also the hardest to estimate. A common
compromise lets only the **factor means** differ, fixing them at zero in a reference class [@jacobucci_ml].

The example Jacobucci and colleagues recommend is Arias et al.'s detection of **careless responders**. In
the majority class every loading was fixed at 1. In the minority class the loadings were +1 or −1 according
to item wording, which is what you would see from someone answering every item the same way even though the
negatively worded items had been reverse-scored. About **4–10%** of cases fell in that inattentive class
[@jacobucci_ml].

Applied to the two-factor Grit model ($N$ ≈ 1,000), a two-class **means-only** model beat one class on AIC
and BIC, though entropy was low (0.62). Freeing the factor variances and covariance improved every index but
produced a **negative variance**, an improper solution. A three-class means-only model improved again
[@jacobucci_ml]. In the two-class model the class intercept was −1.05 on the log-odds scale. That puts about
**26%** of people in class 1 (our conversion: $1/(1+e^{1.05})$). Class 1's factor means were −0.32 (SE 0.10)
and −0.98 (0.07) relative to class 2, so it is lower on both grit facets [@jacobucci_ml].

### Covariates: let them form the classes, or only describe them?

Adding covariates is easy. Deciding what they should do is the hard part [@jacobucci_ml]. If covariates
predict the factors (making the CFA a **MIMIC** model), they help *form* the classes. If you want the classes
fixed first and the covariates only to describe them, use the **three-step approach** (Asparouhov & Muthén):
estimate the classes, compute classification-uncertainty rates, then run a multinomial logistic regression of
class on the covariates that accounts for that uncertainty [@jacobucci_ml]. With all 62 Grit-data variables as
covariates the model was inadmissible. Using five Big Five factor scores instead, every trait except
neuroticism predicted class membership, and conscientiousness had the largest effect (−2.143, SE 0.239)
[@jacobucci_ml]. As an odds ratio, a 1-SD increase in conscientiousness multiplies the odds of being in the
low-grit class by about **0.12** (our conversion).

## SEM trees: groups defined by observed covariates {#sec-semtrees}

A [decision tree](../04-predictive/trees-and-forests.qmd) does two things. It fits nonlinear relationships,
and it puts every observation into a terminal node, so each **node is a subgroup** [@jacobucci_ml]. **SEM
trees** (Brandmaier et al., 2013) use the second feature. The outcome is a whole structural equation model,
and the tree looks for covariate splits that **improve that model's fit**, scored by deviance. Jacobucci and
colleagues call it **exploratory multiple-group modelling** [@jacobucci_ml]. Repeated splits can capture
interactions among covariates and highly nonlinear step-function structure. The cost is the usual cost of a
single tree: instability and less-than-optimal prediction [@jacobucci_ml]. The same idea, with a mixed-effects
model in place of an SEM, appears in [ML with clustered data](ml-with-clustered-data.qmd).

**Demonstration.** With the two-factor Grit CFA as the outcome model and every Grit-data covariate as a
candidate splitter, a one-split SEM tree on $N=1{,}000$ splits on **C8, "I shirk my duties"**, between
responses 1 and 2. The likelihood-ratio gain is **379.7 on 40 df**, because all 40 parameters of the CFA may
differ across the two groups (13 free loadings, 12 residual variances, 3 factor variances and covariance, 12
intercepts) [@jacobucci_ml]. The authors read the right-hand group as higher in conscientiousness. It has
higher loadings on the first grit factor and lower ones on the second. You then compare the two groups'
parameters to see what drives the split [@jacobucci_ml].

**Tuning.** Jacobucci and colleagues stress that these settings should follow best practice and theory, not
be tuned by trial and error [@jacobucci_ml]:

- **Split criterion.** The *naive* method tries every cutpoint and keeps the best likelihood ratio.
  *Cross-validation* scores each cutpoint on held-out folds and is very slow. The **fair** criterion, which
  they recommend, uses half the sample to choose each covariate's best cutpoint and the other half to compare
  covariates. This reduces the usual tree bias towards **predictors with many possible cutpoints**.
- **Minimum node size.** An SEM is estimated in every node, so set a minimum (100 is a reasonable default).
  SEM trees are a **large-sample technique**.
- **Maximum depth.** With very large samples (over about 5,000) trees grow too big to read, so cap the depth.

### Asking about the latent variables only: invariance first {#sec-invariance}

If the question is "which covariates explain differences in the *constructs*?", the groups must measure the
same constructs. That requires **measurement invariance**: equality constraints on loadings, intercepts and
residual variances, so that only the factor means, variances and covariance can differ across groups
[@jacobucci_ml]. One complication is that in a mixture you fix a reference class's factor means at zero, but
an SEM tree has no reference group. The workaround is to **centre every observed variable**, fix the
intercepts at zero (which is roughly where they would be estimated anyway), and read each group's factor
means against the whole-sample mean of zero [@jacobucci_ml].

Under strict invariance only 5 parameters per group are free (two factor variances, their covariance and two
factor means). The Grit tree again splits first on C8 (LR 153.3, 5 df), then on **C1, "I am always
prepared"**, then on **N10, "I often feel blue"** [@jacobucci_ml]:

| Group (path) | $N$ | Grit factor 1 mean | Grit factor 2 mean |
|---|---|---|---|
| C8 low | 238 | −0.49 | −0.38 |
| C8 high, then C1 low | 218 | **+0.40** | **+0.37** |
| C8 high, C1 high, split on N10 | 251 | −0.11 | −0.09 |
| | 293 | +0.19 | +0.10 |

The lowest-grit group is the one the authors read as low on conscientiousness. The factor variances and
covariance hardly differ, so the groups vary in level, not in spread [@jacobucci_ml]. As a check, the
size-weighted average of the four groups' means is 0.000 on both factors, as the centring requires. That
consistency also tells us which two $N$s go with which means in the N10 split (our arithmetic on the printed
tree).

### SEM forests

Single trees are unstable, so as with [random forests](../04-predictive/trees-and-forests.qmd) the remedy is
to grow hundreds of SEM trees on random subsets of covariates (`mtry`) and summarise **variable importance**:
the permutation-based **drop in log-likelihood**, averaged across trees [@jacobucci_ml]. Variables that
formed the single tree should rank high, and here they did. With `mtry` = 20 and 100 trees, a few
conscientiousness items dominated, with **C8 the most important**, and most variables scored near zero. With
`mtry` = 2 and 500 trees the importances spread out much more evenly, because each tree sees only two
candidates and weaker variables get chosen [@jacobucci_ml]. Jacobucci and colleagues explain the trade-off:
low `mtry` gives a more **marginal** reading of importance, and high `mtry` a more **conditional** one,
favouring variables that still matter when others compete [@jacobucci_ml]. Runtimes are long. SEM trees take
minutes to hours, and SEM forests take hours (run them in parallel) [@jacobucci_ml].

```{python}
#| label: fig-groups
#| fig-cap: "Both methods on simulated data, coded by hand (ours, not the book's data). Left: a latent class analysis (a mixture of Bernoullis fitted by EM, 10 random starts per K) on 500 people × 8 binary items generated from three classes (50/20/30%). Each curve is the criterion minus its own minimum, on a log scale. BIC bottoms out at the true K = 3 (orange). AIC barely separates 3 from 4, much as in Jacobucci et al.'s grit table. Entropy, under the tick labels, stays near 0.8 throughout, so it does not pick out K = 3. Right: the split search a SEM tree runs. The outcome model is a bivariate Normal (5 parameters), N = 1,000, and the true groups differ in means at C ≥ 2 on a five-point item. Scanning that item's 4 cutpoints gives a likelihood-ratio gain of 58 at the true cut (orange). A pure-noise continuous covariate has 801 eligible cutpoints (minimum node size 100). Its curve stays under the χ²(5) 95% line here but zig-zags around, which is why the fair criterion keeps cutpoint selection and covariate comparison on separate halves of the data."
#| code-fold: true
#| code-summary: "Show the figure code (LCA by EM with restarts; SEM-tree style cutpoint scan, by hand)"

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(10)

# ---------- Left: latent class analysis by EM (mixture of Bernoullis) ----------
N, J = 500, 8
pi_true = np.array([0.5, 0.2, 0.3])
theta_true = np.array([[0.85]*8,                       # "high on everything"
                       [0.80]*4 + [0.25]*4,            # "high on half"
                       [0.20]*8])                      # "low on everything"
z = rng.choice(3, size=N, p=pi_true)
Y = (rng.random((N, J)) < theta_true[z]).astype(float)

def fit_lca(Y, K, rng, iters=300):
    n = len(Y)
    pi = rng.dirichlet(np.ones(K))
    th = rng.uniform(0.2, 0.8, size=(K, Y.shape[1]))
    for _ in range(iters):
        logp = np.log(pi) + Y @ np.log(th).T + (1 - Y) @ np.log(1 - th).T   # n x K
        m = logp.max(axis=1, keepdims=True)
        ll = float((m.ravel() + np.log(np.exp(logp - m).sum(axis=1))).sum())
        r = np.exp(logp - m); r /= r.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)              # E-step
        nk = r.sum(axis=0)
        pi = nk / n                                                          # M-step
        th = np.clip((r.T @ Y) / nk[:, None], 1e-4, 1 - 1e-4)
    return ll, r

Ks = range(1, 6)
aic, bic, ent = [], [], []
for K in Ks:
    ll, r = max((fit_lca(Y, K, rng) for _ in range(10)), key=lambda t: t[0])  # 10 restarts
    p = K * J + (K - 1)                      # item probabilities + class intercepts
    aic.append(-2 * ll + 2 * p); bic.append(-2 * ll + np.log(N) * p)
    rr = np.clip(r, 1e-12, 1)
    ent.append(np.nan if K == 1 else 1 + (rr * np.log(rr)).sum() / (N * np.log(K)))
aic, bic = np.array(aic), np.array(bic)
kb = int(np.argmin(bic))

# ---------- Right: an SEM-tree split search, by hand ----------
# Outcome "model": two correlated scores (5 parameters: 2 means, 2 variances, 1 covariance)
n = 1000
C = rng.choice([1, 2, 3, 4, 5], size=n, p=[0.24, 0.26, 0.22, 0.16, 0.12])   # ordinal item
noise = rng.normal(size=n)                                                     # continuous noise
S = np.array([[1.0, 0.4], [0.4, 1.0]])
X = rng.multivariate_normal([0, 0], S, size=n)
X[C >= 2] += np.array([0.45, 0.35])        # the real subgroup difference sits at C >= 2

def gauss_ll(X):
    m = X.mean(axis=0); D = X - m
    Sh = D.T @ D / len(X)                  # ML covariance
    return -0.5 * len(X) * (np.log(np.linalg.det(2 * np.pi * Sh)) + 2)

ll0 = gauss_ll(X)
def scan(v, min_n=100):
    vals = np.unique(v)
    cuts = (vals[:-1] + vals[1:]) / 2
    share, lr = [], []
    for c in cuts:
        left = v < c
        if left.sum() < min_n or (~left).sum() < min_n:
            continue
        share.append(left.mean())
        lr.append(2 * (gauss_ll(X[left]) + gauss_ll(X[~left]) - ll0))
    return np.array(share), np.array(lr)

sC, lC = scan(C); sN, lN = scan(noise)

fig, (axL, axR) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(9.6, 3.9))
Kx = np.arange(1, 6)
axL.plot(Kx, aic - aic.min(), "o-", color="#161613", lw=1.6, ms=4, label="AIC")
axL.plot(Kx, bic - bic.min(), "s-", color="#5e5e54", lw=1.6, ms=4, label="BIC")
axL.plot(Kx[kb], 0, "o", color="#e8531d", ms=10, zorder=5, label=f"BIC's choice: K = {Kx[kb]}")
axL.set_yscale("symlog", linthresh=20)
axL.set_xticks(Kx)
axL.set_xticklabels(["1"] + [f"{k}\nentropy {e:.2f}" for k, e in zip(Kx[1:], ent[1:])], fontsize=8)
axL.set_xlabel("number of latent classes K")
axL.set_ylabel("criterion minus its minimum (lower is better)")
axL.set_title("Mixtures: how many hidden groups?")
axL.legend(fontsize=8, frameon=False)

axR.plot(sN, lN, color="#b7b6a8", lw=1.0, label=f"noise covariate ({len(lN)} cut points)")
axR.plot(sC, lC, "o-", color="#161613", lw=1.6, ms=5, label="real covariate (4 cut points)")
ib = int(np.argmax(lC))
axR.plot(sC[ib], lC[ib], "o", color="#e8531d", ms=10, zorder=5, label="the split a SEM tree takes")
axR.axhline(11.07, color="#8a8a7e", lw=1, ls="--")
axR.text(0.98, 11.07, "χ²(5) 95% line", ha="right", va="bottom", fontsize=7.5, color="#8a8a7e",
         transform=axR.get_yaxis_transform())
axR.set_xlabel("share of the sample left of the cut")
axR.set_ylabel("likelihood-ratio gain (df = 5)")
axR.set_title("Trees: which observed split?")
axR.legend(fontsize=8, frameon=False, loc="upper right")
fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

## Choosing between them {#sec-choosing}

Jacobucci and colleagues frame the choice by asking whether you believe the heterogeneity comes from
**unobserved** or **observed** variables [@jacobucci_ml]. Their trade-offs:

- **Multivariate (SEM) trees** work best when there are meaningful covariates to split on. It is not known
  how non-Normal outcomes affect the groups they form. They get expensive with many covariates. With a
  complex outcome model it can be hard to tell which parameters really differ. And they inherit the
  instability of trees [@jacobucci_ml].
- **Mixtures** suit heterogeneity from unobserved variables and will fit better than trees. Non-Normality
  can cause trouble (they point to Bauer & Curran, 2004). Classes are
  harder to describe than tree nodes, and the constraints on class variances and covariances are hard to
  choose [@jacobucci_ml].

You cannot settle the choice with fit statistics. The two models are **not nested**, and since a mixture is
limited only by the number of classes while a tree is limited to splits on covariate values, the mixture will
almost always win on information criteria [@jacobucci_ml]. When unsure, run both and decide mainly on
theoretical grounds. Methods that combine the two (Grimm et al. 2022, the `MplusTrees` package) and the use of
mixtures and trees to detect **non-invariance** and differential item functioning are active research areas
[@jacobucci_ml].

## Common pitfalls

::: {.callout-warning title="Where this goes wrong"}
- **Reifying classes.** A mixture with $K$ classes can describe a population that is really continuous but
  non-Normal. Bruce and Bruce's two "clusters" of stock returns are one heavy-tailed cloud, and Jacobucci and
  colleagues flag non-Normality (Bauer & Curran) as a source of trouble. Treat classes as a description of the
  data, not a discovery of kinds of people. [@bruce_practical; @jacobucci_ml]
- **Treating entropy as evidence for classes.** Entropy measures separation of the fitted classes. The 0.80
  rule of thumb has "no strong theoretical grounds", and a well-separated solution can still have the wrong
  $K$. [@jacobucci_ml]
- **Picking $K$ from one index, or from the naive LRT.** The deviance difference between $K$ and $K-1$
  classes is not $\chi^2$, and simulation studies disagree about which index is best. Read ICs, adjusted or
  bootstrap LRTs, interpretation and stability together. [@jacobucci_ml]
- **One start.** Mixture likelihoods have local optima. Use many starting values and check that the best
  solutions agree. [@jacobucci_ml]
- **Ignoring improper solutions.** A negative variance (as in the free-variance grit FMM) is a warning about
  the model, not a result to report. [@jacobucci_ml]
- **Letting covariates silently reshape the classes.** Covariates entered directly can change which classes
  form. Check the classes before and after, or use a three-step approach. [@jacobucci_ml]
- **Using hard class labels downstream as if they were known.** Assignment is uncertain, and ignoring the
  posterior probabilities brings back the cutoff-score problem the mixture was meant to fix.
  [@jacobucci_ml]
- **Reading SEM-tree groups before checking invariance.** If loadings may differ, "this group has higher
  grit" may only mean grit was measured differently there. Constrain the measurement model first.
  [@jacobucci_ml]
- **Trusting one tree.** Splits are unstable and the naive criterion favours covariates with many cutpoints.
  Use the fair criterion and check the splits against SEM-forest importance. [@jacobucci_ml]
- **Mixing BIC sign conventions.** Mplus-style output wants the *lowest* BIC; `mclust` reports BIC so that the
  *highest* wins. [@jacobucci_ml; @bruce_practical]
:::

## Appendix · the mathematics {#sec-groups-math .unnumbered}

::: {.math-appendix}

**The latent class likelihood.** For person $i$ with $J$ categorical items $y_{ij}$, conditional independence
within class gives

$$
P(y_i) = \sum_{k=1}^{K}\phi_k\prod_{j=1}^{J}P(y_{ij}\mid C_i=k),
\qquad
P(C_i=k\mid y_i)=\frac{\phi_k\prod_j P(y_{ij}\mid C_i=k)}{P(y_i)}.
$$

The second expression (Bayes' rule) gives the posterior probabilities that feed entropy and the three-step
approach. For binary items, $P(y_{ij}\mid C_i=k)=\theta_{kj}^{y_{ij}}(1-\theta_{kj})^{1-y_{ij}}$, which is
Murphy's mixture of Bernoullis, and EM updates $\theta_{kj}$ as the responsibility-weighted share of 1s (the
figure's M-step).

**Counting parameters.** With ordinal items of $c$ categories, each class needs $J(c-1)$ thresholds and the
class variable needs $K-1$ intercepts: $p=K\,J(c-1)+K-1$. For Grit ($J=12$, $c=5$):
$p=48K+K-1 = 48, 97, 146, 195, 244$ for $K=1,\dots,5$, matching Table 10.1. In the figure ($J=8$ binary
items), $p=8K+K-1$.

**Information criteria.** $\text{AIC}=-2\ell+2p$ and $\text{BIC}=-2\ell+p\ln N$. BIC's per-parameter penalty
exceeds AIC's when $\ln N>2$, that is $N>e^2\approx7.4$. `mclust` reports $2\ell-p\ln N$ (the negative), so
for Bruce and Bruce's model $2(-2255.134)-9\ln 1131=-4573.55$.

**Entropy.** Each person contributes $\sum_k P_{ik}\ln P_{ik}\in[-\ln K,0]$. It is 0 for a certain
assignment and $-\ln K$ for a uniform one. Dividing the sum by $N\ln K$ and adding 1 maps the average onto
$[0,1]$. In the toy example, the four people contribute −0.199, −0.325, −0.500 and −0.688, so
$1-1.712/(4\ln2)=0.38$.

**The bootstrap LRT** (McLachlan, as described by Jacobucci et al.). (1) Fit the $K-1$ and $K$ class models
and record $LR_{obs}=2(\ell_K-\ell_{K-1})$. (2) Simulate many datasets from the fitted $(K-1)$-class model.
(3) Fit both models to each and record $LR^{*}$. (4) The $p$-value is the share of $LR^{*}\ge LR_{obs}$. The
simulation stands in for the $\chi^2$ reference distribution, which does not hold for the class-number
comparison.

**The SEM-tree split statistic.** For a node with data $D$ and a candidate split into $D_L,D_R$,

$$
LR=2\big[\ell(\hat\theta_L;D_L)+\ell(\hat\theta_R;D_R)-\ell(\hat\theta;D)\big],
$$

where each $\hat\theta$ is the SEM's ML estimate on that subset. The degrees of freedom equal the number of
parameters allowed to differ: 40 for the unconstrained grit CFA, 5 under strict invariance (two factor
variances, one covariance, two factor means). For the figure's bivariate Normal,
$\ell(\hat\theta;D)=-\tfrac n2\big[\ln\det(2\pi\hat\Sigma)+2\big]$ with $\hat\Sigma$ the ML covariance, so the
LR has 5 df. The naive criterion takes the maximum over all cutpoints and covariates. A covariate with many
cutpoints gets many chances, so its maximum is inflated. The fair criterion picks the cutpoint on one half of
the data and compares covariates on the other half.

**The centring trick.** If every indicator is centred, $\bar y_j=0$, the ML intercepts of the pooled model are
0. Fixing them at 0 in every group then identifies each group's factor means as deviations from the pooled
mean. That is why the size-weighted average of the Fig 10.5 group means is 0 on both factors
($238(-0.485)+218(0.397)+251(-0.107)+293(0.19)\approx 0$).
