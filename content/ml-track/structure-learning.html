Structure Learning

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source

Learning the graph itself from data. Which variables are linked, which way the arrows point, and how much of that the data can tell you at all.

Every other graphical-model entry starts with the graph and fits the numbers. Structure learning turns this round: it searches for the graph, the pattern of links between variables, that best explains the data. It works by scoring graphs (a marginal likelihood that rewards fit and penalises complexity) and searching a space of graphs that grows faster than exponentially. For undirected Gaussian models it reduces to finding the zeros of the precision matrix, which the graphical lasso does. Two limits stay whatever the method. Arrows can only be learned up to Markov equivalence, and a learned graph is causal only under strong assumptions.

Why it matters

So far this track has assumed someone hands you the graph. A hidden Markov model fixes the chain, a Markov random field fixes the lattice, and a DAG is drawn from what the researcher knows. Often you don’t know the graph, and finding it is the question. Which survey items hang together once the others are held fixed? Which words in a corpus go together? Does family background affect a student’s plans directly, or only through parental encouragement?

Murphy separates two reasons to learn a structure (Murphy 2012). Knowledge discovery needs only the pattern of links. You want to see which variables are connected, ideally with a measure of how confident to be in each link. Density estimation needs a full model you can predict with. The distinction matters because the right summary differs. For discovery Murphy recommends the probability of each edge being present, not the single best graph. For prediction you want a good complete model, and he notes that a latent-variable model is often easier to fit and use, at the cost of factors that are hard to interpret (Murphy 2012). The psychometric networks of the measurement entry are structure learning used for discovery. The model-selection logic is the Bayesian model comparison of Part II applied to graphs.

The space of graphs is enormous

The basic obstacle is counting. With V variables there are V(V-1)/2 possible undirected links, so 2^{V(V-1)/2} undirected graphs (Murphy 2012). Directed acyclic graphs are worse. Robinson’s recurrence gives 1, 3, 25, 543, 29,281 and 3,781,503 DAGs on one to six nodes (Murphy 2012). (We extended it: seven nodes already give 1,138,779,265.) You cannot list the posterior over graphs, let alone store it. You also cannot find the single best DAG efficiently in general, because the problem is NP-complete (Chickering 1996, as Murphy reports) (Murphy 2012). Everything below is a way to get a useful answer without visiting every graph.

Quick and dirty: relevance and dependency networks

The simplest method is a relevance network: compute the dependence between every pair of variables (Murphy uses mutual information) and draw an edge wherever it passes a threshold (Murphy 2012). For Gaussian variables mutual information is a function of the correlation alone, so this just draws the large entries of the correlation matrix, a covariance graph (Murphy 2012). It is popular in systems biology. Murphy’s own example builds one from words in newsgroup posts (Murphy 2012).

Its weakness is density. If X_1 affects X_2 and X_2 affects X_3, then X_1 and X_3 are correlated too, so the relevance network draws a 1–3 edge. Since most variables are dependent on most others through some chain, “most pairs will be connected” (Murphy 2012). A graphical model draws conditional dependence instead: X_1 and X_3 are independent given X_2, so there is no edge, and the graph is much sparser. The figure below shows this on a chain of eight variables.

A dependency network gets closer cheaply. It fits one sparse regression per variable, predicting it from all the others, and draws an edge to each selected predictor, its estimated Markov blanket (Murphy 2012). Any sparse method will do: classification trees, \ell_1-penalised linear or logistic regression, or Bayesian variable selection. Murphy’s newsgroup example used \ell_1-penalised logistic regression with the penalty chosen by BIC. Many links are natural (aids–disease, bible–god, cancer–patients). Some look odd until you read the signs: most of the weights are negative (1,173 negative, 286 positive, 8,541 zero), and bmw–christian carries -11.54, an “unlikely combination”, not an association (Murphy 2012). The catch is that a set of separately fitted conditionals does not in general define any valid joint distribution. So the network is good for looking at, for imputing a few missing values by Gibbs sampling, and as a starting point for better methods, but not as a model in its own right (Murphy 2012).

Trees: the one case that is easy

If the graph must be a tree (no loops), the best structure can be found exactly and fast. A tree also has the same parameters and likelihood whether you draw it directed or undirected, and whichever node you root it at (Murphy 2012). The log-likelihood of a tree splits into a part that does not depend on the shape plus one term per edge: the mutual information between its two ends. So the maximum-likelihood tree is the maximum-weight spanning tree with mutual informations as weights. This is the Chow–Liu algorithm (1968), and standard spanning-tree algorithms find it in roughly O(NV^2 + V^2\log V) time (Murphy 2012).

Two refinements matter. Every tree has the same number of parameters, so maximum likelihood is a fair score between trees, but it never leaves an edge out. Score with the marginal likelihood or BIC instead and some edge weights become negative, so the best structure can be a forest, found by the same algorithm after dropping negative edges (Murphy 2012). And a mixture of trees, fitted by EM with a weighted Chow–Liu step, buys more flexibility while each component stays easy to work with (Murphy 2012).

Learning DAGs: a score and a search

For general DAGs the approach is to give each graph a score and search for a high-scoring one.

The score. Maximum likelihood will not do. More arrows always fit better, so it picks a fully connected graph (Murphy 2012). The standard choice is the marginal likelihood p(D\mid G), the probability of the data with the parameters averaged out, which builds in the “Bayesian Occam’s razor” penalty against complexity (Murphy 2012). With categorical variables, complete data and Dirichlet priors on each row of each conditional-probability table, it has a closed form. Better still, it decomposes: it is a product of one local score per node, which depends only on that node and its parents (Murphy 2012). The usual prior is BDeu. You choose one equivalent sample size \alpha, an imaginary prior sample spread evenly over every cell of every table. It is the most widely used prior for this task, and it is built so that graphs the data cannot tell apart get the same score (below) (Murphy 2012).

A worked example. Murphy takes a tiny one from Neapolitan. There are two binary variables and eight cases. X_1 takes value 1 five times. When X_1=1, X_2=1 four times out of five; when X_1=2, once out of three. Does X_1\to X_2 beat no edge at all? With BDeu and \alpha=4, p(D\mid G_1)=7.2150\times10^{-6} and p(D\mid G_2)=6.7465\times10^{-6}, so with equal prior odds the edge has posterior probability 0.517 (Murphy 2012). (We reproduced both numbers exactly.) That is close to a coin flip, and it should be: eight cases say very little. The prior matters here too, which Murphy does not show. Recomputing with other \alpha (our arithmetic), the edge’s probability is 0.37 at \alpha=1, 0.46 at \alpha=2 and 0.53 at \alpha=8. With so little data, the choice of prior decides whether the link “exists”.

The search. Knowing an order of the variables makes the search easy. Each node’s parents can then be chosen separately from the nodes before it, with no risk of cycles, which is the K2 algorithm (Cooper & Herskovits 1992) (Murphy 2012). Without an order, exact search by dynamic programming takes V2^V time and memory and stops being practical at about 16 nodes (Murphy 2012). The common practical method is greedy hill climbing: add, delete or reverse one edge at a time, keep the change that most improves the score, and stop at a local maximum. Because the score decomposes, each proposed change alters only one or two local terms, so it is cheap to evaluate. Murphy suggests starting from the best tree, using random restarts, and limiting the candidate edges to those a dependency network found (Murphy 2012). For discovery, sample graphs instead by Metropolis–Hastings and report how often each edge appears. Sampling orders of the variables rather than graphs mixes faster (Murphy 2012).

Graphs the data cannot tell apart

Some arrows cannot be learned from observational data however much of it you have. The chain X\to Y\to Z, its reverse, and the fork X\leftarrow Y\to Z all say the same thing: X and Z are dependent, but independent given Y. They are Markov equivalent (Murphy 2012). The collider X\to Y\leftarrow Z says the opposite, and it can be told apart. Verma and Pearl’s theorem settles the general case: two DAGs are Markov equivalent exactly when they have the same skeleton (the links, ignoring direction) and the same v-structures (colliders whose parents are not linked) (Murphy 2012). The DAG entry gives the same result as a limit on testing a graph.

A whole equivalence class can be drawn as one partially directed graph (PDAG, or essential graph). Its directed edges are compelled: reversing one would create or destroy a v-structure. Its undirected edges could point either way (Murphy 2012). So structure learning recovers a DAG only “up to Markov equivalence”. Murphy’s advice is not to read too much into the direction of an arrow in a learned graph, since it can often be reversed with no observable consequence (Murphy 2012). BDeu’s design follows from this: Markov-equivalent graphs fit the data equally well, so a good score should not separate them. Geiger and Heckerman showed that the Dirichlet form with pseudo-counts \alpha\,p_0(\cdot) is the only prior that ensures this, given their independence assumptions (Murphy 2012).

A social-science example: who plans to go to college?

Murphy’s most interesting example is a sociological one (Heckerman et al. 1997). In 1968, Sewell and Shah measured five variables on 10,318 Wisconsin high-school seniors: sex, socio-economic status (SES), IQ, parental encouragement and college plans (Murphy 2012). Heckerman’s team computed the exact posterior over all 29,281 five-node DAGs. They gave zero prior probability to graphs in which sex or SES has parents or college plans has children, which is domain knowledge entering as a prior. With BDeu at \alpha=5 (they report the results are robust for \alpha from 3 to 40), the best graph took almost all the posterior mass (Murphy 2012).

The tempting reading is causal. SES, IQ and encouragement all point into college plans, and sex acts only through parental encouragement, which “makes sense”. But the graph also has a direct arrow from SES to IQ, which is surprising and may reflect a hidden common cause (Murphy 2012). So they added a hidden variable H pointing into SES and IQ (and in some variants encouragement), tried 2 to 6 states for it, and scored 40 models. The best one, a binary H, was about 2\times10^{10} times more probable than the best model without a hidden variable (Murphy 2012). SES and IQ are both more likely to be high when H=1, and the authors read H as “parent quality”.

Murphy adds two cautions (Murphy 2012). First, the H–SES arrow can be reversed without changing any v-structure, so the data cannot say which way it points, which is exactly why hidden variables are hard to interpret. Second, the hidden-variable model implies the same conditional independences among the five observed variables as the best model without it. A method that looks only at independences in the data (the constraint-based approach of Pearl & Verma and Spirtes et al.) could never choose between them. The Bayesian score could, because it also weighs parsimony (Murphy 2012). With a hidden variable the marginal likelihood has no closed form. BIC “often severely underestimates” it and so picks models that are too simple; the Cheeseman–Stutz approximation does better, and the variational bound of variational Bayes EM better still (Murphy 2012). Structural EM saves work by filling in the missing data once and using it to score every neighbouring graph (Murphy 2012).

From structure to causes

When can a learned DAG be read causally? Murphy states the assumptions (Murphy 2012):

  • Causal Markov: an arrow A\to B means A directly causes B.
  • Causal sufficiency: no relevant variable is missing, so there are no unmeasured confounders.
  • Faithfulness: every conditional independence in the data comes from the graph’s structure, not from a coincidental cancellation of parameters.

Given these, and all variables observed, some algorithms (for example Chickering’s greedy equivalence search) recover the true equivalence class as the sample grows (Murphy 2012). Each assumption can fail. The college-plans example shows causal sufficiency failing, since the SES→IQ arrow appeared because a common cause was left out. That is the omitted-variable problem under another name.

The difference between seeing and doing still applies. Yellow-stained fingers are evidence that someone smokes. Painting someone’s fingers yellow is not, because the intervention cuts the arrow from smoking to stained fingers (Murphy 2012). If you learn only an equivalence class, the IDA method (Maathuis et al.) computes the causal effect under every DAG in the class and reports the smallest, a lower bound (Murphy 2012). Its test was ambitious. On yeast gene-expression data it ranked the likely targets of 234 genes, using knockout experiments as ground truth. At 10% recall its precision was 66%, low but well above the lasso and elastic net, which were barely better than chance (Murphy 2012).

To orient arrows within an equivalence class you need interventions. With experimental data the score changes in one simple way: when estimating a variable’s conditional distribution, drop the cases in which that variable was set by the experimenter, because it was not produced by its usual mechanism (Cooper & Yoo) (Murphy 2012). Murphy’s example is protein signalling (Sachs et al. 2005): 5,400 measurements of 11 proteins under chemical interventions. Imperfect interventions are modelled as extra nodes in the graph. Edges with posterior probability above 0.5 recovered the known pathway with one false negative (Murphy 2012).

Pearl and Mackenzie set the tone for social scientists. Causal discovery, finding the cause of an outcome from scratch, was Pearl’s own “ambitious dream” when he started on graphical models and remains an area of vigorous research. The main business of causal inference, and of their book, is different: writing down plausible causal knowledge that already exists, combining it with data, and answering questions with practical value (Pearl and Mackenzie 2018). In their view data alone are “profoundly dumb” about causes (Pearl and Mackenzie 2018). A learned graph is a hypothesis to test with design and theory, not a conclusion.

Undirected Gaussian graphs and the graphical lasso

Undirected structures avoid the acyclicity constraint, but their likelihood does not decompose, so every proposed change means refitting the model. That rules out the local search used for DAGs (Murphy 2012). For Gaussian data there is a better route. In a Gaussian graphical model a missing edge is exactly a zero in the precision matrix \Omega=\Sigma^{-1} (see MRFs), so learning the graph means learning a sparse precision matrix.

Fitting a known graph is covariance selection (Dempster 1972). The maximum-likelihood fit has a clear shape. For every pair that is linked, and every variance, the fitted covariance equals the sample covariance. For every pair that is not linked, the precision entry is zero, and the fitted covariance for that pair is whatever value maximises the likelihood under those zeros (Murphy 2012). In Murphy’s four-variable cycle (1-2-3-4-1, from Hastie, Tibshirani & Friedman) the sample covariances of the two unlinked pairs are 5 and 6. The fit replaces them with 1.31 and 0.87 and keeps every other entry (Murphy 2012). We reproduced these values with the textbook algorithm (appendix).

Learning the graph adds an \ell_1 penalty to the precision matrix, the same move as the lasso for regression coefficients. Minimising -\log\det\Omega+\operatorname{tr}(S\Omega)+\lambda\lVert\Omega\rVert_1 pushes small entries to exactly zero. This is the graphical lasso (Murphy 2012). The objective is convex, and Friedman, Hastie and Tibshirani solve it by cycling through the variables and fitting one lasso regression per variable. The figure’s code does exactly that. On the protein data, raising \lambda from 0 to 7, 27 and 36 cuts the graph from 55 edges (all of them) to 18, 11 and 8 (Murphy 2012). Each graph is a plausible hypothesis about which proteins interact. Murphy compares this with the DAG learned from the same data. The DAG can use the interventions; the undirected graph can represent the feedback loops known to exist in that pathway, which a DAG cannot (Murphy 2012).

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# --- the truth: an 8-variable chain, written as a sparse precision matrix ----
P, N = 8, 1000
true_edges = {(i, i + 1) for i in range(P - 1)}
Theta = np.eye(P)
for s, t in true_edges:
    Theta[s, t] = Theta[t, s] = -0.45          # partial correlation 0.45 on each link
Sigma = np.linalg.inv(Theta)
rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
X = rng.multivariate_normal(np.zeros(P), Sigma, size=N)
R = np.corrcoef(X, rowvar=False)

# --- the graphical lasso by hand (Friedman, Hastie & Tibshirani 2008) --------
def soft(x, a):
    return np.sign(x) * np.maximum(np.abs(x) - a, 0.0)

def glasso(S, lam, sweeps=100, tol=1e-6):
    p = S.shape[0]
    W = S + lam * np.eye(p)                      # current estimate of the covariance
    B = np.zeros((p, p))
    for _ in range(sweeps):
        W_old = W.copy()
        for j in range(p):                       # one lasso regression per variable
            idx = np.arange(p) != j
            W11, s12, b = W[np.ix_(idx, idx)], S[idx, j], B[idx, j].copy()
            for _ in range(200):                 # coordinate descent ("shooting")
                b_old = b.copy()
                for k in range(p - 1):
                    r = s12[k] - W11[k] @ b + W11[k, k] * b[k]
                    b[k] = soft(r, lam) / W11[k, k]
                if np.max(np.abs(b - b_old)) < tol:
                    break
            B[idx, j] = b
            W[idx, j] = W[j, idx] = W11 @ b
        if np.max(np.abs(W - W_old)) < tol:
            break
    Th = np.zeros((p, p))                        # recover the precision matrix
    for j in range(p):
        idx = np.arange(p) != j
        Th[j, j] = 1 / (W[j, j] - W[idx, j] @ B[idx, j])
        Th[idx, j] = -B[idx, j] * Th[j, j]
    return (Th + Th.T) / 2

    return {(s, t) for s in range(P) for t in range(s + 1, P) if abs(M[s, t]) > thr}

Reading the figure

The chain is the simplest case of Murphy’s warning about relevance networks. In our sample, variables 1 and 3 have correlation 0.31, close to its population value of 0.32. The correlation is real, but it is carried entirely by variable 2, so a threshold on correlations counts it as a link. The graphical lasso asks the conditional question and leaves it out. To be fair to the relevance network, in this tidy example a cut-off between 0.38 (the largest correlation between unlinked variables) and 0.51 (the smallest between linked ones) would also separate them perfectly. Nothing in the data tells you to put the cut-off there, though, and with hubs and loops the two ranges overlap.

The bottom panel shows the real decision in the graphical lasso: choosing \lambda. Too small, and noise survives as weak links. Too large, and true links disappear. Here there is a wide safe range (about 0.32 to 0.5), because n=1{,}000 is large and the true links are strong. With a few hundred respondents and weaker links the range shrinks or vanishes. In another of our trials (the same chain plus one link closing a five-variable loop, 300 cases), four false links were still there at λ = 0.40, with all eight true ones. That fits Murphy’s warning that the graphical lasso is not model-selection consistent (Meinshausen 2005): even with unlimited data it need not recover the true graph (Murphy 2012).

Beyond the Gaussian lasso

Murphy’s remaining material sets the limits of the method (Murphy 2012):

  • Bayesian GGM structure. The lasso returns one graph. Averaging over graphs gives edge probabilities, as for DAGs. This is exact and fast only for decomposable (chordal) graphs, and those soon become a small minority. Murphy’s ratios of decomposable to all undirected graphs for 2 to 8 nodes are 1, 1, 0.95, 0.8, 0.55, 0.29 and 0.12. Faster approximations (Moghaddam et al. 2009) beat the graphical lasso on both prediction and recovering the structure, at similar cost (Murphy 2012).
  • Non-Gaussian continuous data. The nonparanormal (Liu et al. 2009), the family of Gaussian copulas, transforms each variable monotonically toward normality using its empirical distribution and then runs the graphical lasso (Murphy 2012). This matters for skewed survey scales.
  • Discrete variables. With binary or ordinal items the partition function is as hard as in any MRF, so penalised fitting uses a group lasso on each edge’s block of weights and approximations to the likelihood (Murphy 2012). The Ising networks used for categorical survey items are in this family (Jacobucci et al. 2023).

Common pitfalls

WarningWhere this goes wrong
  • Treating a relevance network as a graphical model. Thresholded correlations draw indirect dependence as links, so most pairs end up connected. Use conditional dependence (a precision matrix, a dependency network, a scored graph). (Murphy 2012)
  • Reading arrow directions off a learned DAG. Within a Markov equivalence class many arrows can be reversed with no observable consequence. Only compelled edges, prior knowledge or interventions orient them. (Murphy 2012)
  • Calling a learned graph causal without its assumptions. Causal Markov, causal sufficiency and faithfulness are all needed. The college-plans SES→IQ arrow came from a missing common cause. (Murphy 2012)
  • Reporting the single best graph as “the” structure. With little data the posterior is flat (0.517 in the eight-case example) and can depend on the prior’s \alpha. For discovery, report edge probabilities. (Murphy 2012)
  • Choosing the score by maximum likelihood. It always prefers more edges. Use a marginal likelihood or a penalised score, and remember that BIC tends to pick graphs that are too simple when variables are hidden. (Murphy 2012)
  • Treating one \lambda as the answer. The graphical-lasso graph changes with the penalty, and the method can keep false links or drop true ones even with lots of data. Show the path, or several graphs. (Murphy 2012)
  • Using a dependency network as a joint model. Separately fitted conditionals need not fit together into any joint distribution, so Gibbs output from them may mean nothing. (Murphy 2012)
  • Interpreting a discovered hidden variable. “Parent quality” is a label the analyst chose. Its arrows can be reversible and its meaning is not identified by the data. (Murphy 2012)

Appendix · the mathematics

Counting graphs. Undirected graphs on V labelled nodes: 2^{V(V-1)/2}. DAGs (Robinson 1973): f(D)=\sum_{i=1}^{D}(-1)^{i+1}\binom{D}{i}2^{i(D-i)}f(D-i),\qquad f(0)=1, giving 1, 3, 25, 543, 29,281, 3,781,503, 1,138,779,265 for D=1,\dots,7.

Gaussian mutual information. For a bivariate normal with correlation \rho, I(X;Y)=-\tfrac12\log(1-\rho^2), increasing in |\rho|, so thresholding MI thresholds |\rho|.

Tree likelihood and Chow–Liu. An undirected tree factorises as p(x\mid T)=\prod_{t}p(x_t)\prod_{(s,t)\in E}\frac{p(x_s,x_t)}{p(x_s)p(x_t)}. At the MLE, \frac{1}{N}\log p(D\mid\hat\theta,T)=\sum_t\sum_k \hat p(x_t=k)\log\hat p(x_t=k)+\sum_{(s,t)\in E(T)} I(x_s;x_t\mid\hat\theta_{st}), and only the second sum depends on T, so the best tree is the maximum-weight spanning tree under weights I(x_s;x_t)\ge0.

Dirichlet marginal likelihood. Let N_{tck} count cases with node t in state k and its parents in configuration c, and put a \mathrm{Dir}(\alpha_{tc}) prior on each row. Then p(D\mid G)=\prod_{t=1}^{V}\prod_{c=1}^{C_t}\frac{\Gamma(\alpha_{tc})}{\Gamma(N_{tc}+\alpha_{tc})}\prod_{k=1}^{K_t}\frac{\Gamma(N_{tck}+\alpha_{tck})}{\Gamma(\alpha_{tck})}, with N_{tc}=\sum_kN_{tck}, \alpha_{tc}=\sum_k\alpha_{tck}. It is a product of local scores, one per family. BDeu sets \alpha_{tck}=\alpha/(K_tC_t).

The eight-case example. Counts: N_1=(5,3); given X_1=1, X_2=(4,1); given X_1=2, X_2=(1,2); marginally X_2=(5,3). With \alpha=4, G_1 (X_1\to X_2) uses pseudo-counts (2,2) for X_1 and (1,1) for each row of X_2; G_2 (no edge) uses (2,2) for both. Then p(D\mid G_1)=7.2150\times10^{-6}, p(D\mid G_2)=6.7465\times10^{-6} and P(G_1\mid D)=0.5168. (Murphy’s text lists G_2’s pseudo-counts as (\alpha/2,\alpha/2) “for each x_1”, as if X_2 still had a parent. That version gives 9.28\times10^{-6}. His printed figure matches the no-parent prior, which is the one used here.) The maximised log-likelihoods differ by 0.88 in G_1’s favour; the marginal likelihood’s Occam penalty almost cancels it.

BIC. \mathrm{BIC}(G)=\log p(D\mid\hat\theta,G)-\tfrac{\log N}{2}\dim(G). Per node for tables, \dim=(K_t-1)C_t. Murphy’s eq. 26.31 writes K_tC_t.

Markov equivalence (Verma & Pearl 1990). G_1\equiv G_2 iff same skeleton and same v-structures. The BDe prior \alpha_{tck}=\alpha\,p_0(x_t=k,x_{\mathrm{pa}(t)}=c) is the unique Dirichlet prior giving equivalent graphs equal marginal likelihood under global and local parameter independence (Geiger & Heckerman 1997).

Interventional data. Score node t with counts that skip cases where t was set: N_{tck}=\sum_{i:\,x_{it}\text{ not set}}\mathbb I(x_{it}=k,\,x_{i,\mathrm{pa}(t)}=c).

Gaussian graphical models. With \Omega=\Sigma^{-1} and sample covariance S, the profile log-likelihood is \ell(\Omega)=\log\det\Omega-\operatorname{tr}(S\Omega), with gradient \Omega^{-1}-S. Under a known graph the MLE satisfies \hat\Sigma_{st}=S_{st} for linked pairs and diagonals, and \hat\Omega_{st}=0 for unlinked pairs: a positive-definite completion of S. For the cycle 1-2-3-4-1 with S=\begin{pmatrix}10&1&5&4\\1&10&2&6\\5&2&10&3\\4&6&3&10\end{pmatrix},\qquad \hat\Sigma_{13}=1.31,\ \hat\Sigma_{24}=0.87, all other entries of \hat\Sigma equal to S, and \hat\Omega_{13}=\hat\Omega_{24}=0.

The graphical lasso. Minimise J(\Omega)=-\log\det\Omega+\operatorname{tr}(S\Omega)+\lambda\lVert\Omega\rVert_1. Partition the working covariance W with column j last. The block update solves the lasso \min_\beta\ \tfrac12\beta^\top W_{11}\beta-s_{12}^\top\beta+\lambda\lVert\beta\rVert_1 by coordinate descent, \beta_k\leftarrow \mathrm{soft}\big(s_{12,k}-\sum_{l\neq k}W_{11,kl}\beta_l,\ \lambda\big)/W_{11,kk}, then sets w_{12}=W_{11}\beta. At convergence \hat\Omega_{jj}=1/(w_{jj}-w_{12}^\top\beta) and \hat\omega_{12}=-\beta\,\hat\Omega_{jj}. The optimality conditions are |W_{st}-S_{st}|\le\lambda off the diagonal, with equality wherever \hat\Omega_{st}\neq0, and W_{jj}=S_{jj}+\lambda. (Our figure’s fits satisfy them to 10^{-6}.) Dropping the penalty and restricting the support to a known graph gives the covariance-selection algorithm above.

The chain in the figure. \Omega is tridiagonal with 1 on the diagonal and -0.45 beside it, so each link’s partial correlation is 0.45. Inverting gives population correlations of 0.53–0.62 between neighbours and 0.32–0.38 between variables two apart (larger in the middle of the chain): nonzero, but zero once you condition on the variable in between.

::: {.lead-kernel}
Every other graphical-model entry starts with the graph and fits the numbers. **Structure learning** turns
this round: it searches for the graph, the pattern of links between variables, that best explains the data.
It works by **scoring** graphs (a marginal likelihood that rewards fit and penalises complexity) and
**searching** a space of graphs that grows faster than exponentially. For undirected Gaussian models it
reduces to finding the **zeros of the precision matrix**, which the **graphical lasso** does. Two limits stay
whatever the method. Arrows can only be learned up to **Markov equivalence**, and a learned graph is causal
only under strong assumptions.
:::

## Why it matters

So far this track has assumed someone hands you the graph. A
[hidden Markov model](hidden-markov-models.qmd) fixes the chain, a
[Markov random field](markov-random-fields.qmd) fixes the lattice, and a
[DAG](../05-causal-inference/directed-acyclic-graphs.qmd) is drawn from what the researcher knows. Often you
don't know the graph, and finding it is the question. Which survey items hang together once the others
are held fixed? Which words in a corpus go together? Does family background affect a student's plans
directly, or only through parental encouragement?

Murphy separates two reasons to learn a structure [@murphy_ml]. **Knowledge discovery** needs only the
pattern of links. You want to *see* which variables are connected, ideally with a measure of how confident
to be in each link. **Density estimation** needs a full model you can predict with. The distinction
matters because the right summary differs. For discovery Murphy recommends the probability of each edge
being present, not the single best graph. For prediction you want a good complete model, and he notes that a
[latent-variable model](mixture-models-and-em.qmd) is often easier to fit and use, at the cost of factors
that are hard to interpret [@murphy_ml]. The
[psychometric networks](../04-predictive/measurement-and-ml.qmd) of the measurement entry are structure
learning used for discovery. The model-selection logic is the
[Bayesian model comparison](../02-inference/bayesian-model-comparison.qmd) of Part II applied to graphs.

## The space of graphs is enormous

The basic obstacle is counting. With $V$ variables there are $V(V-1)/2$ possible undirected links, so
$2^{V(V-1)/2}$ undirected graphs [@murphy_ml]. Directed acyclic graphs are worse. Robinson's recurrence gives
1, 3, 25, 543, 29,281 and 3,781,503 DAGs on one to six nodes [@murphy_ml]. (We extended it: seven nodes
already give 1,138,779,265.) You cannot list the posterior over graphs, let alone store it. You also cannot
find the single best DAG efficiently in general, because the problem is NP-complete (Chickering 1996, as
Murphy reports) [@murphy_ml]. Everything below is a way to get a useful answer without visiting every graph.

## Quick and dirty: relevance and dependency networks

The simplest method is a **relevance network**: compute the dependence between every pair of variables
(Murphy uses mutual information) and draw an edge wherever it passes a threshold [@murphy_ml]. For Gaussian
variables mutual information is a function of the correlation alone, so this just draws the large entries
of the correlation matrix, a **covariance graph** [@murphy_ml]. It is popular in systems biology. Murphy's
own example builds one from words in newsgroup posts [@murphy_ml].

Its weakness is density. If $X_1$ affects $X_2$ and $X_2$ affects $X_3$, then $X_1$ and $X_3$ are correlated
too, so the relevance network draws a $1$–$3$ edge. Since most variables are dependent on most others through
*some* chain, "most pairs will be connected" [@murphy_ml]. A graphical model draws **conditional**
dependence instead: $X_1$ and $X_3$ are independent given $X_2$, so there is no edge, and the graph is much
sparser. The figure below shows this on a chain of eight variables.

A **dependency network** gets closer cheaply. It fits one sparse regression per variable, predicting it from
all the others, and draws an edge to each selected predictor, its estimated
[Markov blanket](markov-random-fields.qmd) [@murphy_ml]. Any sparse method will do: classification trees,
[$\ell_1$-penalised](../04-predictive/regularization.qmd) linear or logistic regression, or Bayesian
variable selection. Murphy's newsgroup example used $\ell_1$-penalised logistic regression with the penalty
chosen by BIC. Many links are natural (aids–disease, bible–god, cancer–patients). Some look odd until you
read the signs: most of the weights are *negative* (1,173 negative, 286 positive, 8,541 zero), and
bmw–christian carries $-11.54$, an "unlikely combination", not an association [@murphy_ml]. The catch is that
a set of separately fitted conditionals does not in general define any valid joint distribution. So the
network is good for looking at, for imputing a few missing values by
[Gibbs sampling](../02-inference/mcmc-in-depth.qmd), and as a starting point for better methods, but not as a
model in its own right [@murphy_ml].

## Trees: the one case that is easy

If the graph must be a **tree** (no loops), the best structure can be found exactly and fast. A tree also has
the same parameters and likelihood whether you draw it directed or undirected, and whichever node you root it
at [@murphy_ml]. The log-likelihood of a tree splits into a part that does not depend on the shape plus one
term per edge: the **mutual information** between its two ends. So the maximum-likelihood tree is the
**maximum-weight spanning tree** with mutual informations as weights. This is the **Chow–Liu algorithm**
(1968), and standard spanning-tree algorithms find it in roughly $O(NV^2 + V^2\log V)$ time [@murphy_ml].

Two refinements matter. Every tree has the same number of parameters, so maximum likelihood is a fair score
between trees, but it never leaves an edge out. Score with the marginal likelihood or BIC instead and some
edge weights become negative, so the best structure can be a **forest**, found by the same algorithm after
dropping negative edges [@murphy_ml]. And a **mixture of trees**, fitted by [EM](mixture-models-and-em.qmd)
with a weighted Chow–Liu step, buys more flexibility while each component stays easy to work with
[@murphy_ml].

## Learning DAGs: a score and a search

For general DAGs the approach is to give each graph a **score** and **search** for a high-scoring one.

**The score.** Maximum likelihood will not do. More arrows always fit better, so it picks a fully connected
graph [@murphy_ml]. The standard choice is the **marginal likelihood** $p(D\mid G)$, the probability of the
data with the parameters averaged out, which builds in the "Bayesian Occam's razor" penalty against
complexity [@murphy_ml]. With categorical variables, complete data and Dirichlet priors on each row of each
conditional-probability table, it has a closed form. Better still, it **decomposes**: it is a product of one
local score per node, which depends only on that node and its parents [@murphy_ml]. The usual prior is
**BDeu**. You choose one **equivalent sample size** $\alpha$, an imaginary prior sample spread evenly over
every cell of every table. It is the most widely used prior for this task, and it is built so that graphs
the data cannot tell apart get the same score (below) [@murphy_ml].

**A worked example.** Murphy takes a tiny one from Neapolitan. There are two binary variables and eight
cases. $X_1$ takes value 1 five times. When $X_1=1$, $X_2=1$ four times out of five; when $X_1=2$, once out of
three. Does $X_1\to X_2$ beat no edge at all? With BDeu and $\alpha=4$, $p(D\mid G_1)=7.2150\times10^{-6}$
and $p(D\mid G_2)=6.7465\times10^{-6}$, so with equal prior odds the edge has posterior probability 0.517
[@murphy_ml]. (We reproduced both numbers exactly.) That is close to a coin flip, and it should be: eight
cases say very little. The prior matters here too, which Murphy does not show. Recomputing with other
$\alpha$ (our arithmetic), the edge's probability is 0.37 at $\alpha=1$, 0.46 at $\alpha=2$ and 0.53 at
$\alpha=8$. With so little data, the choice of prior decides whether the link "exists".

**The search.** Knowing an order of the variables makes the search easy. Each node's parents can then be
chosen separately from the nodes before it, with no risk of cycles, which is the **K2 algorithm** (Cooper &
Herskovits 1992) [@murphy_ml]. Without an order, exact search by dynamic programming takes $V2^V$ time and
memory and stops being practical at about 16 nodes [@murphy_ml]. The common practical method is **greedy
hill climbing**: add, delete or reverse one edge at a time, keep the change that most improves the score,
and stop at a local maximum. Because the score decomposes, each proposed change alters only one or two local
terms, so it is cheap to evaluate. Murphy suggests starting from the best tree, using random restarts, and
limiting the candidate edges to those a dependency network found [@murphy_ml]. For discovery, sample graphs
instead by [Metropolis–Hastings](../02-inference/mcmc-in-depth.qmd) and report how often each edge appears.
Sampling *orders* of the variables rather than graphs mixes faster [@murphy_ml].

## Graphs the data cannot tell apart

Some arrows cannot be learned from observational data however much of it you have. The chain $X\to Y\to Z$,
its reverse, and the fork $X\leftarrow Y\to Z$ all say the same thing: $X$ and $Z$ are dependent, but
independent given $Y$. They are **Markov equivalent** [@murphy_ml]. The collider $X\to Y\leftarrow Z$ says the
opposite, and it can be told apart. **Verma and Pearl's theorem** settles the general case: two DAGs are
Markov equivalent exactly when they have the same skeleton (the links, ignoring direction) and the same
**v-structures** (colliders whose parents are not linked) [@murphy_ml]. The
[DAG entry](../05-causal-inference/directed-acyclic-graphs.qmd#sec-testing-dags) gives the same result as a
limit on testing a graph.

A whole equivalence class can be drawn as one **partially directed graph** (PDAG, or essential graph). Its
directed edges are **compelled**: reversing one would create or destroy a v-structure. Its undirected edges
could point either way [@murphy_ml]. So structure learning recovers a DAG only "up to Markov equivalence".
Murphy's advice is not to read too much into the direction of an arrow in a learned graph, since it can often
be reversed with no observable consequence [@murphy_ml]. BDeu's design follows from this: Markov-equivalent
graphs fit the data equally well, so a good score should not separate them. Geiger and Heckerman showed that
the Dirichlet form with pseudo-counts $\alpha\,p_0(\cdot)$ is the only prior that ensures this, given their
independence assumptions [@murphy_ml].

### A social-science example: who plans to go to college?

Murphy's most interesting example is a sociological one (Heckerman et al. 1997). In 1968, Sewell and Shah
measured five variables on 10,318 Wisconsin high-school seniors: sex, socio-economic status (SES), IQ,
parental encouragement and college plans [@murphy_ml]. Heckerman's team computed the exact posterior over all
29,281 five-node DAGs. They gave zero prior probability to graphs in which sex or SES has parents or college
plans has children, which is domain knowledge entering as a prior. With BDeu at $\alpha=5$ (they report the
results are robust for $\alpha$ from 3 to 40), the best graph took almost all the posterior mass
[@murphy_ml].

The tempting reading is causal. SES, IQ and encouragement all point into college plans, and sex acts only
through parental encouragement, which "makes sense". But the graph also has a direct arrow from SES to IQ,
which is surprising and may reflect a hidden common cause [@murphy_ml]. So they added a hidden variable $H$
pointing into SES and IQ (and in some variants encouragement), tried 2 to 6 states for it, and scored 40
models. The best one, a binary $H$, was about $2\times10^{10}$ times more probable than the best model
without a hidden variable [@murphy_ml]. SES and IQ are both more likely to be high when $H=1$, and the
authors read $H$ as "parent quality".

Murphy adds two cautions [@murphy_ml]. First, the $H$–SES arrow can be reversed without changing any
v-structure, so the data cannot say which way it points, which is exactly why hidden variables are hard to
interpret. Second, the hidden-variable model implies the *same* conditional independences among the five
observed variables as the best model without it. A method that looks only at independences in the data (the
**constraint-based** approach of Pearl & Verma and Spirtes et al.) could never choose between them. The
Bayesian score could, because it also weighs **parsimony** [@murphy_ml]. With a hidden variable the marginal
likelihood has no closed form. BIC "often severely underestimates" it and so picks models that are too
simple; the Cheeseman–Stutz approximation does better, and the variational bound of
[variational Bayes EM](variational-inference.qmd) better still [@murphy_ml]. **Structural EM** saves work by
filling in the missing data once and using it to score every neighbouring graph [@murphy_ml].

## From structure to causes

When can a learned DAG be read causally? Murphy states the assumptions [@murphy_ml]:

- **Causal Markov:** an arrow $A\to B$ means $A$ directly causes $B$.
- **Causal sufficiency:** no relevant variable is missing, so there are no unmeasured confounders.
- **Faithfulness:** every conditional independence in the data comes from the graph's structure, not from a
  coincidental cancellation of parameters.

Given these, and all variables observed, some algorithms (for example Chickering's **greedy equivalence
search**) recover the true equivalence class as the sample grows [@murphy_ml]. Each assumption can fail. The
college-plans example shows causal sufficiency failing, since the SES→IQ arrow appeared because a common cause
was left out. That is the [omitted-variable problem](../03-regression/multiple-regression.qmd)
under another name.

The difference between seeing and doing still applies. Yellow-stained fingers are evidence that someone
smokes. Painting someone's fingers yellow is not, because the intervention cuts the arrow from smoking to
stained fingers [@murphy_ml]. If you learn only an equivalence class, the **IDA** method (Maathuis et al.)
computes the causal effect under every DAG in the class and reports the smallest, a lower bound [@murphy_ml].
Its test was ambitious. On yeast gene-expression data it ranked the likely targets of 234 genes, using
knockout experiments as ground truth. At 10% recall its precision was 66%, low but well above the lasso and
elastic net, which were barely better than chance [@murphy_ml].

To orient arrows within an equivalence class you need **interventions**. With experimental data the score
changes in one simple way: when estimating a variable's conditional distribution, drop the cases in which
that variable was *set* by the experimenter, because it was not produced by its usual mechanism (Cooper & Yoo)
[@murphy_ml]. Murphy's example is protein signalling (Sachs et al. 2005): 5,400 measurements of 11 proteins
under chemical interventions. Imperfect interventions are modelled as extra nodes in the graph. Edges with
posterior probability above 0.5 recovered the known pathway with one false negative [@murphy_ml].

Pearl and Mackenzie set the tone for social scientists. Causal discovery, finding the cause of an outcome
from scratch, was Pearl's own "ambitious dream" when he started on graphical models and remains an area of
vigorous research. The main business of causal inference, and of their book, is different: writing down
**plausible causal knowledge** that already exists, combining it with data, and answering questions with
practical value [@pearl_why]. In their view data alone are "profoundly dumb" about causes [@pearl_why]. A
learned graph is a hypothesis to test with design and theory, not a conclusion.

## Undirected Gaussian graphs and the graphical lasso

Undirected structures avoid the acyclicity constraint, but their likelihood does not decompose, so every
proposed change means refitting the model. That rules out the local search used for DAGs [@murphy_ml]. For
Gaussian data there is a better route. In a Gaussian graphical model a missing edge is exactly a zero in the
**precision matrix** $\Omega=\Sigma^{-1}$ (see [MRFs](markov-random-fields.qmd)), so learning the graph means
learning a sparse precision matrix.

**Fitting a known graph** is **covariance selection** (Dempster 1972). The maximum-likelihood fit has a clear
shape. For every pair that *is* linked, and every variance, the fitted covariance equals the sample
covariance. For every pair that is *not* linked, the precision entry is zero, and the fitted covariance for
that pair is whatever value maximises the likelihood under those zeros [@murphy_ml]. In Murphy's four-variable
cycle ($1-2-3-4-1$, from Hastie, Tibshirani & Friedman) the sample covariances of the two unlinked pairs are 5
and 6. The fit replaces them with 1.31 and 0.87 and keeps every other entry [@murphy_ml]. We reproduced these
values with the textbook algorithm (appendix).

**Learning the graph** adds an $\ell_1$ penalty to the precision matrix, the same move as the
[lasso](../04-predictive/regularization.qmd) for regression coefficients. Minimising
$-\log\det\Omega+\operatorname{tr}(S\Omega)+\lambda\lVert\Omega\rVert_1$ pushes small entries to exactly zero.
This is the **graphical lasso** [@murphy_ml]. The objective is convex, and Friedman, Hastie and Tibshirani
solve it by cycling through the variables and fitting one lasso regression per variable. The figure's code
does exactly that. On the protein data, raising $\lambda$ from 0 to 7, 27 and 36 cuts the graph from 55
edges (all of them) to 18, 11 and 8 [@murphy_ml]. Each graph is a plausible hypothesis about which proteins
interact. Murphy compares this with the DAG learned from the same data. The DAG can use the interventions;
the undirected graph can represent the feedback loops known to exist in that pathway, which a DAG cannot
[@murphy_ml].

```{python}
#| label: fig-structure-learning
#| fig-cap: "Structure learning on simulated data (our own example). The truth (left) is a chain of eight Gaussian variables: each is linked only to its neighbours, with partial correlation 0.45, and n = 1,000 draws. A relevance network (middle), which links every pair with |correlation| ≥ 0.2, finds all 7 true links (ink) but adds 11 false ones (orange, dashed): variables two or three steps apart along the chain are correlated too. The graphical lasso (right), coded by hand, estimates which entries of the precision matrix are zero and recovers the chain exactly at λ = 0.40. Bottom: the graphical-lasso path. Small λ keeps spurious links, false links reach zero at about λ = 0.32, and above about 0.5 the penalty starts deleting true links as well. The dotted line marks the λ shown above."
#| code-fold: true
#| code-summary: "Show the figure code (graphical lasso by block coordinate descent, by hand)"

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# --- the truth: an 8-variable chain, written as a sparse precision matrix ----
P, N = 8, 1000
true_edges = {(i, i + 1) for i in range(P - 1)}
Theta = np.eye(P)
for s, t in true_edges:
    Theta[s, t] = Theta[t, s] = -0.45          # partial correlation 0.45 on each link
Sigma = np.linalg.inv(Theta)
rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
X = rng.multivariate_normal(np.zeros(P), Sigma, size=N)
R = np.corrcoef(X, rowvar=False)

# --- the graphical lasso by hand (Friedman, Hastie & Tibshirani 2008) --------
def soft(x, a):
    return np.sign(x) * np.maximum(np.abs(x) - a, 0.0)

def glasso(S, lam, sweeps=100, tol=1e-6):
    p = S.shape[0]
    W = S + lam * np.eye(p)                      # current estimate of the covariance
    B = np.zeros((p, p))
    for _ in range(sweeps):
        W_old = W.copy()
        for j in range(p):                       # one lasso regression per variable
            idx = np.arange(p) != j
            W11, s12, b = W[np.ix_(idx, idx)], S[idx, j], B[idx, j].copy()
            for _ in range(200):                 # coordinate descent ("shooting")
                b_old = b.copy()
                for k in range(p - 1):
                    r = s12[k] - W11[k] @ b + W11[k, k] * b[k]
                    b[k] = soft(r, lam) / W11[k, k]
                if np.max(np.abs(b - b_old)) < tol:
                    break
            B[idx, j] = b
            W[idx, j] = W[j, idx] = W11 @ b
        if np.max(np.abs(W - W_old)) < tol:
            break
    Th = np.zeros((p, p))                        # recover the precision matrix
    for j in range(p):
        idx = np.arange(p) != j
        Th[j, j] = 1 / (W[j, j] - W[idx, j] @ B[idx, j])
        Th[idx, j] = -B[idx, j] * Th[j, j]
    return (Th + Th.T) / 2

def edges(M, thr=1e-8):
    return {(s, t) for s in range(P) for t in range(s + 1, P) if abs(M[s, t]) > thr}

relevance = edges(R, 0.2)                        # edge wherever |correlation| >= 0.2
LAM = 0.40
lasso_graph = edges(glasso(R, LAM))
lams = np.round(np.linspace(0.02, 0.80, 40), 3)
path = [edges(glasso(R, l)) for l in lams]
found = [len(E & true_edges) for E in path]
false = [len(E - true_edges) for E in path]

# --- draw -------------------------------------------------------------------
ang = np.pi / 2 - 2 * np.pi * np.arange(P) / P
xy = np.c_[np.cos(ang), np.sin(ang)]

def draw(ax, E, title):
    for s, t in sorted(E):
        ok = (s, t) in true_edges
        ax.plot(*xy[[s, t]].T, color="#161613" if ok else "#e8531d",
                lw=1.7 if ok else 1.2, ls="-" if ok else "--", zorder=1)
    ax.scatter(*xy.T, s=270, color="#edece3", edgecolor="#161613", lw=1.2, zorder=2)
    for i, (x, y) in enumerate(xy):
        ax.text(x, y, str(i + 1), ha="center", va="center", fontsize=8.5, zorder=3)
    ax.set_title(f"{title}\n{len(E & true_edges)} of 7 true links, "
                 f"{len(E - true_edges)} false", fontsize=9.5)
    ax.set_xlim(-1.3, 1.3); ax.set_ylim(-1.3, 1.3); ax.set_aspect("equal")
    ax.axis("off")

fig = plt.figure(figsize=(8.6, 6.6))
gs = fig.add_gridspec(2, 3, height_ratios=[1, 0.95])
draw(fig.add_subplot(gs[0, 0]), true_edges, "the truth: a chain\n1 – 2 – 3 – … – 8")
draw(fig.add_subplot(gs[0, 1]), relevance, "relevance network\n(|correlation| ≥ 0.2)")
draw(fig.add_subplot(gs[0, 2]), lasso_graph, f"graphical lasso\n(λ = {LAM:.2f})")
ax = fig.add_subplot(gs[1, :])
ax.step(lams, found, where="mid", color="#161613", lw=1.8, label="true links kept (of 7)")
ax.step(lams, false, where="mid", color="#e8531d", lw=1.8, label="false links")
ax.axvline(LAM, color="#8a8a7e", lw=1, ls=":")
ax.set_xlabel("graphical-lasso penalty λ  (larger λ = sparser graph)")
ax.set_ylabel("number of links")
ax.legend(frameon=False, fontsize=8.5, loc="upper right")
fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

## Reading the figure

The chain is the simplest case of Murphy's warning about relevance networks. In our sample, variables 1 and
3 have correlation 0.31, close to its population value of 0.32. The correlation is real, but it is carried
entirely by variable 2, so a threshold on correlations counts it as a link. The graphical lasso asks the
conditional question and leaves it out. To be fair to the relevance network, in this tidy example a cut-off
between 0.38 (the largest correlation between unlinked variables) and 0.51 (the smallest between linked
ones) would also separate them perfectly. Nothing in the data tells you to put the cut-off there, though,
and with hubs and loops the two ranges overlap.

The bottom panel shows the real decision in the graphical lasso: choosing $\lambda$. Too small, and noise
survives as weak links. Too large, and true links disappear. Here there is a wide safe range (about 0.32 to
0.5), because $n=1{,}000$ is large and the true links are strong. With a few hundred respondents and
weaker links the range shrinks or vanishes. In another of our trials (the same chain plus one link closing a
five-variable loop, 300 cases), four false links were still there at λ = 0.40, with all eight true ones. That fits Murphy's warning that the graphical
lasso is **not model-selection consistent** (Meinshausen 2005): even with unlimited data it need not recover
the true graph [@murphy_ml].

## Beyond the Gaussian lasso

Murphy's remaining material sets the limits of the method [@murphy_ml]:

- **Bayesian GGM structure.** The lasso returns one graph. Averaging over graphs gives edge probabilities, as
  for DAGs. This is exact and fast only for **decomposable** (chordal) graphs, and those soon become a small
  minority. Murphy's ratios of decomposable to all undirected graphs for 2 to 8 nodes are 1, 1, 0.95, 0.8,
  0.55, 0.29 and 0.12. Faster approximations (Moghaddam et al. 2009) beat the graphical lasso on both
  prediction and recovering the structure, at similar cost [@murphy_ml].
- **Non-Gaussian continuous data.** The **nonparanormal** (Liu et al. 2009), the family of Gaussian copulas,
  transforms each variable monotonically toward normality using its empirical distribution and then runs the
  graphical lasso [@murphy_ml]. This matters for skewed survey scales.
- **Discrete variables.** With binary or ordinal items the partition function is as hard as in any
  [MRF](markov-random-fields.qmd), so penalised fitting uses a group lasso on each edge's block of weights
  and approximations to the likelihood [@murphy_ml]. The [Ising networks](../04-predictive/measurement-and-ml.qmd)
  used for categorical survey items are in this family [@jacobucci_ml].

## Common pitfalls

::: {.callout-warning title="Where this goes wrong"}
- **Treating a relevance network as a graphical model.** Thresholded correlations draw indirect dependence
  as links, so most pairs end up connected. Use conditional dependence (a precision matrix, a dependency
  network, a scored graph). [@murphy_ml]
- **Reading arrow directions off a learned DAG.** Within a Markov equivalence class many arrows can be
  reversed with no observable consequence. Only compelled edges, prior knowledge or interventions orient
  them. [@murphy_ml]
- **Calling a learned graph causal without its assumptions.** Causal Markov, causal sufficiency and
  faithfulness are all needed. The college-plans SES→IQ arrow came from a missing common cause. [@murphy_ml]
- **Reporting the single best graph as "the" structure.** With little data the posterior is flat (0.517 in
  the eight-case example) and can depend on the prior's $\alpha$. For discovery, report edge probabilities.
  [@murphy_ml]
- **Choosing the score by maximum likelihood.** It always prefers more edges. Use a marginal likelihood or a
  penalised score, and remember that BIC tends to pick graphs that are too simple when variables are hidden.
  [@murphy_ml]
- **Treating one $\lambda$ as the answer.** The graphical-lasso graph changes with the penalty, and the
  method can keep false links or drop true ones even with lots of data. Show the path, or several
  graphs. [@murphy_ml]
- **Using a dependency network as a joint model.** Separately fitted conditionals need not fit together into
  any joint distribution, so Gibbs output from them may mean nothing. [@murphy_ml]
- **Interpreting a discovered hidden variable.** "Parent quality" is a label the analyst chose. Its arrows
  can be reversible and its meaning is not identified by the data. [@murphy_ml]
:::

## Appendix · the mathematics {#sec-structure-math .unnumbered}

::: {.math-appendix}

**Counting graphs.** Undirected graphs on $V$ labelled nodes: $2^{V(V-1)/2}$. DAGs (Robinson 1973):
$$
f(D)=\sum_{i=1}^{D}(-1)^{i+1}\binom{D}{i}2^{i(D-i)}f(D-i),\qquad f(0)=1,
$$
giving 1, 3, 25, 543, 29,281, 3,781,503, 1,138,779,265 for $D=1,\dots,7$.

**Gaussian mutual information.** For a bivariate normal with correlation $\rho$,
$I(X;Y)=-\tfrac12\log(1-\rho^2)$, increasing in $|\rho|$, so thresholding MI thresholds $|\rho|$.

**Tree likelihood and Chow–Liu.** An undirected tree factorises as
$p(x\mid T)=\prod_{t}p(x_t)\prod_{(s,t)\in E}\frac{p(x_s,x_t)}{p(x_s)p(x_t)}$. At the MLE,
$$
\frac{1}{N}\log p(D\mid\hat\theta,T)=\sum_t\sum_k \hat p(x_t=k)\log\hat p(x_t=k)+\sum_{(s,t)\in E(T)} I(x_s;x_t\mid\hat\theta_{st}),
$$
and only the second sum depends on $T$, so the best tree is the maximum-weight spanning tree under weights
$I(x_s;x_t)\ge0$.

**Dirichlet marginal likelihood.** Let $N_{tck}$ count cases with node $t$ in state $k$ and its parents in
configuration $c$, and put a $\mathrm{Dir}(\alpha_{tc})$ prior on each row. Then
$$
p(D\mid G)=\prod_{t=1}^{V}\prod_{c=1}^{C_t}\frac{\Gamma(\alpha_{tc})}{\Gamma(N_{tc}+\alpha_{tc})}\prod_{k=1}^{K_t}\frac{\Gamma(N_{tck}+\alpha_{tck})}{\Gamma(\alpha_{tck})},
$$
with $N_{tc}=\sum_kN_{tck}$, $\alpha_{tc}=\sum_k\alpha_{tck}$. It is a product of local scores, one per
family. BDeu sets $\alpha_{tck}=\alpha/(K_tC_t)$.

**The eight-case example.** Counts: $N_1=(5,3)$; given $X_1=1$, $X_2=(4,1)$; given $X_1=2$, $X_2=(1,2)$;
marginally $X_2=(5,3)$. With $\alpha=4$, $G_1$ ($X_1\to X_2$) uses pseudo-counts $(2,2)$ for $X_1$ and
$(1,1)$ for each row of $X_2$; $G_2$ (no edge) uses $(2,2)$ for both. Then
$p(D\mid G_1)=7.2150\times10^{-6}$, $p(D\mid G_2)=6.7465\times10^{-6}$ and
$P(G_1\mid D)=0.5168$. *(Murphy's text lists $G_2$'s pseudo-counts as $(\alpha/2,\alpha/2)$ "for each
$x_1$", as if $X_2$ still had a parent. That version gives $9.28\times10^{-6}$. His printed figure matches
the no-parent prior, which is the one used here.)* The maximised log-likelihoods differ by 0.88 in $G_1$'s
favour; the marginal likelihood's Occam penalty almost cancels it.

**BIC.** $\mathrm{BIC}(G)=\log p(D\mid\hat\theta,G)-\tfrac{\log N}{2}\dim(G)$. Per node for tables,
$\dim=(K_t-1)C_t$. Murphy's eq. 26.31 writes $K_tC_t$.

**Markov equivalence (Verma & Pearl 1990).** $G_1\equiv G_2$ iff same skeleton and same v-structures. The
BDe prior $\alpha_{tck}=\alpha\,p_0(x_t=k,x_{\mathrm{pa}(t)}=c)$ is the unique Dirichlet prior giving
equivalent graphs equal marginal likelihood under global and local parameter independence (Geiger &
Heckerman 1997).

**Interventional data.** Score node $t$ with counts that skip cases where $t$ was set:
$N_{tck}=\sum_{i:\,x_{it}\text{ not set}}\mathbb I(x_{it}=k,\,x_{i,\mathrm{pa}(t)}=c)$.

**Gaussian graphical models.** With $\Omega=\Sigma^{-1}$ and sample covariance $S$, the profile
log-likelihood is $\ell(\Omega)=\log\det\Omega-\operatorname{tr}(S\Omega)$, with gradient $\Omega^{-1}-S$.
Under a known graph the MLE satisfies $\hat\Sigma_{st}=S_{st}$ for linked pairs and diagonals, and
$\hat\Omega_{st}=0$ for unlinked pairs: a positive-definite completion of $S$. For the cycle $1-2-3-4-1$
with
$$
S=\begin{pmatrix}10&1&5&4\\1&10&2&6\\5&2&10&3\\4&6&3&10\end{pmatrix},\qquad
\hat\Sigma_{13}=1.31,\ \hat\Sigma_{24}=0.87,
$$
all other entries of $\hat\Sigma$ equal to $S$, and $\hat\Omega_{13}=\hat\Omega_{24}=0$.

**The graphical lasso.** Minimise $J(\Omega)=-\log\det\Omega+\operatorname{tr}(S\Omega)+\lambda\lVert\Omega\rVert_1$.
Partition the working covariance $W$ with column $j$ last. The block update solves the lasso
$\min_\beta\ \tfrac12\beta^\top W_{11}\beta-s_{12}^\top\beta+\lambda\lVert\beta\rVert_1$ by coordinate
descent, $\beta_k\leftarrow \mathrm{soft}\big(s_{12,k}-\sum_{l\neq k}W_{11,kl}\beta_l,\ \lambda\big)/W_{11,kk}$,
then sets $w_{12}=W_{11}\beta$. At convergence $\hat\Omega_{jj}=1/(w_{jj}-w_{12}^\top\beta)$ and
$\hat\omega_{12}=-\beta\,\hat\Omega_{jj}$. The optimality conditions are $|W_{st}-S_{st}|\le\lambda$
off the diagonal, with equality wherever $\hat\Omega_{st}\neq0$, and $W_{jj}=S_{jj}+\lambda$. (Our figure's
fits satisfy them to $10^{-6}$.) Dropping the penalty and restricting the support to a known graph gives the
covariance-selection algorithm above.

**The chain in the figure.** $\Omega$ is tridiagonal with 1 on the diagonal and $-0.45$ beside it, so each
link's partial correlation is $0.45$. Inverting gives population correlations of 0.53–0.62 between
neighbours and 0.32–0.38 between variables two apart (larger in the middle of the chain): nonzero, but zero
once you condition on the variable in between.
