Belief Propagation & Expectation Propagation

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source

Inference by gossip. Each node in a graphical model tells its neighbours what it believes, everyone updates, and on a tree the beliefs settle on the exact answer. On a graph with loops the same gossip is only an approximation, but often a very good one, and it is how your phone decodes its signal and how online games rank their players.

Belief propagation (BP) computes the marginal probability of every node in a graphical model by passing local messages along the edges: each node multiplies what it hears from all its neighbours but one, and passes the summary on to the last. On a tree this is exact. Run it anyway on a graph with loops (loopy BP) and you get an approximation that is usually better than mean field, though not guaranteed to converge. Expectation propagation (EP) does the same with messages squeezed into a simple shape, such as a Gaussian, by matching moments, and that is what powers the TrueSkill player rankings.

Why it matters

A graphical model, whether a DAG or a Markov random field, is a compact way to write down a huge joint distribution. The question you actually want answered is usually small: given what I have observed, how likely is this one variable to be in that state? Answering it means summing the joint over every other variable, and the number of terms grows exponentially. Belief propagation is the standard way to do that sum by exploiting the graph’s structure, and the reason Bayesian networks became practical at all (Murphy 2012; Pearl and Mackenzie 2018).

You have already met two special cases. The forward–backward algorithm of hidden Markov models is BP on a chain, and the Kalman smoother is BP on a chain of Gaussians (Murphy 2012). This entry is the general version, for trees and then for graphs with loops, and its approximate cousin EP. It completes the inference toolkit of this track. Variational inference fits a simple distribution by optimisation; MCMC samples. BP and EP pass messages.

The idea: messages on a tree

Pearl describes how he arrived at the algorithm. Reading David Rumelhart’s sketch of how a network of neurons might read the phrase “THE CAR”, passing information up, down and sideways between levels, he became convinced that machine reasoning under uncertainty needed the same message-passing architecture. The hard part was working out what the messages are. His answer, months later: conditional probabilities in one direction and likelihood ratios in the other (Pearl and Mackenzie 2018). A parent tells its child “here is my current belief”, and the child updates by conditional probability. A child tells its parent “here is how much more likely my evidence is under each of your states”, and the parent multiplies its belief by that ratio. Nothing in this is invented, Pearl stresses: both rules are Bayes’s rule. The real challenge, which took him and his students years, was proving that whatever order the messages go out in, the network settles into an equilibrium that matches the textbook answer (Pearl and Mackenzie 2018).

Murphy gives the modern recipe for an undirected tree (Murphy 2012). Each node has local evidence (what its own observation says), and each edge has a potential linking its two ends. Then:

  1. Pick a root and let the tree hang from it. Collect: starting at the leaves, each node multiplies its local evidence by the messages from its children, and turns the result into a message about its parent by passing it through the edge potential. At the root, all the evidence has been seen. Collecting the normalising constants on the way up also gives the probability of the evidence.
  2. Distribute: send messages back down. The message from a node to a child is everything that node knows except what that child told it.

The “except” is the whole trick. If a node sent back its full belief, the child would hear its own evidence echoed and count it twice. After one sweep up and one down, every node’s belief is its exact posterior marginal (Murphy 2012).

There is also a parallel version. Every node starts with flat messages, and at each round all nodes listen to all their neighbours and send new messages at once. After as many rounds as the diameter of the graph (the longest shortest path between two nodes), every node has heard from every other and the beliefs are exact. Murphy compares this to a systolic array, after its resemblance to a beating heart, and notes the analogy with solving linear systems: the up-and-down sweep is like the Gauss–Seidel method, the parallel version like Jacobi (Murphy 2012). For a Gaussian model the messages are themselves Gaussians, so each one is a mean and a precision. Computing the posterior means is then the same as solving the linear system with the precision matrix, and on a tree BP does it in time proportional to the number of variables rather than its cube (Murphy 2012).

Exact inference on any graph: variable elimination and the junction tree

BP is exact on trees. On a graph with loops you can still get the exact answer, at a price that depends on how the loops are arranged.

Variable elimination: push the sums inside

Murphy’s example, from Koller and Friedman, is the student network: a course’s coherence and difficulty, the student’s intelligence, their grade, SAT score, recommendation letter, whether they get the job, and whether they are happy (Murphy 2012). To get P(J) by brute force you sum the joint distribution over every combination of the other seven variables, 2^7 = 128 terms even if everything is binary. Variable elimination (Zhang and Poole 1996; also called bucket elimination, and in genetics the peeling algorithm for pedigrees) instead pushes each sum as far inside the product as it will go (Murphy 2012). Summing out C first only involves the two factors that mention C, and leaves a small new factor in D alone. Then D is summed out of the factors that mention it, and so on, until only J is left. A conditional such as P(J \mid I = 1, H = 0) works the same way with the observed variables held fixed. Normalising at the end gives the probability of the evidence (Murphy 2012).

Nothing here is specific to probability. The same steps work whenever “add” and “multiply” obey the distributive law (a commutative semiring). Swap the sum for a max and you get the most probable configuration (the Viterbi idea of hidden Markov models); swap in min and plus (the min-sum form) and you minimise a total cost; swap in “or” and “and” and you get logical satisfiability. Murphy also lists constraint satisfaction and the fast Fourier transform as instances (Murphy 2012).

The order matters: fill-in edges and treewidth

The cost of variable elimination is set by the largest factor it has to build, because building it means looping over every combination of that factor’s variables (Murphy 2012). That size depends on the elimination order. Summing out a variable joins all its neighbours into one factor, which is the same as adding fill-in edges between any of them not already connected. In the student network, the order C, D, I, H, G, S, L adds a single fill-in (between grade and SAT, when intelligence is summed out) and never handles more than four variables at once. The order G, I, S, L, H, C, D starts by summing out grade, which sits at the centre of the graph. It adds nine fill-ins and its first step has to handle six variables at once (Murphy 2012). (We checked both counts by running the elimination symbolically.) With binary variables that is a table of 2^4 = 16 entries against 2^6 = 64, and the gap grows exponentially with the size of the graph.

The size of the largest factor, minus one, is the induced width of that order, and the smallest induced width over all orders is the graph’s treewidth. The best possible cost of exact inference grows exponentially in the treewidth (Murphy 2012). Chains and trees have treewidth 1: eliminate from the leaves inwards and nothing is ever filled in. That is why Markov chains and tree models are so widely used. A grid is the bad case. An m \times n lattice has treewidth of order \min(m, n), so exact inference on a 100 \times 100 Ising model would take on the order of 2^{100} steps (Murphy 2012). Finding the order with the smallest width is itself NP-hard, so in practice greedy heuristics are used (Murphy 2012).

Variable elimination has a second weakness: it answers one question at a time. To get every marginal of an HMM it would rerun the whole sweep for each time point, while forward–backward stores the forward messages and reuses them. It is the same contrast as answering one query at a time against BP’s collect-and-distribute on a tree (Murphy 2012).

The junction tree: BP on clusters

The junction tree algorithm combines the two. It runs variable elimination once on paper, adding the fill-in edges. The result is a chordal (triangulated) graph, in which every cycle of four or more nodes has a shortcut edge across it. The maximal cliques of a chordal graph can be arranged as a tree: in the student network, {C, D}, {G, I, D}, {G, S, I}, {G, J, S, L} and {H, G, J} (Murphy 2012). The tree must have the running intersection property: every variable’s cliques form one connected piece of the tree, so anything two cliques need to agree on is passed along the path between them (Murphy 2012). Neighbouring cliques share a separator: here {D}, {G, I}, {G, S} and {G, J}.

Inference is then ordinary belief propagation with cliques as the nodes: collect messages to a root, distribute them back, and each clique ends up holding the exact joint distribution of its variables, from which any single-variable marginal follows (Murphy 2012). The Hugin (Lauritzen–Spiegelhalter) form stores each message in its separator and, on the way down, divides out what was sent up, so nothing is counted twice. Murphy calls the result junction tree calibration (Murphy 2012). On an HMM the cliques are consecutive pairs of states, the separators are single states, and the junction tree algorithm is forward–backward. For Gaussian models it is the Kalman smoother (Murphy 2012). Swapping sum for max again gives the most probable configuration, and the distribute phase can also draw posterior samples (Murphy 2012).

The cost is the same as variable elimination’s, exponential in the treewidth, but every marginal comes from one pair of sweeps (Murphy 2012).

Why approximation is unavoidable

Could a cleverer algorithm avoid the exponential cost? Almost certainly not. Murphy shows that exact inference can encode logical satisfiability: build a network whose final node is “true” only if a 3-SAT formula has a satisfying assignment, and computing its probability answers the SAT problem (Murphy 2012). Exact inference is in fact #P-hard, harder than NP-hard, because the normalising constant has to count satisfying assignments, not just find one. There is not even a guarantee of polynomial-time approximation in general (Murphy 2012). Murphy’s summary of the toolkit (Murphy 2012):

Method Works exactly when Notes
Forward–backward the graph is a chain HMMs, Kalman smoother
Belief propagation the graph is a tree this entry
Variable elimination treewidth is low one query at a time
Junction tree treewidth is low all marginals at once
Loopy BP, EP approximate below
Mean field approximate variational inference
Gibbs sampling approximate MCMC

The rest of this entry is about the approximate rows.

Loopy belief propagation

On a graph with cycles, a node’s message can travel round a loop and come back to it, so evidence gets counted more than once. Pearl saw this in 1988: messages “may circulate indefinitely around the loops”, and even when they settle, the equilibrium need not be the true posterior. He nonetheless suggested BP as an approximation for loopy networks (Murphy 2012). Loopy BP is exactly that: run the same message updates on the loopy graph and hope (Murphy 2012).

The idea took off by an unusual route. In 1993 Claude Berrou, an engineer at France Telecom, announced the turbo code, an error-correcting code that came close to the theoretical limit on efficiency (Pearl and Mackenzie 2018). His trick was to encode each message twice, once directly and once after scrambling it, and to decode by letting two decoders repeatedly pass each other their beliefs about the bits. No formula decodes such a message exactly, but the iteration recovered the right bits almost every time, with much shorter codewords (Pearl and Mackenzie 2018). Berrou did not know that his decoder was belief propagation on a loopy Bayesian network. David MacKay recognised it five years later, and McEliece and colleagues published the equivalence in 1998 (Pearl and Mackenzie 2018; Murphy 2012). Robert Gallager had in fact invented a code decoded the same way, the low-density parity-check (LDPC) code, back in 1960, too early for the hardware it needed; MacKay rediscovered it in 1998 (Pearl and Mackenzie 2018). Pearl’s tally: 3G phones used turbo codes, 4G phones Gallager’s turbo-like codes, so “you have a Bayesian network in your pocket” (Pearl and Mackenzie 2018).

Murphy suggests why loopy BP works so well there. LDPC graphs are locally tree-like: their cycles are long, of length about \log m in a graph of m nodes, so over a few rounds each node’s neighbourhood looks like a tree (Murphy 2012). After the coding results, Murphy and colleagues showed experimentally in 1999 that loopy BP also works well on other graphical models, and it spread from there (Murphy 2012).

Factor graphs

Messages are easiest to write on a factor graph: a bipartite graph with round nodes for variables and square nodes for factors, and an edge from each factor to every variable it involves (Murphy 2012). Any directed model converts by making one factor per conditional distribution, and any undirected model by making one factor per clique or per edge. BP then has two kinds of message: a variable tells a factor the product of what all other factors told it, and a factor tells a variable what it implies once the other variables’ messages are summed out (Murphy 2012). Does it converge, and is it right?

Not always, on both counts (Murphy 2012). Two tools help.

  • The computation tree. Running loopy BP for T rounds gives exactly the answer of ordinary BP on a tree that unrolls the loops T levels deep. If the edges are weak, the far leaves of that tree barely affect the root and the iteration settles; strong edges let distant echoes keep arriving (Murphy 2012).
  • Practical fixes. Damping sends a weighted average of the new and the previous message (a weight of about 0.5 is standard). Asynchronous updates use new messages as soon as they are computed, rather than updating everything at once. Residual BP (Elidan et al. 2006) always sends next the message that changed most; Sutton and McCallum’s refinement was about five times faster again (Murphy 2012). On an 11 × 11 Ising grid with random couplings, Murphy reports (from Koller and Friedman) that damped asynchronous updating made every message converge, against about half undamped and few for the synchronous schedule (Murphy 2012). Oscillation can itself be a sign that the approximation is poor for this graph (Murphy 2012).

On accuracy, the clean results are few. With a single loop, the max-product version (the one that finds the most probable configuration) gives the right answer if it converges. For Gaussian models, if loopy BP converges, the means are exact but the variances are not, typically too small: the beliefs are overconfident (Murphy 2012).

A three-variable loop shows the Gaussian result with our own numbers. Take precision matrix entries of 1 on the diagonal and −0.4 off it (so the three variables are positively correlated), with linear terms (1, 0, -1). The exact posterior means are 0.714, 0 and −0.714, each with variance 2.14. Gaussian loopy BP returns the same three means and a variance of 1.67 for each: about 22% too small, because the loop feeds each variable’s evidence back to it as if it were new. (Flip the off-diagonal signs to +0.4 and our check gives variances that are too large, 1.67 against an exact 1.30, which is why Murphy says “typically”.)

import itertools, math
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# ---- left: 3 x 3 Ising grid, exact vs loopy BP vs mean field ----
n, h = 3, 0.1
N = n * n
E = [(r*n+c, r*n+c+1) for r in range(n) for c in range(n-1)] + \
    [(r*n+c, (r+1)*n+c) for r in range(n-1) for c in range(n)]
nbrs = {i: [] for i in range(N)}
for s, t in E:
    nbrs[s].append(t); nbrs[t].append(s)
states = np.array(list(itertools.product([-1, 1], repeat=N)))

def exact(J):                                     # brute force over all 512 states
    logw = h * states.sum(1) + J * sum(states[:, s] * states[:, t] for s, t in E)
    w = np.exp(logw - logw.max()); w /= w.sum()
    return w @ (states == 1)

def loopy_bp(J, damp=0.5):
    x = np.array([-1, 1])
    psi_n, psi_e = np.exp(h * x), np.exp(J * np.outer(x, x))
    m = {(s, t): np.full(2, 0.5) for s in range(N) for t in nbrs[s]}
    for _ in range(3000):
        new = {}
        for s, t in m:                            # all incoming messages except the recipient's
            prod = psi_n * np.prod([m[(u, s)] for u in nbrs[s] if u != t], axis=0)
            msg = psi_e.T @ prod
            new[(s, t)] = damp * msg / msg.sum() + (1 - damp) * m[(s, t)]
        done = max(np.abs(new[k] - m[k]).max() for k in m) < 1e-12
        m = new
        if done:
            break
    bel = np.array([psi_n * np.prod([m[(u, s)] for u in nbrs[s]], axis=0) for s in range(N)])
    return bel[:, 1] / bel.sum(1)

    for _ in range(3000):

            mu[s] = np.tanh(h + J * mu[nbrs[s]].sum())
    return (1 + mu) / 2

Why loopy BP works: the variational view

Murphy explains, following Wainwright and Jordan’s 300-page review (which its authors call “the monster”), what loopy BP is actually computing (Murphy 2012). Exact inference can be written as an optimisation over every set of node and edge marginals that some joint distribution could produce. That set is too complex to search. Loopy BP searches a larger, simpler set instead: pseudo-marginals that only have to agree locally, so each edge’s table sums to its two nodes’ tables. They need not fit together globally. Murphy’s three-node example is a triangle in which each pair of binary variables agrees with probability 0.8, 0.8 and 0.2. Each edge table is fine on its own, but no joint distribution can make X_1 = X_2 and X_2 = X_3 each 80% likely while X_1 = X_3 is only 20% likely (Murphy 2012). On a tree, locally consistent tables always fit together; with loops they need not.

Loopy BP pairs this relaxed set with the Bethe approximation to the entropy, the formula that is exact for trees (sum of node entropies minus edge mutual informations), named after the physicist Hans Bethe. The key result is that the fixed points of loopy BP are exactly the stationary points of this Bethe problem: the messages are exponentiated Lagrange multipliers for the local-consistency constraints (Murphy 2012). The objective is not concave, so there can be several fixed points. On trees it is exact.

This also explains how loopy BP compares with mean field (Murphy 2012). Loopy BP is exact on trees and keeps track of pairs of variables, while mean field only keeps single-variable marginals, so loopy BP is usually more accurate. Less obviously, mean field’s objective has far more local optima, and Weiss (2001) found that starting mean field from uniform or random values often gave poor results where loopy BP from uniform messages did well. Mean field’s advantages are that it gives a lower bound on the normalising constant, useful inside a learning algorithm, and that it extends more easily to other distributions (Murphy 2012). Grouping tightly looped nodes into clusters gives generalised BP, more accurate at more cost (Murphy 2012).

Expectation propagation

Loopy BP needs messages you can compute exactly: discrete tables, or Gaussians in a Gaussian model. Many models mix the two, and then the true message has no tidy form. Expectation propagation (Minka 2001) keeps every message in a simple family, usually Gaussian, and chooses the member that best matches the true one (Murphy 2012).

It grows out of assumed density filtering (ADF), an online method from the state-space chapter: predict, update with the next observation exactly, then project the result back onto a Gaussian by matching its mean and variance (Murphy 2012). Matching moments minimises the forward KL divergence from the exact posterior to the approximation, the “covering” direction the variational-inference entry contrasts with the reverse KL that mean field uses (Murphy 2012). ADF’s weakness is that the answer depends on the order in which the data arrive. EP fixes this by making repeated passes, refining each term’s approximation in the light of all the others (Murphy 2012). Each step for one term i:

  1. Remove term i’s current approximation from the overall approximation (for Gaussians, subtract its precision and precision-weighted mean). What is left is the cavity: the beliefs implied by all the other terms.
  2. Include the true term i in place of its approximation. The result is usually not Gaussian.
  3. Project: find the Gaussian with the same mean and variance as that result.
  4. Update term i’s approximation as the new Gaussian divided by the cavity, so it can be removed again on the next pass.

Two quirks follow (Murphy 2012). A term’s approximation is a function, not a distribution, and its “variance” can come out negative (it curves up rather than down) even though the overall approximation is fine. And EP is not guaranteed to converge. In return it can be very efficient: on Minka’s clutter problem, estimating a mean when some observations are background outliers, EP gave better accuracy per unit of computer time than variational Bayes or MCMC (Murphy 2012). And loopy BP is a special case of EP, in which the simple family is a product of independent node marginals and the terms being refined are the edges (Murphy 2012).

TrueSkill: ranking players from who beat whom

Murphy’s showcase application is TrueSkill (Herbrich et al. 2007), the system Microsoft built to rank players on Xbox Live, which processes over 10^5 games a day, “one of the largest application[s] of Bayesian statistics to date” (Murphy 2012). The model has three layers (Murphy 2012):

  • Each player i has a hidden skill s_i, with a Gaussian prior, which can drift slowly over time.
  • In a game, each player’s performance is their skill plus Gaussian noise; a team’s performance is the sum of its members’.
  • The outcome is the sign of the performance difference: the higher performance wins.

This is very like a probit regression whose inputs are differences of latent skills, and with logistic noise instead of Gaussian it becomes the Bradley–Terry model of paired comparisons (Murphy 2012). The win/loss outcome is not conjugate to the Gaussian skills, so even on a tree inference needs iteration. EP keeps every message Gaussian. Murphy sets the algorithm out as six message steps per game; the key one squeezes the truncated belief about the performance difference (“we know the winner performed better”) back into a Gaussian by matching moments (Murphy 2012).

The update has a clear reading. Murphy’s two correction functions, \Psi for the mean and \Lambda for the variance, are near zero when the expected player wins and large after an upset: an unsurprising result barely moves the ratings, a surprising one moves them a lot (Murphy 2012). Our own numbers, with every prior variance 1 and performance-noise variance 1:

Game (prior skill means) Prior P(winner wins) Winner’s new mean Loser’s new mean New variance (each)
Two newcomers, 0 vs 0 0.50 0.46 −0.46 0.79
Favourite wins, 1 vs 0 0.72 1.27 −0.27 0.84
Underdog wins, 0 beats 1 0.28 0.69 0.31 0.75
Big upset, 0 beats 3 0.04 1.23 1.77 0.71

The favourite gains 0.27 for an expected win; the 0-rated underdog who beats a 3-rated player gains 1.23 and the loser drops by the same amount. Every game also shrinks the variance, by more after a surprise, because a surprising result is more informative. Running Murphy’s six message steps by hand on the first row reproduces these numbers exactly: the moment-matched belief about the performance difference has mean 1.38 and variance 1.09, the message back to the winner has mean 2.17 and variance 3.71, and combining it with the prior gives 0.46 and 0.79.

Murphy demonstrates the batch version on players with a known partial order: sample games in which a stronger player always beats a weaker one, run five EP iterations, and the posterior means recover the ranking (Murphy 2012). The right panel of the figure repeats the experiment with our own six players and 26 games. In practice EP is combined with ADF so ratings update online as games come in (Murphy 2012). The same researchers extended the approach to ad recommendation and to predicting click-through rates on the Bing search engine, and built a general EP toolbox, Infer.NET (Murphy 2012).

A social-science reading

Pearl’s other showcase is Bonaparte, the Netherlands Forensic Institute’s system for identifying disaster victims from relatives’ DNA, used after the Malaysia Airlines Flight 17 crash (Pearl and Mackenzie 2018). It turns a family pedigree into a Bayesian network whose hidden nodes are the alleles each person got from each parent, enters the DNA results, and lets belief propagation spread them through the family, so that even an aunt’s or a second cousin’s sample helps. Each identification made then feeds back into the network (Pearl and Mackenzie 2018). Pearl highlights two virtues: the network reacts as a whole to new evidence, and every step can be traced, unlike a black box. The final call is still left to human experts, who combine the network’s likelihood ratios with the other evidence (Pearl and Mackenzie 2018).

Both stories have the same shape: many local pieces of evidence (a game result, a DNA test), one global latent structure (a skill ranking, a family tree), and messages that let each piece of evidence update beliefs about everything connected to it. The same structure appears in social data wherever paired comparisons are used to rank things: survey respondents choosing between two policies, reviewers comparing two proposals, or students rating essays two at a time. (This reading is ours.)

Common pitfalls

WarningWhere this goes wrong
  • Trusting loopy BP’s uncertainty. Evidence that loops back is double-counted, so beliefs are typically overconfident (0.83 against an exact 0.74 for the centre spin at J = 0.5; Gaussian variances 1.67 against 2.14 in our loop). Means can be right while variances are wrong. (Murphy 2012)
  • Treating non-convergence as a software bug. Loopy BP and EP are not guaranteed to converge. Try damping and asynchronous schedules first, but persistent oscillation can mean the approximation is poor for this graph. (Murphy 2012)
  • Sending a node its own message back. The message to a neighbour must leave out what that neighbour said. Including it makes even a tree double-count evidence. (Murphy 2012)
  • Reading pseudo-marginals as a joint distribution. Loopy BP’s node and edge tables need only agree locally; there may be no distribution that has them all (the 0.8 / 0.8 / 0.2 triangle). (Murphy 2012)
  • Reading EP’s site approximations as probabilities. Individual factor approximations can have negative “variance”. Only the combined approximation is a distribution. (Murphy 2012)
  • Treating a skill rating as fixed. TrueSkill’s rating is a posterior mean with a variance; a player with two games (P6 in the figure) is far less certain than one with thirteen. Rank on the mean alone and the newcomer’s luck becomes a ranking. (Our gloss on the model’s design.) (Murphy 2012)
  • Assuming exact inference is just slow. Its cost grows exponentially in the treewidth, and a bad elimination order can make even a small network expensive (four variables at a time against six in the student network). On grids and densely looped graphs, exact answers are out of reach, which is why the approximations exist. (Murphy 2012)
  • Letting the network decide. Even Bonaparte, an exact and transparent BP system, hands its likelihood ratios to human experts for the final identification. (Pearl and Mackenzie 2018)

Appendix · the mathematics

BP on a pairwise model. For p(x)\propto\prod_s\psi_s(x_s)\prod_{(s,t)}\psi_{st}(x_s,x_t), the sum-product message and belief are m_{s\to t}(x_t)=\sum_{x_s}\psi_s(x_s)\,\psi_{st}(x_s,x_t)\prod_{u\in\mathrm{nbr}(s)\setminus t}m_{u\to s}(x_s), \qquad \mathrm{bel}_s(x_s)\propto\psi_s(x_s)\prod_{t\in\mathrm{nbr}(s)}m_{t\to s}(x_s). On a tree, after the collect and distribute passes (or diameter-many parallel rounds), \mathrm{bel}_s is the exact marginal p(x_s\mid v), and p(v)=\prod_t Z_t from the local normalisers.

Factor graphs. Variable to factor: m_{x\to f}(x)=\prod_{h\in\mathrm{nbr}(x)\setminus f}m_{h\to x}(x). Factor to variable: m_{f\to x}(x)=\sum_{y}f(x,y)\prod_{y\in\mathrm{nbr}(f)\setminus x}m_{y\to f}(y). Belief: \mathrm{bel}(x)\propto\prod_{f\in\mathrm{nbr}(x)}m_{f\to x}(x).

Variable elimination and treewidth. Eliminating x_t replaces the factors containing it by \tau_t(\cdot)=\sum_{x_t}\prod_{c\ni t}\psi_c(x_c), whose scope is x_t’s current neighbours. With K states per variable and created cliques \mathcal C(G(\prec)), the cost is \sum_{c}K^{|c|}=O(K^{w(\prec)+1}), where the induced width is w(\prec)=\max_c|c|-1; the treewidth is w=\min_\prec w(\prec). Student network: order C, D, I, H, G, S, L creates cliques {C,D}, {D,I,G}, {G,I,S}, {G,J,H}, {G,L,S,J} (w=3, one fill-in G–S); order G, I, S, L, H, C, D starts with the six-variable clique {G,I,D,L,J,H} (w=5, nine fill-ins).

Junction tree (Hugin). The tree represents p(x)=\prod_{c}\psi_c(x_c)\big/\prod_{s}\psi_s(x_s) over cliques and separators. Upward, \psi^*_{ij}(S_{ij})=\sum_{C_i\setminus S_{ij}}\psi_i(C_i) and \psi^*_j\propto\psi_j\,\psi^*_{ij}/\psi_{ij}; downward, \psi^{**}_{ij}=\sum_{C_i\setminus S_{ij}}\psi^*_i and \psi^{**}_j\propto\psi^*_j\,\psi^{**}_{ij}/\psi^*_{ij}. After both passes each clique potential is p(x_{C}\mid v). Cost O(|\mathcal C|K^{w+1}); for Gaussians O(|\mathcal C|w^3).

Damping. \tilde m^{k}_{ts}=\lambda\,m_{ts}+(1-\lambda)\,\tilde m^{k-1}_{ts}, 0\le\lambda\le1; the figure uses \lambda=0.5.

Gaussian BP. For p(x)\propto\exp(-\tfrac12x^\top Ax+b^\top x), messages are Gaussian with precision and mean P_{i\to j}=-\frac{A_{ij}^2}{P_{i\setminus j}},\qquad \mu_{i\to j}=-\frac{A_{ij}\,\mu_{i\setminus j}}{P_{i\to j}}, \qquad P_{i\setminus j}=A_{ii}+\sum_{k\in\mathrm{nbr}(i)\setminus j}P_{k\to i}, with \mu_{i\setminus j}=(b_i+\sum_{k\ne j}P_{k\to i}\mu_{k\to i})/P_{i\setminus j} (the standard form, here in our notation). For the loop with A_{ii}=1, A_{ij}=-0.4, b=(1,0,-1): A^{-1}b=(0.714,0,-0.714) and \mathrm{diag}(A^{-1})=2.14; the fixed point has every message precision -0.16/P with P=1+P_{\text{msg}}, so P_{\text{msg}}=-0.2, belief precision 1-0.4=0.6 and variance 1.67, while the means agree with A^{-1}b.

Local polytope and Bethe. Pseudo-marginals \tau satisfy \sum_{x_s}\tau_s(x_s)=1 and \sum_{x_t}\tau_{st}(x_s,x_t)=\tau_s(x_s); the set \mathbb L(G) contains the marginal polytope \mathbb M(G), with equality iff G is a tree. The Bethe entropy and variational problem are H_{\text{Bethe}}(\tau)=\sum_s H_s(\tau_s)-\sum_{(s,t)}I_{st}(\tau_{st}),\qquad \max_{\tau\in\mathbb L(G)}\ \theta^\top\tau+H_{\text{Bethe}}(\tau), an approximation to \log Z. Setting the Lagrangian’s gradient to zero and writing M_{ts}(x_s)=\exp\lambda_{ts}(x_s) gives \tau_s\propto e^{\theta_s}\prod_{t}M_{ts} and M_{ts}(x_s)\propto\sum_{x_t}\exp\{\theta_{st}(x_s,x_t)+\theta_t(x_t)\}\prod_{u\in\mathrm{nbr}(t)\setminus s}M_{ut}(x_t): the LBP updates.

EP. With p(x)\propto\prod_i f_i(x) and q(x)\propto\prod_i\tilde f_i(x), repeat: cavity q_{-i}=q/\tilde f_i; q^{\text{new}}=\arg\min_q\mathrm{KL}\big(Z_i^{-1}f_iq_{-i}\,\|\,q\big), solved by matching moments, with Z_i=\int q_{-i}f_i\,dx; then \tilde f_i=Z_i\,q^{\text{new}}/q_{-i}. For Gaussians, division subtracts natural parameters (precision \lambda and \eta=\lambda\mu).

TrueSkill, one game. With s_i\sim\mathcal N(\mu_i,v_i), unit performance noise folded into the game factor, and i beating j: the difference has prior d\sim\mathcal N(\mu_i-\mu_j,\,c^2) with c^2=1+v_i+v_j (Murphy’s simplified model). Truncating to d>0 and matching moments, \mu_d=\mu_{ij}+c\,\Psi(\mu_{ij}/c),\qquad v_d=c^2\big[1-\Lambda(\mu_{ij}/c)\big],\qquad \Psi(x)=\frac{\phi(x)}{\Phi(x)},\quad \Lambda(x)=\Psi(x)\big(\Psi(x)+x\big), with \mu_{ij}=\mu_i-\mu_j. Passing the message back gives the closed form \mu_i'=\mu_i+\frac{v_i}{c}\Psi(\mu_{ij}/c), v_i'=v_i\big[1-\frac{v_i}{c^2}\Lambda(\mu_{ij}/c)\big], and symmetrically \mu_j'=\mu_j-\frac{v_j}{c}\Psi. Two newcomers: c^2=3, \Psi(0)=0.798, \Lambda(0)=0.637, so \mu'=\pm0.798/\sqrt3=\pm0.461 and v'=1-0.637/3=0.788. Through Murphy’s message steps: \mu_d=1.382, v_d=1.090; the message back up has precision 1/1.090-1/3=0.584 (variance 1.712) and mean 2.171; adding the loser’s variance and the unit noise gives a message to the winner with variance 3.712, and 1+1/3.712=1.269 is the posterior precision (variance 0.788), mean (2.171/3.712)/1.269=0.461.

**Belief propagation** (BP) computes the marginal probability of every node in a graphical model by passing
local **messages** along the edges: each node multiplies what it hears from all its neighbours but one,
and passes the summary on to the last. On a **tree** this is exact. Run it anyway on a graph with loops
(**loopy BP**) and you get an approximation that is usually better than mean field, though not guaranteed
to converge. **Expectation propagation** (EP) does the same with messages squeezed into a simple shape,
such as a Gaussian, by matching moments, and that is what powers the **TrueSkill** player rankings.
:::

## Why it matters

A graphical model, whether a [DAG](../05-causal-inference/directed-acyclic-graphs.qmd) or a
[Markov random field](markov-random-fields.qmd), is a compact way to write down a huge joint distribution.
The question you actually want answered is usually small: *given what I have observed, how likely is this one
variable to be in that state?* Answering it means summing the joint over every other variable, and the
number of terms grows exponentially. Belief propagation is the standard way to do that sum by exploiting the
graph's structure, and the reason Bayesian networks became practical at all [@murphy_ml; @pearl_why].

You have already met two special cases. The forward–backward algorithm of
[hidden Markov models](hidden-markov-models.qmd) is BP on a chain, and the
[Kalman smoother](../04-predictive/state-space-and-kalman-filter.qmd) is BP on a chain of Gaussians
[@murphy_ml]. This entry is the general version, for trees and then for graphs with loops, and its
approximate cousin EP. It completes the inference toolkit of this track. [Variational inference](variational-inference.qmd)
fits a simple distribution by optimisation; [MCMC](../02-inference/mcmc-in-depth.qmd) samples. BP and EP
**pass messages**.

## The idea: messages on a tree

Pearl describes how he arrived at the algorithm. Reading David Rumelhart's sketch of how a network of
neurons might read the phrase "THE CAR", passing information up, down and sideways between levels, he
became convinced that machine reasoning under uncertainty needed the same message-passing architecture. The
hard part was working out what the messages *are*. His answer, months later: **conditional probabilities
in one direction and likelihood ratios in the other** [@pearl_why]. A parent tells its child "here is my
current belief", and the child updates by conditional probability. A child tells its parent "here is how
much more likely my evidence is under each of your states", and the parent multiplies its belief by that
ratio. Nothing in this is invented, Pearl stresses: both rules are Bayes's rule. The real challenge, which
took him and his students years, was proving that whatever order the messages go out in, the network
settles into an equilibrium that matches the textbook answer [@pearl_why].

Murphy gives the modern recipe for an undirected tree [@murphy_ml]. Each node has **local evidence** (what
its own observation says), and each edge has a **potential** linking its two ends. Then:

1. **Pick a root** and let the tree hang from it. **Collect**: starting at the leaves, each node multiplies
   its local evidence by the messages from its children, and turns the result into a message about its
   parent by passing it through the edge potential. At the root, all the evidence has been seen. Collecting
   the normalising constants on the way up also gives the probability of the evidence.
2. **Distribute**: send messages back down. The message from a node to a child is everything that node
   knows **except what that child told it**.

The "except" is the whole trick. If a node sent back its full belief, the child would hear its own evidence
echoed and count it twice. After one sweep up and one down, every node's belief is its exact posterior
marginal [@murphy_ml].

There is also a **parallel** version. Every node starts with flat messages, and at each round all nodes
listen to all their neighbours and send new messages at once. After as many rounds as the **diameter** of
the graph (the longest shortest path between two nodes), every node has heard from every other and the
beliefs are exact. Murphy compares this to a **systolic array**, after its resemblance to a beating heart,
and notes the analogy with solving linear systems: the up-and-down sweep is like the Gauss–Seidel method,
the parallel version like Jacobi [@murphy_ml]. For a Gaussian model the messages are themselves Gaussians,
so each one is a mean and a precision. Computing the posterior means is then the same as solving the linear
system with the precision matrix, and on a tree BP does it in time proportional to the number of variables
rather than its cube [@murphy_ml].

## Exact inference on any graph: variable elimination and the junction tree {#sec-ve-jta}

BP is exact on trees. On a graph with loops you can still get the exact answer, at a price that depends on
how the loops are arranged.

### Variable elimination: push the sums inside

Murphy's example, from Koller and Friedman, is the **student network**: a course's **c**oherence and
**d**ifficulty, the student's **i**ntelligence, their **g**rade, **S**AT score, recommendation **l**etter,
whether they get the **j**ob, and whether they are **h**appy [@murphy_ml]. To get $P(J)$ by brute force you
sum the joint distribution over every combination of the other seven variables, $2^7 = 128$ terms even if
everything is binary. **Variable elimination** (Zhang and Poole 1996; also called bucket elimination, and in
genetics the **peeling** algorithm for pedigrees) instead pushes each sum as far inside the product as it
will go [@murphy_ml]. Summing out $C$ first only involves the two factors that mention $C$, and leaves a
small new factor in $D$ alone. Then $D$ is summed out of the factors that mention it, and so on, until only
$J$ is left. A conditional such as $P(J \mid I = 1, H = 0)$ works the same way with the observed variables
held fixed. Normalising at the end gives the **probability of the evidence** [@murphy_ml].

Nothing here is specific to probability. The same steps work whenever "add" and "multiply" obey the
distributive law (a **commutative semiring**). Swap the sum for a max and you get the most probable
configuration (the Viterbi idea of [hidden Markov models](hidden-markov-models.qmd)); swap in
min and plus (the **min-sum** form) and you minimise a total cost; swap in "or" and "and" and you get
logical satisfiability. Murphy
also lists constraint satisfaction and the fast Fourier transform as instances [@murphy_ml].

### The order matters: fill-in edges and treewidth

The cost of variable elimination is set by the **largest factor it has to build**, because building it
means looping over every combination of that factor's variables [@murphy_ml]. That size depends on the
**elimination order**. Summing out a variable joins all its neighbours into one factor, which is the same as
adding **fill-in edges** between any of them not already connected. In the student network, the order
C, D, I, H, G, S, L adds a single fill-in (between grade and SAT, when intelligence is summed out) and never
handles more than four variables at once. The order G, I, S, L, H, C, D starts by summing out grade, which
sits at the centre of the graph. It adds nine fill-ins and its first step has to handle six variables at
once [@murphy_ml]. (We checked both counts by running the elimination symbolically.) With binary variables
that is a table of $2^4 = 16$ entries against $2^6 = 64$, and the gap grows exponentially with the size of
the graph.

The size of the largest factor, minus one, is the **induced width** of that order, and the smallest induced
width over all orders is the graph's **treewidth**. The best possible cost of exact inference grows
**exponentially in the treewidth** [@murphy_ml]. Chains and trees have treewidth 1: eliminate from the
leaves inwards and nothing is ever filled in. That is why Markov chains and tree models are so widely used.
A grid is the bad case. An $m \times n$ lattice has treewidth of order $\min(m, n)$, so exact inference on a
$100 \times 100$ Ising model would take on the order of $2^{100}$ steps [@murphy_ml]. Finding the order with
the smallest width is itself NP-hard, so in practice greedy heuristics are used [@murphy_ml].

Variable elimination has a second weakness: it answers **one question at a time**. To get every marginal of
an HMM it would rerun the whole sweep for each time point, while forward–backward stores the forward
messages and reuses them. It is the same contrast as answering one query at a time against BP's
collect-and-distribute on a tree [@murphy_ml].

### The junction tree: BP on clusters

The **junction tree algorithm** combines the two. It runs variable elimination once **on paper**, adding the
fill-in edges. The result is a **chordal** (triangulated) graph, in which every cycle of four or more nodes
has a shortcut edge across it. The maximal cliques of a chordal graph can be arranged as a tree: in the
student network, {C, D}, {G, I, D}, {G, S, I}, {G, J, S, L} and {H, G, J} [@murphy_ml]. The tree must have
the **running intersection property**: every variable's cliques form one connected piece of the tree, so
anything two cliques need to agree on is passed along the path between them [@murphy_ml]. Neighbouring
cliques share a **separator**: here {D}, {G, I}, {G, S} and {G, J}.

Inference is then ordinary belief propagation with **cliques as the nodes**: collect messages to a root,
distribute them back, and each clique ends up holding the exact joint distribution of its variables, from
which any single-variable marginal follows [@murphy_ml]. The **Hugin** (Lauritzen–Spiegelhalter) form
stores each message in its separator and, on the way down, divides out what was sent up, so nothing is
counted twice. Murphy calls the result **junction tree calibration** [@murphy_ml]. On an HMM the cliques are
consecutive pairs of states, the separators are single states, and the junction tree algorithm *is*
forward–backward. For Gaussian models it is the Kalman smoother [@murphy_ml]. Swapping sum for max again
gives the most probable configuration, and the distribute phase can also draw posterior samples
[@murphy_ml].

The cost is the same as variable elimination's, exponential in the treewidth, but every marginal comes from
one pair of sweeps [@murphy_ml].

### Why approximation is unavoidable

Could a cleverer algorithm avoid the exponential cost? Almost certainly not. Murphy shows that exact
inference can encode logical **satisfiability**: build a network whose final node is "true" only if a 3-SAT
formula has a satisfying assignment, and computing its probability answers the SAT problem [@murphy_ml].
Exact inference is in fact **#P-hard**, harder than NP-hard, because the normalising constant has to *count*
satisfying assignments, not just find one. There is not even a guarantee of polynomial-time approximation
in general [@murphy_ml]. Murphy's summary of the toolkit [@murphy_ml]:

| Method | Works exactly when | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Forward–backward | the graph is a chain | [HMMs](hidden-markov-models.qmd), Kalman smoother |
| Belief propagation | the graph is a tree | this entry |
| Variable elimination | treewidth is low | one query at a time |
| Junction tree | treewidth is low | all marginals at once |
| Loopy BP, EP | approximate | below |
| Mean field | approximate | [variational inference](variational-inference.qmd) |
| Gibbs sampling | approximate | [MCMC](../02-inference/mcmc-in-depth.qmd) |

The rest of this entry is about the approximate rows.

## Loopy belief propagation

On a graph with cycles, a node's message can travel round a loop and come back to it, so evidence gets
counted more than once. Pearl saw this in 1988: messages "may circulate indefinitely around the loops", and
even when they settle, the equilibrium need not be the true posterior. He nonetheless suggested BP as an
approximation for loopy networks [@murphy_ml]. **Loopy BP** is exactly that: run the same message updates
on the loopy graph and hope [@murphy_ml].

The idea took off by an unusual route. In 1993 Claude Berrou, an engineer at France Telecom, announced the
**turbo code**, an error-correcting code that came close to the theoretical limit on efficiency [@pearl_why].
His trick was to encode each message twice, once directly and once after scrambling it, and to decode by
letting two decoders repeatedly pass each other their beliefs about the bits. No formula decodes such a
message exactly, but the iteration recovered the right bits almost every time, with much shorter codewords
[@pearl_why]. Berrou did not know that his decoder was belief propagation on a loopy Bayesian network.
David MacKay recognised it five years later, and McEliece and colleagues published the equivalence in
1998 [@pearl_why; @murphy_ml]. Robert Gallager had in fact invented a code decoded the same way, the
**low-density parity-check** (LDPC) code, back in 1960, too early for the hardware it needed; MacKay
rediscovered it in 1998 [@pearl_why]. Pearl's tally: 3G phones used turbo codes, 4G phones Gallager's
turbo-like codes, so "you have a Bayesian network in your pocket" [@pearl_why].

Murphy suggests why loopy BP works so well there. LDPC graphs are **locally tree-like**: their cycles are
long, of length about $\log m$ in a graph of $m$ nodes, so over a few rounds each node's neighbourhood
looks like a tree [@murphy_ml]. After the coding results, Murphy and colleagues showed experimentally in
1999 that loopy BP also works well on other graphical models, and it spread from there [@murphy_ml].

### Factor graphs

Messages are easiest to write on a **factor graph**: a bipartite graph with round nodes for variables and
square nodes for factors, and an edge from each factor to every variable it involves [@murphy_ml]. Any
directed model converts by making one factor per conditional distribution, and any undirected model by
making one factor per clique or per edge. BP then has two kinds of message: a variable tells a factor the
product of what all *other* factors told it, and a factor tells a variable what it implies once the other
variables' messages are summed out [@murphy_ml]. The TrueSkill model below is drawn this way.

### Does it converge, and is it right?

Not always, on both counts [@murphy_ml]. Two tools help.

- **The computation tree.** Running loopy BP for $T$ rounds gives exactly the answer of ordinary BP on a
  tree that unrolls the loops $T$ levels deep. If the edges are weak, the far leaves of that tree barely
  affect the root and the iteration settles; strong edges let distant echoes keep arriving [@murphy_ml].
- **Practical fixes.** **Damping** sends a weighted average of the new and the previous message (a weight of
  about 0.5 is standard). **Asynchronous** updates use new messages as soon as they are computed, rather
  than updating everything at once. **Residual BP** (Elidan et al. 2006) always sends next the message
  that changed most; Sutton and McCallum's refinement was about five times faster again
  [@murphy_ml]. On an 11 × 11 Ising grid with random couplings, Murphy reports (from Koller and Friedman)
  that damped asynchronous updating made every message converge, against about half undamped and few
  for the synchronous schedule [@murphy_ml]. Oscillation can itself be a sign that the approximation is poor
  for this graph [@murphy_ml].

On accuracy, the clean results are few. With a single loop, the max-product version (the one that finds the
most probable configuration) gives the right answer if it converges. For Gaussian models, if loopy BP
converges, the **means are exact but the variances are not**, typically too small: the beliefs are
overconfident [@murphy_ml].

A three-variable loop shows the Gaussian result with our own numbers. Take precision matrix entries of 1 on
the diagonal and −0.4 off it (so the three variables are positively correlated), with linear terms
$(1, 0, -1)$. The exact posterior means are 0.714, 0 and −0.714, each with variance 2.14. Gaussian loopy BP
returns **the same three means** and a variance of **1.67** for each: about 22% too small, because the loop
feeds each variable's evidence back to it as if it were new. (Flip the off-diagonal signs to +0.4 and our
check gives variances that are too *large*, 1.67 against an exact 1.30, which is why Murphy says
"typically".)

Left: the marginal probability that the centre spin of a 3 × 3 Ising grid is +1, with a small field (h = 0.1) on every spin, as the coupling J between neighbours grows. Exact (ink) is computed by summing all 512 states; loopy belief propagation (orange, damped) tracks it closely up to about J = 0.3 and then overshoots; mean field (olive, dashed) overshoots much earlier. Both approximations become overconfident at strong coupling, LBP less so. Right: TrueSkill by expectation propagation, following Murphy's demonstration: six players with a known partial order (P1 beats P2 and P3; P2 and P3 beat P4; P4 beats P5; P3 beats P6), 26 games drawn at random from those pairings with the stronger player always winning, five EP sweeps from a standard-normal prior. P6 played only two games, both lost to P3, so the data say only that P6 is below P3: its estimate has the widest uncertainty. All numbers are our own computation."

import itertools, math
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# ---- left: 3 x 3 Ising grid, exact vs loopy BP vs mean field ----
n, h = 3, 0.1
N = n * n
E = [(r*n+c, r*n+c+1) for r in range(n) for c in range(n-1)] + \
    [(r*n+c, (r+1)*n+c) for r in range(n-1) for c in range(n)]
nbrs = {i: [] for i in range(N)}
for s, t in E:
    nbrs[s].append(t); nbrs[t].append(s)
states = np.array(list(itertools.product([-1, 1], repeat=N)))

def exact(J):                                     # brute force over all 512 states
    logw = h * states.sum(1) + J * sum(states[:, s] * states[:, t] for s, t in E)
    w = np.exp(logw - logw.max()); w /= w.sum()
    return w @ (states == 1)

def loopy_bp(J, damp=0.5):
    x = np.array([-1, 1])
    psi_n, psi_e = np.exp(h * x), np.exp(J * np.outer(x, x))
    m = {(s, t): np.full(2, 0.5) for s in range(N) for t in nbrs[s]}
    for _ in range(3000):
        new = {}
        for s, t in m:                            # all incoming messages except the recipient's
            prod = psi_n * np.prod([m[(u, s)] for u in nbrs[s] if u != t], axis=0)
            msg = psi_e.T @ prod
            new[(s, t)] = damp * msg / msg.sum() + (1 - damp) * m[(s, t)]
        done = max(np.abs(new[k] - m[k]).max() for k in m) < 1e-12
        m = new
        if done:
            break
    bel = np.array([psi_n * np.prod([m[(u, s)] for u in nbrs[s]], axis=0) for s in range(N)])
    return bel[:, 1] / bel.sum(1)

def mean_field(J):
    mu = np.zeros(N)
    for _ in range(3000):
        for s in range(N):
            mu[s] = np.tanh(h + J * mu[nbrs[s]].sum())
    return (1 + mu) / 2

Js = np.linspace(0.0, 1.0, 41)
ex = np.array([exact(J)[4] for J in Js])
lb = np.array([loopy_bp(J)[4] for J in Js])
mf = np.array([mean_field(J)[4] for J in Js])

# ---- right: TrueSkill by expectation propagation (Murphy's six steps) ----
order = [(0, 1), (0, 2), (1, 3), (2, 3), (3, 4), (2, 5)]   # (winner, loser): stronger always wins
rng = np.random.default_rng(3)
games = [order[k] for k in rng.integers(0, len(order), 26)]
P, mu0, v0 = 6, 0.0, 1.0
lam_msg = np.zeros((len(games), 2)); eta_msg = np.zeros((len(games), 2))  # game -> player messages

def Psi(x):
    return math.exp(-x * x / 2) / math.sqrt(2 * math.pi) / (0.5 * (1 + math.erf(x / math.sqrt(2))))

def skill_posteriors():                           # step 1: prior times every game's message
    lam = np.full(P, 1 / v0); eta = np.full(P, mu0 / v0)
    for k, (a, b) in enumerate(games):
        lam[a] += lam_msg[k, 0]; eta[a] += eta_msg[k, 0]
        lam[b] += lam_msg[k, 1]; eta[b] += eta_msg[k, 1]
    return lam, eta

for sweep in range(5):
    for g, (w, l) in enumerate(games):
        lam, eta = skill_posteriors()
        lw, ew = lam[w] - lam_msg[g, 0], eta[w] - eta_msg[g, 0]   # step 2: divide out this game
        ll, el = lam[l] - lam_msg[g, 1], eta[l] - eta_msg[g, 1]
        vw, mw, vl, ml = 1 / lw, ew / lw, 1 / ll, el / ll
        vd, md = 1 + vw + vl, mw - ml                             # step 3: belief about the difference
        x = md / math.sqrt(vd)                                    # step 4: moment-match "winner won"
        mg = md + math.sqrt(vd) * Psi(x)
        vg = vd * (1 - Psi(x) * (Psi(x) + x))
        lu, eu = 1 / vg - 1 / vd, mg / vg - md / vd               # step 5: divide out the incoming message
        vu, mu_u = 1 / lu, eu / lu
        v_to_w, m_to_w = 1 + vu + vl, mu_u + ml                   # step 6: back up to the two players
        v_to_l, m_to_l = 1 + vu + vw, mw - mu_u
        lam_msg[g] = [1 / v_to_w, 1 / v_to_l]
        eta_msg[g] = [m_to_w / v_to_w, m_to_l / v_to_l]

lam, eta = skill_posteriors()
post_m, post_s = eta / lam, np.sqrt(1 / lam)
played = np.bincount(np.array(games).ravel(), minlength=P)

fig, (ax1, ax2) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(9.4, 4.0))
ax1.plot(Js, ex, color="#161613", lw=2.0, label="exact (all 512 states)")
ax1.plot(Js, lb, color="#e8531d", lw=2.0, label="loopy belief propagation")
ax1.plot(Js, mf, color="#8a8a7e", lw=1.6, ls="--", label="mean field")
ax1.set_xlabel("coupling strength J")
ax1.set_ylabel("P(centre spin = +1)")
ax1.set_title("3 × 3 Ising grid, small field h = 0.1", fontsize=10)
ax1.set_ylim(0.45, 1.02)
ax1.legend(loc="lower right", fontsize=8.5, frameon=False)

ax2.errorbar(np.arange(P), post_m, yerr=post_s, fmt="o", color="#161613", ms=6, capsize=4, lw=1.6)
ax2.axhline(0, color="#b7b6a8", lw=1, ls=":")
ax2.set_xticks(np.arange(P))
ax2.set_xticklabels([f"P{i+1}\n({played[i]} games)" for i in range(P)], fontsize=8.5)
ax2.set_ylabel("skill: posterior mean ± 1 SD")
ax2.set_title("TrueSkill by EP: 26 games, 5 sweeps", fontsize=10)
fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()

spins barely talk to each other. As the coupling grows, mean field, which replaces each neighbour by its

$J = 0.5$, because evidence that has gone round the grid's loops comes back looking like new evidence. As a
check on the code, the same routine on a five-node chain, which has no loops, reproduces the exact marginals
to four decimals.

## Why loopy BP works: the variational view

Murphy explains, following Wainwright and Jordan's 300-page review (which its authors call "the
monster"), what loopy BP is actually computing [@murphy_ml]. Exact inference can be written as an
optimisation over every set of node and edge marginals that **some** joint distribution could produce.
That set is too complex to search. Loopy BP searches a larger, simpler set instead: **pseudo-marginals**
that only have to agree **locally**, so each edge's table sums to its two nodes' tables. They need not fit
together globally. Murphy's three-node example is a triangle in which each pair of binary variables
agrees with probability 0.8, 0.8 and 0.2. Each edge table is fine on its own, but no joint distribution
can make $X_1 = X_2$ and $X_2 = X_3$ each 80% likely while $X_1 = X_3$ is only 20% likely [@murphy_ml]. On a
tree, locally consistent tables always fit together; with loops they need not.

Loopy BP pairs this relaxed set with the **Bethe approximation** to the entropy, the formula that is exact
for trees (sum of node entropies minus edge mutual informations), named after the physicist Hans Bethe. The
key result is that **the fixed points of loopy BP are exactly the stationary points of this Bethe problem**:
the messages are exponentiated Lagrange multipliers for the local-consistency constraints [@murphy_ml]. The
objective is not concave, so there can be several fixed points. On trees it is exact.

This also explains how loopy BP compares with [mean field](variational-inference.qmd) [@murphy_ml]. Loopy
BP is exact on trees and keeps track of pairs of variables, while mean field only keeps single-variable
marginals, so loopy BP is usually more accurate. Less obviously, mean field's objective has far more local
optima, and Weiss (2001) found that starting mean field from uniform or random values often gave poor
results where loopy BP from uniform messages did well. Mean field's advantages are that it gives a **lower
bound** on the normalising constant, useful inside a learning algorithm, and that it extends more easily to
other distributions [@murphy_ml]. Grouping tightly looped nodes into clusters gives **generalised BP**, more
accurate at more cost [@murphy_ml].

## Expectation propagation

Loopy BP needs messages you can compute exactly: discrete tables, or Gaussians in a Gaussian model. Many
models mix the two, and then the true message has no tidy form. **Expectation propagation** (Minka 2001)
keeps every message in a simple family, usually Gaussian, and chooses the member that best matches the
true one [@murphy_ml].

It grows out of **assumed density filtering** (ADF), an online method from the state-space chapter:
predict, update with the next observation exactly, then **project** the result back onto a Gaussian by
matching its mean and variance [@murphy_ml]. Matching moments minimises the **forward** KL divergence from
the exact posterior to the approximation, the "covering" direction the
[variational-inference entry](variational-inference.qmd) contrasts with the reverse KL that mean field uses
[@murphy_ml]. ADF's weakness is that the answer depends on the order in which the data arrive. EP fixes
this by making repeated passes, refining each term's approximation in the light of all the others
[@murphy_ml]. Each step for one term $i$:

1. **Remove** term $i$'s current approximation from the overall approximation (for Gaussians, subtract its
   precision and precision-weighted mean). What is left is the **cavity**: the beliefs implied by all the
   other terms.
2. **Include** the true term $i$ in place of its approximation. The result is usually not Gaussian.
3. **Project**: find the Gaussian with the same mean and variance as that result.
4. **Update** term $i$'s approximation as the new Gaussian divided by the cavity, so it can be removed
   again on the next pass.

Two quirks follow [@murphy_ml]. A term's approximation is a function, not a distribution, and its "variance"
can come out **negative** (it curves up rather than down) even though the overall approximation is fine.
And EP is not guaranteed to converge. In return it can be very efficient: on Minka's **clutter problem**,
estimating a mean when some observations are background outliers, EP gave better accuracy per unit of
computer time than variational Bayes or MCMC [@murphy_ml]. And **loopy BP is a special case of EP**, in
which the simple family is a product of independent node marginals and the terms being refined are the
edges [@murphy_ml].

## TrueSkill: ranking players from who beat whom

Murphy's showcase application is **TrueSkill** (Herbrich et al. 2007), the system Microsoft built to rank
players on Xbox Live, which processes over $10^5$ games a day, "one of the largest application[s] of
Bayesian statistics to date" [@murphy_ml]. The model has three layers [@murphy_ml]:

- Each player $i$ has a hidden **skill** $s_i$, with a Gaussian prior, which can drift slowly over time.
- In a game, each player's **performance** is their skill plus Gaussian noise; a team's performance is the
  sum of its members'.
- The **outcome** is the sign of the performance difference: the higher performance wins.

This is very like a probit regression whose inputs are differences of latent skills, and with logistic
noise instead of Gaussian it becomes the **Bradley–Terry** model of paired comparisons [@murphy_ml]. The
win/loss outcome is not conjugate to the Gaussian skills, so even on a tree inference needs iteration. EP
keeps every message Gaussian. Murphy sets the algorithm out as six message steps per game; the key one
squeezes the truncated belief about the performance difference ("we know the winner performed better")
back into a Gaussian by matching moments [@murphy_ml].

The update has a clear reading. Murphy's two correction functions, $\Psi$ for the mean and $\Lambda$ for the
variance, are near zero when the expected player wins and large after an upset: an unsurprising result
barely moves the ratings, a surprising one moves them a lot [@murphy_ml]. Our own numbers, with every prior
variance 1 and performance-noise variance 1:

| Game (prior skill means) | Prior P(winner wins) | Winner's new mean | Loser's new mean | New variance (each) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Two newcomers, 0 vs 0 | 0.50 | 0.46 | −0.46 | 0.79 |
| Favourite wins, 1 vs 0 | 0.72 | 1.27 | −0.27 | 0.84 |
| Underdog wins, 0 beats 1 | 0.28 | 0.69 | 0.31 | 0.75 |
| Big upset, 0 beats 3 | 0.04 | 1.23 | 1.77 | 0.71 |

The favourite gains 0.27 for an expected win; the 0-rated underdog who beats a 3-rated player gains 1.23
and the loser drops by the same amount. Every game also **shrinks the variance**, by more after a
surprise, because a surprising result is more informative. Running Murphy's six message steps by hand on the
first row reproduces these numbers exactly: the moment-matched belief about the performance difference has
mean 1.38 and variance 1.09, the message back to the winner has mean 2.17 and variance 3.71, and combining
it with the prior gives 0.46 and 0.79.

Murphy demonstrates the batch version on players with a known partial order: sample games in which a
stronger player always beats a weaker one, run five EP iterations, and the posterior means recover the
ranking [@murphy_ml]. The right panel of the figure repeats the experiment with our own six players and 26
games. In practice EP is combined with ADF so ratings update online as games come in [@murphy_ml]. The
same researchers extended the approach to ad recommendation and to predicting click-through rates on the
Bing search engine, and built a general EP toolbox, Infer.NET [@murphy_ml].

### A social-science reading

Pearl's other showcase is **Bonaparte**, the Netherlands Forensic Institute's system for identifying
disaster victims from relatives' DNA, used after the Malaysia Airlines Flight 17 crash [@pearl_why]. It
turns a family pedigree into a Bayesian network whose hidden nodes are the alleles each person got from
each parent, enters the DNA results, and lets belief propagation spread them through the family, so that
even an aunt's or a second cousin's sample helps. Each identification made then feeds back into the network
[@pearl_why]. Pearl highlights two virtues: the network reacts as a whole to new evidence, and every step
can be traced, unlike a black box. The final call is still left to human experts, who combine the
network's likelihood ratios with the other evidence [@pearl_why].

Both stories have the same shape: many **local** pieces of evidence (a game result, a DNA test), one
**global** latent structure (a skill ranking, a family tree), and messages that let each piece of evidence
update beliefs about everything connected to it. The same structure appears in social data wherever
**paired comparisons** are used to rank things: survey respondents choosing between two policies,
reviewers comparing two proposals, or students rating essays two at a time. *(This reading is ours.)*

## Common pitfalls

::: {.callout-warning title="Where this goes wrong"}
- **Trusting loopy BP's uncertainty.** Evidence that loops back is double-counted, so beliefs are typically
  overconfident (0.83 against an exact 0.74 for the centre spin at $J = 0.5$; Gaussian variances 1.67
  against 2.14 in our loop). Means can be right while variances are wrong. [@murphy_ml]
- **Treating non-convergence as a software bug.** Loopy BP and EP are not guaranteed to converge. Try damping
  and asynchronous schedules first, but persistent oscillation can mean the approximation is poor for this
  graph. [@murphy_ml]
- **Sending a node its own message back.** The message to a neighbour must leave out what that neighbour
  said. Including it makes even a tree double-count evidence. [@murphy_ml]
- **Reading pseudo-marginals as a joint distribution.** Loopy BP's node and edge tables need only agree
  locally; there may be no distribution that has them all (the 0.8 / 0.8 / 0.2 triangle). [@murphy_ml]
- **Reading EP's site approximations as probabilities.** Individual factor approximations can have negative
  "variance". Only the combined approximation is a distribution. [@murphy_ml]
- **Treating a skill rating as fixed.** TrueSkill's rating is a posterior mean with a variance; a player with
  two games (P6 in the figure) is far less certain than one with thirteen. Rank on the mean alone and
  the newcomer's luck becomes a ranking. *(Our gloss on the model's design.)* [@murphy_ml]
- **Assuming exact inference is just slow.** Its cost grows exponentially in the treewidth, and a bad
  elimination order can make even a small network expensive (four variables at a time against six in the
  student network). On grids and densely looped graphs, exact answers are out of reach, which is why the
  approximations exist. [@murphy_ml]
- **Letting the network decide.** Even Bonaparte, an exact and transparent BP system, hands its likelihood
  ratios to human experts for the final identification. [@pearl_why]
:::

## Appendix · the mathematics {#sec-bp-math .unnumbered}

::: {.math-appendix}

**BP on a pairwise model.** For $p(x)\propto\prod_s\psi_s(x_s)\prod_{(s,t)}\psi_{st}(x_s,x_t)$, the
sum-product message and belief are
$$
m_{s\to t}(x_t)=\sum_{x_s}\psi_s(x_s)\,\psi_{st}(x_s,x_t)\prod_{u\in\mathrm{nbr}(s)\setminus t}m_{u\to s}(x_s),
\qquad
\mathrm{bel}_s(x_s)\propto\psi_s(x_s)\prod_{t\in\mathrm{nbr}(s)}m_{t\to s}(x_s).
$$
On a tree, after the collect and distribute passes (or diameter-many parallel rounds), $\mathrm{bel}_s$ is
the exact marginal $p(x_s\mid v)$, and $p(v)=\prod_t Z_t$ from the local normalisers.

**Factor graphs.** Variable to factor: $m_{x\to f}(x)=\prod_{h\in\mathrm{nbr}(x)\setminus f}m_{h\to x}(x)$.
Factor to variable: $m_{f\to x}(x)=\sum_{y}f(x,y)\prod_{y\in\mathrm{nbr}(f)\setminus x}m_{y\to f}(y)$.
Belief: $\mathrm{bel}(x)\propto\prod_{f\in\mathrm{nbr}(x)}m_{f\to x}(x)$.

**Variable elimination and treewidth.** Eliminating $x_t$ replaces the factors containing it by
$\tau_t(\cdot)=\sum_{x_t}\prod_{c\ni t}\psi_c(x_c)$, whose scope is $x_t$'s current neighbours. With
$K$ states per variable and created cliques $\mathcal C(G(\prec))$, the cost is
$\sum_{c}K^{|c|}=O(K^{w(\prec)+1})$, where the induced width is $w(\prec)=\max_c|c|-1$; the treewidth is
$w=\min_\prec w(\prec)$. Student network: order C, D, I, H, G, S, L creates cliques {C,D}, {D,I,G},
{G,I,S}, {G,J,H}, {G,L,S,J} ($w=3$, one fill-in G–S); order G, I, S, L, H, C, D starts with the
six-variable clique {G,I,D,L,J,H} ($w=5$, nine fill-ins).

**Junction tree (Hugin).** The tree represents $p(x)=\prod_{c}\psi_c(x_c)\big/\prod_{s}\psi_s(x_s)$ over
cliques and separators. Upward, $\psi^*_{ij}(S_{ij})=\sum_{C_i\setminus S_{ij}}\psi_i(C_i)$ and
$\psi^*_j\propto\psi_j\,\psi^*_{ij}/\psi_{ij}$; downward, $\psi^{**}_{ij}=\sum_{C_i\setminus S_{ij}}\psi^*_i$
and $\psi^{**}_j\propto\psi^*_j\,\psi^{**}_{ij}/\psi^*_{ij}$. After both passes each clique potential is
$p(x_{C}\mid v)$. Cost $O(|\mathcal C|K^{w+1})$; for Gaussians $O(|\mathcal C|w^3)$.

**Damping.** $\tilde m^{k}_{ts}=\lambda\,m_{ts}+(1-\lambda)\,\tilde m^{k-1}_{ts}$, $0\le\lambda\le1$; the
figure uses $\lambda=0.5$.

**Gaussian BP.** For $p(x)\propto\exp(-\tfrac12x^\top Ax+b^\top x)$, messages are Gaussian with precision
and mean
$$
P_{i\to j}=-\frac{A_{ij}^2}{P_{i\setminus j}},\qquad \mu_{i\to j}=-\frac{A_{ij}\,\mu_{i\setminus j}}{P_{i\to j}},
\qquad P_{i\setminus j}=A_{ii}+\sum_{k\in\mathrm{nbr}(i)\setminus j}P_{k\to i},
$$
with $\mu_{i\setminus j}=(b_i+\sum_{k\ne j}P_{k\to i}\mu_{k\to i})/P_{i\setminus j}$ (the standard form, here
in our notation). For the loop with $A_{ii}=1$, $A_{ij}=-0.4$, $b=(1,0,-1)$: $A^{-1}b=(0.714,0,-0.714)$ and
$\mathrm{diag}(A^{-1})=2.14$; the fixed point has every message precision $-0.16/P$ with
$P=1+P_{\text{msg}}$, so $P_{\text{msg}}=-0.2$, belief precision $1-0.4=0.6$ and variance $1.67$, while the
means agree with $A^{-1}b$.

**Local polytope and Bethe.** Pseudo-marginals $\tau$ satisfy $\sum_{x_s}\tau_s(x_s)=1$ and
$\sum_{x_t}\tau_{st}(x_s,x_t)=\tau_s(x_s)$; the set $\mathbb L(G)$ contains the marginal polytope
$\mathbb M(G)$, with equality iff $G$ is a tree. The Bethe entropy and variational problem are
$$
H_{\text{Bethe}}(\tau)=\sum_s H_s(\tau_s)-\sum_{(s,t)}I_{st}(\tau_{st}),\qquad
\max_{\tau\in\mathbb L(G)}\ \theta^\top\tau+H_{\text{Bethe}}(\tau),
$$
an approximation to $\log Z$. Setting the Lagrangian's gradient to zero and writing
$M_{ts}(x_s)=\exp\lambda_{ts}(x_s)$ gives
$\tau_s\propto e^{\theta_s}\prod_{t}M_{ts}$ and
$M_{ts}(x_s)\propto\sum_{x_t}\exp\{\theta_{st}(x_s,x_t)+\theta_t(x_t)\}\prod_{u\in\mathrm{nbr}(t)\setminus s}M_{ut}(x_t)$:
the LBP updates.

**EP.** With $p(x)\propto\prod_i f_i(x)$ and $q(x)\propto\prod_i\tilde f_i(x)$, repeat: cavity
$q_{-i}=q/\tilde f_i$; $q^{\text{new}}=\arg\min_q\mathrm{KL}\big(Z_i^{-1}f_iq_{-i}\,\|\,q\big)$, solved by
matching moments, with $Z_i=\int q_{-i}f_i\,dx$; then $\tilde f_i=Z_i\,q^{\text{new}}/q_{-i}$. For Gaussians,
division subtracts natural parameters (precision $\lambda$ and $\eta=\lambda\mu$).

**TrueSkill, one game.** With $s_i\sim\mathcal N(\mu_i,v_i)$, unit performance noise folded into the game
factor, and $i$ beating $j$: the difference has prior $d\sim\mathcal N(\mu_i-\mu_j,\,c^2)$ with
$c^2=1+v_i+v_j$ (Murphy's simplified model). Truncating to $d>0$ and matching moments,
$$
\mu_d=\mu_{ij}+c\,\Psi(\mu_{ij}/c),\qquad v_d=c^2\big[1-\Lambda(\mu_{ij}/c)\big],\qquad
\Psi(x)=\frac{\phi(x)}{\Phi(x)},\quad \Lambda(x)=\Psi(x)\big(\Psi(x)+x\big),
$$
with $\mu_{ij}=\mu_i-\mu_j$. Passing the message back gives the closed form
$\mu_i'=\mu_i+\frac{v_i}{c}\Psi(\mu_{ij}/c)$, $v_i'=v_i\big[1-\frac{v_i}{c^2}\Lambda(\mu_{ij}/c)\big]$, and
symmetrically $\mu_j'=\mu_j-\frac{v_j}{c}\Psi$. Two newcomers: $c^2=3$, $\Psi(0)=0.798$, $\Lambda(0)=0.637$,
so $\mu'=\pm0.798/\sqrt3=\pm0.461$ and $v'=1-0.637/3=0.788$. Through Murphy's message steps: $\mu_d=1.382$,
$v_d=1.090$; the message back up has precision $1/1.090-1/3=0.584$ (variance 1.712) and mean 2.171; adding
the loser's variance and the unit noise gives a message to the winner with variance 3.712, and
$1+1/3.712=1.269$ is the posterior precision (variance 0.788), mean $(2.171/3.712)/1.269=0.461$.

:::
