Empirical Bayes & Automatic Relevance Determination

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source

Let the data decide how hard the prior should pull. With one dial you get a self-tuning ridge regression; with one dial per variable, irrelevant variables switch themselves off.

A penalty is a prior, and every prior has a strength. Instead of tuning that strength by cross-validation, you can pick the value under which the observed data were most probable, with the coefficients averaged out. Do this once and you have a ridge regression that tunes itself. Give every variable its own strength and something surprising happens: the variables that do not help predict the outcome have their strength driven to infinity, and their coefficients are set to zero.

Why it matters

Every regularised model in this encyclopedia has a dial. Ridge and the lasso have \lambda; a Gaussian process has a length-scale; a hierarchical model has a between-group variance. The usual way to set a dial is cross-validation: try a grid of values and keep the one that predicts held-out data best. That works for one dial, or two. It does not work for twenty. Murphy makes the point directly: you cannot use cross-validation to tune D separate hyperparameters, one per feature. (Murphy 2012)

This entry covers the alternative. Empirical Bayes, also called type-II maximum likelihood and, in machine learning, the evidence procedure, chooses the dial by maximising the marginal likelihood: the probability of the data with the coefficients integrated out. (Murphy 2012) It scales to one dial per variable, and in that form, called automatic relevance determination (ARD) or sparse Bayesian learning (SBL), it becomes a Bayesian method for deciding which variables matter. It sits next to selection stability, which asks the same question by resampling, and it reappears inside Gaussian processes, neural networks and factor models.

How Bayesian do you want to be?

Empirical Bayes is easiest to place on a ladder. Write the model in two levels: the data depend on parameters \theta (the regression coefficients), and the parameters have a prior whose strength is set by a hyperparameter \eta (a precision, a variance, a \lambda). Murphy lists five ways to handle the two levels, ordered by how many of them you integrate over: the more integrals, the “more Bayesian” the method. (Murphy 2012)

Method What it does
Maximum likelihood Pick \theta to fit the data. No prior at all.
MAP estimation Pick \theta to fit the data and the prior, with \eta fixed in advance. The lasso and ridge live here.
ML-II (empirical Bayes) Integrate \theta out, then pick \eta to maximise the probability of the data.
MAP-II As ML-II, but with a prior on \eta too.
Full Bayes Integrate over both \theta and \eta; report the joint posterior.

The step from MAP to ML-II is the one that matters here. Under MAP, someone has to choose \eta. Under ML-II, the data choose it, through the marginal likelihood. Murphy’s argument for why this is safe is that \eta is usually far lower-dimensional than \theta, so it is much less prone to overfitting. (Murphy 2012)

Two sources disagree about how to feel about this. Murphy concedes that empirical Bayes breaks the rule that a prior should be chosen without looking at the data, but treats it as a cheap approximation to a full hierarchical model, much as MAP approximates a full posterior. He adds that it has good frequentist properties, which is why non-Bayesians use it: the James–Stein estimator can be derived this way. (Murphy 2012) Lambert is less forgiving. He calls empirical Bayes a “half-Bayesian/half-Frequentist” compromise that uses the data to set the priors, finds it convoluted, and prefers to put priors on the hyperparameters and fit the full hierarchical model. (Lambert 2018) His worked case, Casella’s drug trial with method-of-moments hyperparameters, is in the Bayesian machinery. This entry uses the other route to the same estimate: maximise the marginal likelihood directly.

One dial: the evidence procedure for ridge

Ridge regression is MAP estimation under a normal prior, w \sim \mathcal N(0, \alpha^{-1} I), where the precision \alpha is the dial. A large \alpha is a tight prior around zero (heavy shrinkage); a small \alpha is a loose one (little shrinkage). Because both the prior and the likelihood are normal, the coefficients can be integrated out exactly, and the marginal likelihood p(y \mid X, \alpha) has a closed form. The evidence procedure picks the \alpha that maximises it. (Murphy 2012)

Murphy’s demonstration is to set the two methods side by side on the same data: five-fold cross-validated test error against \log \lambda, and log evidence against \log \alpha. They choose the same value. (Murphy 2012) Panel (a) of the figure below repeats the experiment on our own simulated data.

Why should a probability of the data pick a sensible penalty? Because the marginal likelihood has the same two-part shape as the GP’s: a fit term that prefers loose priors (they let the coefficients reach the data) and a volume term that penalises them (a loose prior spreads probability over many datasets you did not see). This is the Bayesian Occam’s razor, and the evidence procedure is it applied to a single dial.

One dial per variable: ARD

Now give each coefficient its own precision:

w_j \sim \mathcal N\!\left(0,\ 1/\alpha_j\right), \qquad j = 1, \dots, D,

and choose all D precisions, plus the noise level, by maximising the marginal likelihood. Then plug them back in and report the posterior mean of the coefficients. (Murphy 2012) The method has two names. In neural networks it is automatic relevance determination (MacKay 1995; Neal 1996); for linear models Tipping (2001) called it sparse Bayesian learning. (Murphy 2012)

Read each \alpha_j as a verdict on variable j:

  • If \hat\alpha_j \approx 0, the prior on w_j has no pull, and \hat w_j is close to its unpenalised least-squares value.
  • If \hat\alpha_j \to \infty, the prior is certain that w_j = 0, so the posterior mean is zero too. The variable is “irrelevant” and its weight is pruned out. (Murphy 2012)

That is the surprise Murphy flags: every prior here is Gaussian, the shape that in ridge regression never produces exact zeros, and yet the result is (approximately) sparse. (Murphy 2012) The sparsity does not come from the shape of the prior. It comes from tuning the prior’s width separately for each variable.

Whence sparsity? A picture with two data points

Tipping’s argument, which Murphy reproduces, uses the smallest possible regression: one predictor and two observations, so x and y are both arrows in a plane. (Murphy 2012) The marginal likelihood is a two-dimensional normal density evaluated at y. Its covariance has two parts. One is round, the noise. The other is a cigar lying along the direction of x, the variation the coefficient could add; its length is 1/\alpha.

  • If x points roughly at y, stretching the cigar puts more probability where y actually is. Some finite \alpha is best.
  • If x points away from y, the cigar only spreads probability in directions where no data fell, while thinning it everywhere else. The density at y is highest when the cigar is gone, \alpha = \infty. The marginal likelihood “punishes” a coefficient for wasting probability mass on an irrelevant direction. (Murphy 2012)
NoteThe two-point example in numbers

Take y = (1, 2) and noise variance 1, and compute the log marginal likelihood as \alpha varies (our own illustration, verified numerically).

  • Irrelevant predictor, x = (2, -0.8), almost at right angles to y: log evidence is -6.25 at \alpha = 0.1, -5.19 at \alpha = 1, and climbs all the way to -4.34 as \alpha \to \infty. The best prior is “certainly zero”. The coefficient is pruned, even though least squares would have given it 0.09.
  • Relevant predictor, x = (1, 2), pointing straight at y: log evidence peaks at \hat\alpha = 1.25 (value -3.14), well above the -4.34 of pruning it. The posterior mean is 0.8, shrunk from the least-squares 1.0.

For a single predictor the optimum has a closed form, given in the appendix: the predictor survives only if its alignment with y is larger than noise alone would produce.

See it

The figure is our own simulation (numpy only). Sixty observations, twenty standardised predictors, of which four carry real effects (2, −1.5, 1 and 0.6) and sixteen are noise. Panel (a) is the one-dial evidence procedure for ridge, compared with five-fold cross-validation. Panel (b) is ARD, fitted with the fixed-point updates described below, against ordinary least squares.

Show the figure code (evidence curve, 5-fold CV, ARD fixed-point updates) 
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
N, D = 60, 20
X = rng.normal(size=(N, D))
w_true = np.zeros(D)
w_true[[1, 6, 11, 16]] = [2.0, -1.5, 1.0, 0.6]
y = X @ w_true + rng.normal(0, 1.0, N)
beta = 1.0  # noise precision, held fixed in panel (a) so the two methods are comparable

# ---------- (a) evidence vs 5-fold CV for a single ridge precision ----------
def log_evidence(alpha):
    C = np.eye(N) / beta + X @ X.T / alpha          # marginal covariance of y
    _, logdet = np.linalg.slogdet(C)
    return -0.5 * (N * np.log(2 * np.pi) + logdet + y @ np.linalg.solve(C, y))

alphas = np.logspace(-3, 3, 61)
evid = np.array([log_evidence(a) for a in alphas])

folds = np.arange(N) % 5
rng.shuffle(folds)
def cv_mse(alpha):
    err = 0.0
    for k in range(5):
        tr, te = folds != k, folds == k
        w = np.linalg.solve(X[tr].T @ X[tr] + (alpha / beta) * np.eye(D), X[tr].T @ y[tr])
        err += np.sum((y[te] - X[te] @ w) ** 2)
    return err / N
cv = np.array([cv_mse(a) for a in alphas])

# ---------- (b) ARD by the fixed-point updates (MacKay / Tipping) ----------
alpha_j = np.ones(D)
b_hat = 1.0
keep = np.ones(D, bool)
for _ in range(20000):
    Xk = X[:, keep]
    Sigma = np.linalg.inv(b_hat * Xk.T @ Xk + np.diag(alpha_j[keep]))
    mu = b_hat * Sigma @ Xk.T @ y
    gamma = 1 - alpha_j[keep] * np.diag(Sigma)       # how well-determined each weight is
    alpha_j[keep] = gamma / mu**2
    resid = y - Xk @ mu
    b_hat = (N - gamma.sum()) / (resid @ resid)
    keep &= alpha_j < 1e8                             # alpha -> infinity: prune
Xk = X[:, keep]
Sigma = np.linalg.inv(b_hat * Xk.T @ Xk + np.diag(alpha_j[keep]))
w_ard = np.zeros(D)
w_ard[keep] = b_hat * Sigma @ Xk.T @ y
w_ols = np.linalg.lstsq(X, y, rcond=None)[0]

# ---------- draw ----------
fig, (axA, axB) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(10.2, 4.3), gridspec_kw={"width_ratios": [1, 1.25]})

la = np.log10(alphas)
axA.plot(la, evid, color="#161613", lw=2)
axA.set_xlabel(r"prior precision, $\log_{10}\alpha$  (right = more shrinkage)")
axA.set_ylabel("log marginal likelihood")
axA.axvline(la[evid.argmax()], color="#e8531d", lw=1.6)
axA2 = axA.twinx()
axA2.plot(la, cv, color="#5e5e54", lw=1.4, alpha=0.9)
axA2.axvline(la[cv.argmin()], color="#5e5e54", lw=1.2, ls="--")
axA2.set_ylabel("5-fold CV error (grey)")
axA2.grid(False)
axA.set_ylim(evid.max() - 60, evid.max() + 4)
axA.text(la[evid.argmax()] + 0.12, evid.max() - 52, "evidence\npicks here", color="#e8531d", fontsize=8)
axA.set_title("(a) One dial: evidence vs cross-validation", fontsize=10, loc="left")

idx = np.arange(D)
axB.axhline(0, color="#b7b6a8", lw=0.8)
axB.vlines(idx - 0.17, 0, w_ols, color="#b7b6a8", lw=3, label="least squares")
axB.vlines(idx + 0.17, 0, w_ard, color="#e8531d", lw=3, label="ARD posterior mean")
axB.plot(idx, w_true, "o", mfc="none", mec="#161613", ms=6, label="true coefficient")
pruned = (~keep) & (w_true == 0)
axB.plot(idx[pruned] + 0.17, np.zeros(pruned.sum()), "x", color="#e8531d", ms=5)
axB.set_xticks(idx)
axB.set_xticklabels([str(i + 1) for i in idx], fontsize=7)
axB.set_xlabel("predictor (real effects: 2, 7, 12, 17)")
axB.set_ylabel("coefficient")
axB.legend(fontsize=8, frameon=False, loc="upper right")
axB.set_title(f"(b) One dial per variable: ARD prunes {pruned.sum()} of 16 noise terms (x)",
              fontsize=10, loc="left")
fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()

Two things to read off panel (b). ARD does what it promises on the real effects: all four are kept, with values close to the truth. And it is not an oracle: five noise variables survive with small coefficients (the largest about 0.24), because with sixty observations their chance alignment with y was large enough to pass the test in the two-point box above. What ARD reports is the best guess at the coefficients given its chosen precisions. It does not report how sure it is about which variables belong. That limitation is the first pitfall below.

How ARD is fitted

The marginal likelihood cannot be maximised in one step, because each \alpha_j depends on the current posterior for w, and the posterior depends on all the \alphas. Murphy describes three ways to iterate. (Murphy 2012)

  • EM. Treat w as missing data. The E step computes its normal posterior given the current precisions; the M step sets each \alpha_j to one over the expected square of w_j. It is the simplest route and links ARD to the EM algorithm. (Murphy 2012)
  • Fixed-point updates (used in the figure). Faster and more direct. They introduce a quantity \gamma_j between 0 and 1 that measures how well-determined weight j is by the data rather than by the prior (MacKay 1992). Their sum is the model’s effective degrees of freedom, the same idea as the effective number of parameters of a smoothing spline. (Murphy 2012) In the figure the nine surviving weights have \gamma_j between 0.37 and 1.00.
  • Iteratively reweighted \ell_1. Treat ARD as a sequence of weighted lasso problems, re-setting each variable’s penalty from all the current weights at every step (Wipf & Nagarajan 2010). Unlike the adaptive lasso, which raises a weight’s penalty simply because the weight is small, this scheme raises it only when doing so makes the whole solution sparser. (Murphy 2012)

Two warnings come with all three. The objective is not convex, so the answer can depend on where you start. And for logistic regression the E step cannot be done exactly; a Laplace approximation makes EM usable again but is not guaranteed to converge, while the reweighted-\ell_1 route needs no approximation. (Murphy 2012)

What ARD is, seen as a penalty

ARD looks unlike the lasso: it integrates out the coefficients and optimises the dials, where MAP estimation does the opposite. Wipf and Nagarajan (2007) showed that it is nonetheless a MAP estimate, under an unusual penalty. Murphy reports the result and its consequences. (Murphy 2012)

  • The penalty couples the variables. Lasso and ridge penalise each coefficient separately (a factorial prior). ARD’s implied penalty does not. Because the weights are correlated in the posterior (“explaining away”), estimating one \alpha_j borrows information from every feature. The implied prior even depends on the data. (Murphy 2012)
  • It keeps the right target with fewer traps. The ideal selection criterion, \ell_0 (count the non-zero coefficients), has the right global optimum but is full of local optima. The lasso’s \ell_1 has no local optima but a different global optimum. The ARD objective has far fewer local optima than \ell_0 while keeping \ell_0’s global optimum. (Murphy 2012)
  • It is a continuous spike-and-slab. The ARD marginal likelihood has the same form as the spike-and-slab model’s, with the 0/1 inclusion indicators replaced by continuous precisions. Maximising it is a tractable stand-in for finding the most probable set of included variables. (Murphy 2012)

The same trick elsewhere

Once you see “one precision per thing, tuned by the evidence”, it turns up across machine learning.

  • Relevance vector machines. Put a kernel basis function on every training case, so there are as many weights as observations, and let ARD prune them. Murphy compares this RVM with \ell_1- and \ell_2-penalised kernel machines and an SVM on the same problems. The four predict about equally well; the RVM uses the fewest basis functions, so it is fastest to apply, and it is also fastest to train, because the SVM’s penalty must be cross-validated while the RVM’s hyperparameters come from empirical Bayes. Murphy calls this result fairly typical. (Murphy 2012)
  • The ARD kernel in Gaussian processes. Give the squared-exponential kernel a separate length-scale for each input dimension. A dimension whose length-scale goes to infinity is ignored. Fitting the length-scales by the GP’s marginal likelihood is ARD for Gaussian processes. (Murphy 2012)
  • Neural networks. Use one precision for all the weights leaving each input, and one for all the weights leaving each hidden unit. An input whose precision goes to infinity is pruned out, as is a hidden unit; grouping the weights this way gives an effect like the group lasso. (Murphy 2012) See deep learning.
  • How many latent dimensions? In factor analysis and mixtures of factor analysers, set the number of latent dimensions to its maximum and let ARD, combined with EM, prune the unneeded loadings (Bishop 1999; Ghahramani & Beal 2000). Murphy shows Beal’s result: with little data the method prefers simple models, and as the sample grows it discovers more clusters and more dimensions, settling on the true six subspaces of dimension 1, 2, 2, 3, 4 and 7. (Murphy 2012) That is the Occam’s razor at work, and a reminder that what ARD prunes depends on N. See factor analysis and model selection for latent-variable models.
  • Variational Bayes. Instead of point-estimating the precisions, approximate their posterior. Murphy derives a variational algorithm for the ARD model; see variational inference. (Murphy 2012)

Common pitfalls

WarningWhere this goes wrong

  • Reading the sparse result as uncertainty about which variables matter. Sparse Bayesian learning is Bayesian about the values of the coefficients, given the chosen precisions. It does not express uncertainty about the set of relevant variables, which is usually the more interesting question. (Murphy 2012) For that, resample (stability selection) or use a full hierarchical prior.

  • Treating “pruned” as “proven irrelevant”, or “kept” as “proven relevant”. In the figure five noise variables survived with sixty observations. With little data the evidence prefers simpler models and prunes more; with more data it keeps more. (Murphy 2012) A pruning list is an estimate, not a test.

  • Calling empirical Bayes fully Bayesian. The hyperparameters are point estimates chosen with the same data, and their uncertainty is ignored. Murphy treats this as an approximation to a hierarchical model; Lambert prefers to fit that hierarchical model outright. (Murphy 2012; Lambert 2018)

  • Trusting one run of a non-convex fit. The ARD objective is not convex, so results can depend on the starting values. Try several starts. (Murphy 2012)

  • Assuming the logistic version converges like the linear one. With a binary outcome the E step is approximate, and EM with a Laplace approximation is not guaranteed to converge. (Murphy 2012)

  • Tuning many dials by cross-validation. It is the wrong tool once there is one hyperparameter per variable; the grid explodes. That is the case the evidence procedure was made for. (Murphy 2012)

  • Reading ARD’s coefficients causally. ARD is a prediction and compression device. A variable it prunes can still be a confounder you must adjust for; see the double lasso for selection that respects that.

Appendix · the mathematics

Empirical Bayes in general. With data \mathcal D, parameters \theta and hyperparameters \eta, \hat\eta_{\text{EB}} = \arg\max_\eta\, p(\mathcal D\mid\eta) = \arg\max_\eta \int p(\mathcal D\mid\theta)\,p(\theta\mid\eta)\,d\theta , and inference then proceeds with p(\theta\mid\mathcal D,\hat\eta). MAP-II adds a hyperprior, \arg\max_\eta p(\mathcal D\mid\eta)\,p(\eta). (Murphy 2012)

The ARD model and its evidence. Let p(y\mid X,w,\beta)=\mathcal N(y\mid Xw,\beta^{-1}I_N) and p(w)=\mathcal N(w\mid 0, A^{-1}) with A=\operatorname{diag}(\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_D). Integrating w out, p(y\mid X,\alpha,\beta)=\mathcal N(y\mid 0, C_\alpha),\qquad C_\alpha=\beta^{-1}I_N+XA^{-1}X^\top , and the objective to minimise is \ell(\alpha,\beta)=\log|C_\alpha|+y^\top C_\alpha^{-1}y . The first term is the volume (Occam) penalty; the second is the fit. Ridge’s evidence procedure is the special case \alpha_j=\alpha for all j. (Murphy 2012)

The posterior given the precisions. p(w\mid\mathcal D,\hat\alpha,\hat\beta)=\mathcal N(\mu,\Sigma),\qquad \Sigma^{-1}=\hat\beta X^\top X+A,\qquad \mu=\hat\beta\,\Sigma X^\top y . (Murphy 2012)

EM. The E step computes \mu,\Sigma; the M step (flat hyperpriors) sets \alpha_j = 1/\mathbb E[w_j^2] = 1/(\mu_j^2+\Sigma_{jj}), and with a single shared \alpha, \alpha = D/(\mu^\top\mu+\operatorname{tr}\Sigma). The noise update is \beta^{-1}=\big(\lVert y-X\mu\rVert^2+\beta^{-1}\sum_j(1-\alpha_j\Sigma_{jj})\big)/N. (Murphy 2012)

Fixed point. Setting the derivatives of \ell to zero gives \gamma_j = 1-\alpha_j\Sigma_{jj},\qquad \alpha_j \leftarrow \frac{\gamma_j}{\mu_j^2},\qquad \beta^{-1}\leftarrow \frac{\lVert y-X\mu\rVert^2}{N-\sum_j\gamma_j}, iterated with \mu,\Sigma to convergence. \gamma_j\in[0,1] is near 1 when the data pin w_j down and near 0 when the prior does; \sum_j\gamma_j is the effective number of parameters. These are the updates in the figure’s code. (Murphy 2012)

A single predictor in closed form. With one column x, write s=\beta\,x^\top x (how much information the predictor carries) and q=\beta\,x^\top y (how strongly it lines up with y). Using the matrix determinant lemma and Sherman–Morrison on C_\alpha=\beta^{-1}I+\alpha^{-1}xx^\top, the \alpha-dependent part of the log evidence is \tfrac12\big[\log\alpha-\log(\alpha+s)+q^2/(\alpha+s)\big], whose maximiser is \hat\alpha=\begin{cases}\dfrac{s^2}{q^2-s}, & q^2>s,\\[6pt] \infty, & q^2\le s.\end{cases} The predictor is pruned exactly when q^2\le s: when its alignment with y is no larger than noise alone would produce. In the two-point example (\beta=1), the relevant x=(1,2) has s=5, q=5, so \hat\alpha=25/20=1.25 and \mu=q/(s+\hat\alpha)=0.8; the irrelevant x=(2,-0.8) has s=4.64, q=0.4, so q^2<s and \hat\alpha=\infty. (Our derivation, checked against a numerical maximisation on random problems; with several predictors the same test applies to each one given the others, which is the basis of Tipping and Faul’s greedy algorithm that Murphy mentions.)

ARD as MAP. Wipf and Nagarajan (2007) show that ARD’s posterior mean solves \hat w_{\text{ARD}}=\arg\min_w\ \beta\lVert y-Xw\rVert_2^2+g_{\text{ARD}}(w),\qquad g_{\text{ARD}}(w)=\min_{\alpha\ge0}\ \sum_j\alpha_j w_j^2+\log|C_\alpha| , a non-factorial penalty that is concave and non-decreasing in each |w_j|, which is why it can be minimised by iteratively reweighted \ell_1. (Murphy 2012)

A penalty is a prior, and every prior has a strength. Instead of tuning that strength by
cross-validation, you can pick the value under which the observed data were **most probable**, with the
coefficients averaged out. Do this once and you have a ridge regression that tunes itself. Give **every
variable its own strength** and something surprising happens: the variables that do not help predict

## Why it matters

Every regularised model in this encyclopedia has a dial. [Ridge and the lasso](../04-predictive/regularization.qmd)
have $\lambda$; a [Gaussian process](gaussian-processes.qmd) has a length-scale; a
[hierarchical model](../02-inference/hierarchical-bayesian-models.qmd) has a between-group variance. The
usual way to set a dial is [cross-validation](../04-predictive/cross-validation.qmd): try a grid of
values and keep the one that predicts held-out data best. That works for one dial, or two. It does not
work for twenty. Murphy makes the point directly: you cannot use cross-validation to tune $D$ separate
hyperparameters, one per feature. [@murphy_ml]

This entry covers the alternative. **Empirical Bayes**, also called **type-II maximum likelihood** and,
in machine learning, the **evidence procedure**, chooses the dial by maximising the **marginal
likelihood**: the probability of the data with the coefficients integrated out. [@murphy_ml] It scales
to one dial per variable, and in that form, called **automatic relevance determination (ARD)** or
**sparse Bayesian learning (SBL)**, it becomes a Bayesian method for deciding which variables matter.
It sits next to [selection stability](../04-predictive/selection-stability.qmd), which asks the same
question by resampling, and it reappears inside Gaussian processes, neural networks and factor models.

## How Bayesian do you want to be? {#sec-ladder}

Empirical Bayes is easiest to place on a ladder. Write the model in two levels: the data depend on
parameters $\theta$ (the regression coefficients), and the parameters have a prior whose strength is set
by a **hyperparameter** $\eta$ (a precision, a variance, a $\lambda$). Murphy lists five ways to handle
the two levels, ordered by how many of them you integrate over: the more integrals, the "more Bayesian"
the method. [@murphy_ml]

| Method | What it does |
|---|---|
| Maximum likelihood | Pick $\theta$ to fit the data. No prior at all. |
| MAP estimation | Pick $\theta$ to fit the data *and* the prior, with $\eta$ fixed in advance. The lasso and ridge live here. |
| **ML-II (empirical Bayes)** | **Integrate $\theta$ out**, then pick $\eta$ to maximise the probability of the data. |
| MAP-II | As ML-II, but with a prior on $\eta$ too. |
| Full Bayes | Integrate over both $\theta$ and $\eta$; report the joint posterior. |

The step from MAP to ML-II is the one that matters here. Under MAP, someone has to choose $\eta$.
Under ML-II, the data choose it, through the marginal likelihood. Murphy's argument for why this is safe
is that $\eta$ is usually far lower-dimensional than $\theta$, so it is much less prone to overfitting.
[@murphy_ml]

Two sources disagree about how to feel about this. **Murphy** concedes that empirical Bayes breaks the
rule that a prior should be chosen without looking at the data, but treats it as a cheap approximation
to a full hierarchical model, much as MAP approximates a full posterior. He adds that it has good
frequentist properties, which is why non-Bayesians use it: the James–Stein estimator can be derived this
way. [@murphy_ml] **Lambert** is less forgiving. He calls empirical Bayes a "half-Bayesian/half-Frequentist"
compromise that uses the data to set the priors, finds it convoluted, and prefers to put priors on the
hyperparameters and fit the full [hierarchical model](../02-inference/hierarchical-bayesian-models.qmd).
[@lambert_bayes] His worked case, Casella's drug trial with method-of-moments hyperparameters, is in
[the Bayesian machinery](../02-inference/bayesian-machinery.qmd). This entry uses the other route to the
same estimate: maximise the marginal likelihood directly.

## One dial: the evidence procedure for ridge {#sec-evidence}

Ridge regression is MAP estimation under a normal prior, $w \sim \mathcal N(0, \alpha^{-1} I)$, where the
**precision** $\alpha$ is the dial. A large $\alpha$ is a tight prior around zero (heavy shrinkage); a
small $\alpha$ is a loose one (little shrinkage). Because both the prior and the likelihood are normal,
the coefficients can be integrated out exactly, and the marginal likelihood $p(y \mid X, \alpha)$ has a
closed form. The evidence procedure picks the $\alpha$ that maximises it. [@murphy_ml]

Murphy's demonstration is to set the two methods side by side on the same data: five-fold
cross-validated test error against $\log \lambda$, and log evidence against $\log \alpha$. They choose
the same value. [@murphy_ml] Panel (a) of the figure below repeats the experiment on our own simulated
data.

Why should a probability of the data pick a sensible penalty? Because the marginal likelihood has the
same two-part shape as the [GP's](gaussian-processes.qmd#learning-the-hyperparameters): a **fit** term
that prefers loose priors (they let the coefficients reach the data) and a **volume** term that
penalises them (a loose prior spreads probability over many datasets you did not see). This is the
[Bayesian Occam's razor](../02-inference/bayesian-model-comparison.qmd), and the evidence procedure is
it applied to a single dial.

## One dial per variable: ARD {#sec-ard}

Now give each coefficient its own precision:

$$
w_j \sim \mathcal N\!\left(0,\ 1/\alpha_j\right), \qquad j = 1, \dots, D,
$$

and choose all $D$ precisions, plus the noise level, by maximising the marginal likelihood. Then plug
them back in and report the posterior mean of the coefficients. [@murphy_ml] The method has two names.
In neural networks it is **automatic relevance determination** (MacKay 1995; Neal 1996); for linear
models Tipping (2001) called it **sparse Bayesian learning**. [@murphy_ml]

Read each $\alpha_j$ as a verdict on variable $j$:

- If $\hat\alpha_j \approx 0$, the prior on $w_j$ has no pull, and $\hat w_j$ is close to its
  unpenalised least-squares value.
- If $\hat\alpha_j \to \infty$, the prior is certain that $w_j = 0$, so the posterior mean is zero
  too. The variable is "irrelevant" and its weight is **pruned out**. [@murphy_ml]

That is the surprise Murphy flags: every prior here is Gaussian, the shape that in ridge regression
*never* produces exact zeros, and yet the result is (approximately) **sparse**. [@murphy_ml] The
sparsity does not come from the shape of the prior. It comes from tuning the prior's width separately for
each variable.

### Whence sparsity? A picture with two data points {#sec-whence}

Tipping's argument, which Murphy reproduces, uses the smallest possible regression: one predictor and
two observations, so $x$ and $y$ are both arrows in a plane. [@murphy_ml] The marginal likelihood is a
two-dimensional normal density evaluated at $y$. Its covariance has two parts. One is round, the noise.
The other is a cigar lying along the direction of $x$, the variation the coefficient could add; its
length is $1/\alpha$.

- If **$x$ points roughly at $y$**, stretching the cigar puts more probability where $y$ actually is.
  Some finite $\alpha$ is best.
- If **$x$ points away from $y$**, the cigar only spreads probability in directions where no data
  fell, while thinning it everywhere else. The density at $y$ is highest when the cigar is gone,
  $\alpha = \infty$. The marginal likelihood "punishes" a coefficient for wasting probability mass on
  an irrelevant direction. [@murphy_ml]

::: {.callout-note title="The two-point example in numbers"}
Take $y = (1, 2)$ and noise variance 1, and compute the log marginal likelihood as $\alpha$ varies (our
own illustration, verified numerically).

- **Irrelevant predictor**, $x = (2, -0.8)$, almost at right angles to $y$: log evidence is
  $-6.25$ at $\alpha = 0.1$, $-5.19$ at $\alpha = 1$, and climbs all the way to $-4.34$ as
  $\alpha \to \infty$. The best prior is "certainly zero". The coefficient is pruned, even though least
  squares would have given it 0.09.
- **Relevant predictor**, $x = (1, 2)$, pointing straight at $y$: log evidence peaks at
  $\hat\alpha = 1.25$ (value $-3.14$), well above the $-4.34$ of pruning it. The posterior mean is 0.8,
  shrunk from the least-squares 1.0.

For a single predictor the optimum has a closed form, given in the appendix: the predictor survives
only if its alignment with $y$ is larger than noise alone would produce.
:::

### See it

The figure is our own simulation (numpy only). Sixty observations, twenty standardised predictors, of
which four carry real effects (2, −1.5, 1 and 0.6) and sixteen are noise. Panel (a) is the one-dial
evidence procedure for ridge, compared with five-fold cross-validation. Panel (b) is ARD, fitted with
the fixed-point updates described [below](#sec-algorithms), against ordinary least squares.

```{python}
#| label: fig-ard
#| fig-cap: "Empirical Bayes in two forms, on one simulated dataset (N = 60, 20 predictors, 4 real effects). (a) One dial: the log marginal likelihood of ridge regression as the prior precision α varies (ink) and the five-fold cross-validated error (grey, right axis). The evidence peaks (orange line) close to where cross-validation bottoms out (dashed grey), without holding any data out. (b) One dial per variable: ARD's posterior means (orange) against least squares (grey) and the truth (open circles). ARD keeps all four real effects, sets 11 of the 16 noise coefficients exactly to zero, and leaves 5 small ones; least squares sets none to zero."
#| code-fold: true
#| code-summary: "Show the figure code (evidence curve, 5-fold CV, ARD fixed-point updates)"

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
N, D = 60, 20
X = rng.normal(size=(N, D))
w_true = np.zeros(D)
w_true[[1, 6, 11, 16]] = [2.0, -1.5, 1.0, 0.6]
y = X @ w_true + rng.normal(0, 1.0, N)
beta = 1.0  # noise precision, held fixed in panel (a) so the two methods are comparable

# ---------- (a) evidence vs 5-fold CV for a single ridge precision ----------
def log_evidence(alpha):
    C = np.eye(N) / beta + X @ X.T / alpha          # marginal covariance of y
    _, logdet = np.linalg.slogdet(C)
    return -0.5 * (N * np.log(2 * np.pi) + logdet + y @ np.linalg.solve(C, y))

alphas = np.logspace(-3, 3, 61)
evid = np.array([log_evidence(a) for a in alphas])

folds = np.arange(N) % 5
rng.shuffle(folds)
def cv_mse(alpha):
    err = 0.0
    for k in range(5):
        tr, te = folds != k, folds == k
        w = np.linalg.solve(X[tr].T @ X[tr] + (alpha / beta) * np.eye(D), X[tr].T @ y[tr])
        err += np.sum((y[te] - X[te] @ w) ** 2)
    return err / N
cv = np.array([cv_mse(a) for a in alphas])

# ---------- (b) ARD by the fixed-point updates (MacKay / Tipping) ----------
alpha_j = np.ones(D)
b_hat = 1.0
keep = np.ones(D, bool)
for _ in range(20000):
    Xk = X[:, keep]
    Sigma = np.linalg.inv(b_hat * Xk.T @ Xk + np.diag(alpha_j[keep]))
    mu = b_hat * Sigma @ Xk.T @ y
    gamma = 1 - alpha_j[keep] * np.diag(Sigma)       # how well-determined each weight is
    alpha_j[keep] = gamma / mu**2
    resid = y - Xk @ mu
    b_hat = (N - gamma.sum()) / (resid @ resid)
    keep &= alpha_j < 1e8                             # alpha -> infinity: prune
Xk = X[:, keep]
Sigma = np.linalg.inv(b_hat * Xk.T @ Xk + np.diag(alpha_j[keep]))
w_ard = np.zeros(D)
w_ard[keep] = b_hat * Sigma @ Xk.T @ y
w_ols = np.linalg.lstsq(X, y, rcond=None)[0]

# ---------- draw ----------
fig, (axA, axB) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(10.2, 4.3), gridspec_kw={"width_ratios": [1, 1.25]})

la = np.log10(alphas)
axA.plot(la, evid, color="#161613", lw=2)
axA.set_xlabel(r"prior precision, $\log_{10}\alpha$  (right = more shrinkage)")
axA.set_ylabel("log marginal likelihood")
axA.axvline(la[evid.argmax()], color="#e8531d", lw=1.6)
axA2 = axA.twinx()
axA2.plot(la, cv, color="#5e5e54", lw=1.4, alpha=0.9)
axA2.axvline(la[cv.argmin()], color="#5e5e54", lw=1.2, ls="--")
axA2.set_ylabel("5-fold CV error (grey)")
axA2.grid(False)
axA.set_ylim(evid.max() - 60, evid.max() + 4)
axA.text(la[evid.argmax()] + 0.12, evid.max() - 52, "evidence\npicks here", color="#e8531d", fontsize=8)
axA.set_title("(a) One dial: evidence vs cross-validation", fontsize=10, loc="left")

idx = np.arange(D)
axB.axhline(0, color="#b7b6a8", lw=0.8)
axB.vlines(idx - 0.17, 0, w_ols, color="#b7b6a8", lw=3, label="least squares")
axB.vlines(idx + 0.17, 0, w_ard, color="#e8531d", lw=3, label="ARD posterior mean")
axB.plot(idx, w_true, "o", mfc="none", mec="#161613", ms=6, label="true coefficient")
pruned = (~keep) & (w_true == 0)
axB.plot(idx[pruned] + 0.17, np.zeros(pruned.sum()), "x", color="#e8531d", ms=5)
axB.set_xticks(idx)
axB.set_xticklabels([str(i + 1) for i in idx], fontsize=7)
axB.set_xlabel("predictor (real effects: 2, 7, 12, 17)")
axB.set_ylabel("coefficient")
axB.legend(fontsize=8, frameon=False, loc="upper right")
axB.set_title(f"(b) One dial per variable: ARD prunes {pruned.sum()} of 16 noise terms (x)",
              fontsize=10, loc="left")
fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

Two things to read off panel (b). ARD does what it promises on the real effects: all four are kept,
with values close to the truth. And it is **not an oracle**: five noise variables survive with small
coefficients (the largest about 0.24), because with sixty observations their chance alignment with $y$
was large enough to pass the test in the two-point box above. What ARD reports is the best guess at the
coefficients **given** its chosen precisions. It does not report how sure it is about *which* variables
belong. That limitation is the first pitfall below.

## How ARD is fitted {#sec-algorithms}

The marginal likelihood cannot be maximised in one step, because each $\alpha_j$ depends on the current
posterior for $w$, and the posterior depends on all the $\alpha$s. Murphy describes three ways to iterate.
[@murphy_ml]

- **EM.** Treat $w$ as missing data. The E step computes its normal posterior given the current
  precisions; the M step sets each $\alpha_j$ to one over the expected square of $w_j$. It is the
  simplest route and links ARD to the [EM algorithm](mixture-models-and-em.qmd). [@murphy_ml]
- **Fixed-point updates** (used in the figure). Faster and more direct. They introduce a quantity
  $\gamma_j$ between 0 and 1 that measures how **well-determined** weight $j$ is by the data rather than by
  the prior (MacKay 1992). Their sum is the model's **effective degrees of freedom**, the same idea as the
  effective number of parameters of a [smoothing spline](beyond-linearity-splines-and-gams.qmd).
  [@murphy_ml] In the figure the nine surviving weights have $\gamma_j$ between 0.37 and 1.00.
- **Iteratively reweighted $\ell_1$.** Treat ARD as a sequence of weighted lasso problems, re-setting
  each variable's penalty from **all** the current weights at every step (Wipf & Nagarajan 2010).
  Unlike the [adaptive lasso](../04-predictive/prediction-with-latent-variables.qmd#sec-priors), which
  raises a weight's penalty simply because the weight is small, this scheme raises it only when doing so
  makes the whole solution sparser. [@murphy_ml]

Two warnings come with all three. The objective is **not convex**, so the answer can depend on where you
start. And for **logistic regression** the E step cannot be done exactly; a Laplace approximation makes
EM usable again but is not guaranteed to converge, while the reweighted-$\ell_1$ route needs no
approximation. [@murphy_ml]

## What ARD is, seen as a penalty {#sec-map}

ARD looks unlike the lasso: it integrates out the coefficients and optimises the dials, where MAP
estimation does the opposite. Wipf and Nagarajan (2007) showed that it is nonetheless a MAP estimate, under
an unusual penalty. Murphy reports the result and its consequences. [@murphy_ml]

- **The penalty couples the variables.** Lasso and ridge penalise each coefficient separately (a
  *factorial* prior). ARD's implied penalty does not. Because the weights are correlated in the
  posterior ("explaining away"), estimating one $\alpha_j$ borrows information from every feature. The
  implied prior even depends on the data. [@murphy_ml]
- **It keeps the right target with fewer traps.** The ideal selection criterion, $\ell_0$ (count the
  non-zero coefficients), has the right global optimum but is full of local optima. The lasso's $\ell_1$
  has no local optima but a different global optimum. The ARD objective has far fewer local optima than
  $\ell_0$ while keeping $\ell_0$'s global optimum. [@murphy_ml]
- **It is a continuous spike-and-slab.** The ARD marginal likelihood has the same form as the
  spike-and-slab model's, with the 0/1 inclusion indicators replaced by continuous precisions. Maximising
  it is a tractable stand-in for finding the most probable set of included variables. [@murphy_ml]

## The same trick elsewhere {#sec-elsewhere}

Once you see "one precision per thing, tuned by the evidence", it turns up across machine learning.

- **Relevance vector machines.** Put a kernel basis function on every training case, so there are as
  many weights as observations, and let ARD prune them. Murphy compares this RVM with $\ell_1$- and
  $\ell_2$-penalised kernel machines and an [SVM](support-vector-machines.qmd) on the same problems. The
  four predict about equally well; the RVM uses the fewest basis functions, so it is fastest to apply,
  and it is also fastest to train, because the SVM's penalty must be cross-validated while the RVM's
  hyperparameters come from empirical Bayes. Murphy calls this result fairly typical. [@murphy_ml]
- **The ARD kernel in Gaussian processes.** Give the squared-exponential kernel a separate length-scale
  for each input dimension. A dimension whose length-scale goes to infinity is ignored. Fitting the
  length-scales by the GP's marginal likelihood is ARD for
  [Gaussian processes](gaussian-processes.qmd). [@murphy_ml]
- **Neural networks.** Use one precision for all the weights leaving each input, and one for all the
  weights leaving each hidden unit. An input whose precision goes to infinity is pruned out, as is a
  hidden unit; grouping the weights this way gives an effect like the
  [group lasso](../04-predictive/selection-stability.qmd#sec-groups). [@murphy_ml] See
  [deep learning](deep-learning-neural-networks.qmd).
- **How many latent dimensions?** In factor analysis and mixtures of factor analysers, set the number of
  latent dimensions to its maximum and let ARD, combined with EM, prune the unneeded loadings (Bishop
  1999; Ghahramani & Beal 2000). Murphy shows Beal's result: with little data the method prefers simple
  models, and as the sample grows it discovers more clusters and more dimensions, settling on the true
  six subspaces of dimension 1, 2, 2, 3, 4 and 7. [@murphy_ml] That is the Occam's razor at work, and
  a reminder that what ARD prunes depends on $N$. See [factor analysis](../03-regression/factor-analysis.qmd)
  and [model selection for latent-variable models](latent-classes-and-sem-trees.qmd).
- **Variational Bayes.** Instead of point-estimating the precisions, approximate their posterior. Murphy
  derives a variational algorithm for the ARD model; see [variational inference](variational-inference.qmd).
  [@murphy_ml]

## Common pitfalls

::: {.callout-warning title="Where this goes wrong"}

- **Reading the sparse result as uncertainty about which variables matter.** Sparse Bayesian learning is
  Bayesian about the *values* of the coefficients, given the chosen precisions. It does not express
  uncertainty about the *set* of relevant variables, which is usually the more interesting question.
  [@murphy_ml] For that, resample ([stability selection](../04-predictive/selection-stability.qmd#sec-stability))
  or use a full hierarchical prior.

- **Treating "pruned" as "proven irrelevant", or "kept" as "proven relevant".** In the figure five
  noise variables survived with sixty observations. With little data the evidence prefers simpler models
  and prunes more; with more data it keeps more. [@murphy_ml] A pruning list is an estimate, not a test.

- **Calling empirical Bayes fully Bayesian.** The hyperparameters are point estimates chosen with the
  same data, and their uncertainty is ignored. Murphy treats this as an approximation to a hierarchical
  model; Lambert prefers to fit that hierarchical model outright. [@murphy_ml; @lambert_bayes]

- **Trusting one run of a non-convex fit.** The ARD objective is not convex, so results can depend on
  the starting values. Try several starts. [@murphy_ml]

- **Assuming the logistic version converges like the linear one.** With a binary outcome the E step is
  approximate, and EM with a Laplace approximation is not guaranteed to converge. [@murphy_ml]

- **Tuning many dials by cross-validation.** It is the wrong tool once there is one hyperparameter per
  variable; the grid explodes. That is the case the evidence procedure was made for. [@murphy_ml]

- **Reading ARD's coefficients causally.** ARD is a prediction and compression device. A variable it
  prunes can still be a confounder you must adjust for; see [the double lasso](../04-predictive/double-lasso.qmd)
  for selection that respects that.
:::

## Appendix · the mathematics {.unnumbered}

::: {.math-appendix}

**Empirical Bayes in general.** With data $\mathcal D$, parameters $\theta$ and hyperparameters $\eta$,
$$
\hat\eta_{\text{EB}} = \arg\max_\eta\, p(\mathcal D\mid\eta) = \arg\max_\eta \int p(\mathcal D\mid\theta)\,p(\theta\mid\eta)\,d\theta ,
$$
and inference then proceeds with $p(\theta\mid\mathcal D,\hat\eta)$. MAP-II adds a hyperprior,
$\arg\max_\eta p(\mathcal D\mid\eta)\,p(\eta)$. [@murphy_ml]

**The ARD model and its evidence.** Let $p(y\mid X,w,\beta)=\mathcal N(y\mid Xw,\beta^{-1}I_N)$ and
$p(w)=\mathcal N(w\mid 0, A^{-1})$ with $A=\operatorname{diag}(\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_D)$. Integrating $w$ out,
$$
p(y\mid X,\alpha,\beta)=\mathcal N(y\mid 0, C_\alpha),\qquad C_\alpha=\beta^{-1}I_N+XA^{-1}X^\top ,
$$
and the objective to minimise is
$$
\ell(\alpha,\beta)=\log|C_\alpha|+y^\top C_\alpha^{-1}y .
$$
The first term is the volume (Occam) penalty; the second is the fit. Ridge's evidence procedure is the
special case $\alpha_j=\alpha$ for all $j$. [@murphy_ml]

**The posterior given the precisions.**
$$
p(w\mid\mathcal D,\hat\alpha,\hat\beta)=\mathcal N(\mu,\Sigma),\qquad
\Sigma^{-1}=\hat\beta X^\top X+A,\qquad \mu=\hat\beta\,\Sigma X^\top y .
$$
[@murphy_ml]

**EM.** The E step computes $\mu,\Sigma$; the M step (flat hyperpriors) sets
$\alpha_j = 1/\mathbb E[w_j^2] = 1/(\mu_j^2+\Sigma_{jj})$, and with a single shared $\alpha$,
$\alpha = D/(\mu^\top\mu+\operatorname{tr}\Sigma)$. The noise update is
$\beta^{-1}=\big(\lVert y-X\mu\rVert^2+\beta^{-1}\sum_j(1-\alpha_j\Sigma_{jj})\big)/N$. [@murphy_ml]

**Fixed point.** Setting the derivatives of $\ell$ to zero gives
$$
\gamma_j = 1-\alpha_j\Sigma_{jj},\qquad
\alpha_j \leftarrow \frac{\gamma_j}{\mu_j^2},\qquad
\beta^{-1}\leftarrow \frac{\lVert y-X\mu\rVert^2}{N-\sum_j\gamma_j},
$$
iterated with $\mu,\Sigma$ to convergence. $\gamma_j\in[0,1]$ is near 1 when the data pin $w_j$ down and
near 0 when the prior does; $\sum_j\gamma_j$ is the effective number of parameters. These are the updates
in the figure's code. [@murphy_ml]

**A single predictor in closed form.** With one column $x$, write $s=\beta\,x^\top x$ (how much
information the predictor carries) and $q=\beta\,x^\top y$ (how strongly it lines up with $y$). Using
the matrix determinant lemma and Sherman–Morrison on $C_\alpha=\beta^{-1}I+\alpha^{-1}xx^\top$, the
$\alpha$-dependent part of the log evidence is
$\tfrac12\big[\log\alpha-\log(\alpha+s)+q^2/(\alpha+s)\big]$, whose maximiser is
$$
\hat\alpha=\begin{cases}\dfrac{s^2}{q^2-s}, & q^2>s,\\[6pt] \infty, & q^2\le s.\end{cases}
$$
The predictor is pruned exactly when $q^2\le s$: when its alignment with $y$ is no larger than noise
alone would produce. In the two-point example ($\beta=1$), the relevant $x=(1,2)$ has $s=5$, $q=5$, so
$\hat\alpha=25/20=1.25$ and $\mu=q/(s+\hat\alpha)=0.8$; the irrelevant $x=(2,-0.8)$ has $s=4.64$,
$q=0.4$, so $q^2<s$ and $\hat\alpha=\infty$. (Our derivation, checked against a numerical maximisation
on random problems; with several predictors the same test applies to each one given the others, which
is the basis of Tipping and Faul's greedy algorithm that Murphy mentions.)

**ARD as MAP.** Wipf and Nagarajan (2007) show that ARD's posterior mean solves
$$
\hat w_{\text{ARD}}=\arg\min_w\ \beta\lVert y-Xw\rVert_2^2+g_{\text{ARD}}(w),\qquad
g_{\text{ARD}}(w)=\min_{\alpha\ge0}\ \sum_j\alpha_j w_j^2+\log|C_\alpha| ,
$$
a non-factorial penalty that is concave and non-decreasing in each $|w_j|$, which is why it can be
minimised by iteratively reweighted $\ell_1$. [@murphy_ml]

:::
