Variational Inference

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source

When the exact posterior is out of reach, pick a simpler distribution and slide it as close to the posterior as you can. Inference becomes optimisation: faster than sampling, more honest than a point estimate, and predictably overconfident.

Variational inference (VI) replaces an intractable posterior with the closest member of a tractable family, where “closest” is measured by the Kullback–Leibler divergence. You cannot compute that distance directly, but you can maximise a lower bound on the log evidence that is equivalent to it. The most common version, mean field, pretends the unknowns are independent and updates each one against the average of the others, which is fast but tends to understate uncertainty.

Why it matters

Bayesian inference ends in a posterior p(x\mid\mathcal D), and for most interesting models nobody can compute it exactly. Murphy lists the walls you hit: exact algorithms for discrete graphical models take time exponential in the graph’s treewidth; Gaussian models are cubic, still too slow with many variables; and neither handles general continuous or mixed models (Murphy 2012). The usual way round is MCMC, which is exact in the limit but can be slow, and is hard to judge for convergence. Lambert points out the catch: to know whether a sampler has reached the posterior you would need the posterior (Lambert 2018).

Variational inference takes a different deal. Choose a family of simple distributions q, such as independent Gaussians or a product of categorical distributions, and search that family for the member nearest the true posterior. Inference becomes an optimisation problem, and Murphy’s summary is that VI “often gives us the speed benefits of MAP estimation but the statistical benefits of the Bayesian approach” (Murphy 2012). You get a whole distribution, not a point, at a cost close to fitting a point.

For a social scientist the most visible use is text. The topic models behind most computational text analysis were introduced by Blei, Ng and Jordan with a variational EM algorithm (Jacobucci et al. 2023). In network analysis, the unknown block memberships of a stochastic block model (which students fall into which friendship groups) can likewise be assigned by variational EM (Jacobucci et al. 2023). Large hierarchical models, mixture models with many components and models with one latent variable per respondent are where VI earns its place.

The yardstick: KL divergence

To say “nearest” you need a distance between distributions. The standard choice is the Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence. It is zero when the two distributions are identical, positive otherwise, and not symmetric: the divergence of q from p is not the divergence of p from q (Lambert 2018).

Lambert gives two pictures of what it measures (Lambert 2018):

  • Secret codes. Morse code was tuned to English: the most common letter, e, gets the shortest symbol, a rare letter like q a long one. Use the English code to send Spanish and your messages get longer than they need to be. The KL divergence is that informational penalty for using a code built for one distribution on data from another. The penalty from English to Spanish differs from Spanish to English, which is why the measure is asymmetric. Lambert adds a Scrabble version: playing in Spanish with an English board’s letter frequencies.
  • A betting game. Urn p holds one red ball and nine grey cubes. You set fair odds from p: you pay a friend £1 for a grey cube, they pay you £9 for a red ball, and on urn p nobody wins on average. Now they draw from a second urn q with an unknown mix. If q matches p the divergence is zero and the game stays fair; if q holds more red balls you profit in the long run, and the divergence gauges how sure you can be of winning. When q holds nothing but balls you are certain to win, and the divergence is infinite.

In the model-comparison entry the same divergence explains why predictive accuracy is scored on the log scale. Here it does a different job: it is the thing VI minimises.

The trick: a bound you can compute

The obvious target, the divergence between the true posterior p^* and our approximation q, needs expectations under p^*, which is the very thing we cannot compute. VI reverses the order and minimises \mathrm{KL}(q\,\|\,p^*), which only needs expectations under the simple q (Murphy 2012). Even that has a snag: evaluating p^*(x)=p(x,\mathcal D)/p(\mathcal D) needs the normalising constant p(\mathcal D), the evidence, which is usually the hardest quantity of all.

The way out is to work with the unnormalised posterior p(x,\mathcal D), which is easy to evaluate. Murphy shows that the divergence from the unnormalised version is the true divergence minus the constant \log p(\mathcal D) (Murphy 2012). Constants do not move the optimum, so minimising the computable quantity minimises the one we wanted. Flip the sign and you get a lower bound on the log evidence,

\mathcal L(q) \;=\; \log p(\mathcal D) \;-\; \mathrm{KL}(q\,\|\,p^*) \;\le\; \log p(\mathcal D),

often called the evidence lower bound (ELBO). Raising the bound and shrinking the divergence are the same move, and the bound is tight exactly when q equals the posterior (Murphy 2012). If that looks familiar, it should: the EM algorithm climbs the same bound, with an E-step that can afford to set q to the exact posterior over the latent labels. VI is what you do when it can’t.

Murphy gives two readings of the objective (Murphy 2012):

  • Fit versus prior. Up to sign, it is the expected negative log-likelihood under q plus the KL divergence from q to the prior. Good approximations explain the data without straying further from the prior than the data demand, the same tension as a penalised regression.
  • Energy versus entropy. It is an expected “energy” minus the entropy of q, which is why physicists call it the variational free energy. Squeezing q onto the single best point lowers the energy but loses entropy; the optimum balances the two.

Which way round? Zero-forcing and zero-avoiding

Because KL is asymmetric, the direction matters, and it decides how the approximation fails (Murphy 2012).

  • Reverse KL, \mathrm{KL}(q\,\|\,p), the one VI uses, blows up wherever q puts mass and p has none. So q is zero-forcing: it keeps out of regions the posterior rules out, and typically under-estimates the support of p. Faced with two peaks, a single-peaked q locks onto one of them.
  • Forward KL, \mathrm{KL}(p\,\|\,q), blows up wherever p has mass and q has none. So q is zero-avoiding: it stretches to cover all of p, and with two peaks it sits between them, centring its mass in a valley where the true density is low.

Murphy’s verdict is that for a multimodal posterior the reverse direction is not only easier to compute but “more sensible statistically”: an approximation that describes one real mode is more useful than one centred on a point the posterior thinks is implausible (Murphy 2012).

The second case in the figure is the one that matters for routine use. Approximate an elongated, correlated 2-D Gaussian by a product of two independent Gaussians. Reverse KL gets the mean right but the approximation is too compact: its width in each direction is set by the posterior’s narrowest direction, not its marginal spread. Forward KL gives the product of the true marginals, which is too broad (Murphy 2012). Murphy’s general lesson: minimising reverse KL with a factorised q “often” (not always) gives an approximation that is overconfident (Murphy 2012).

Mean field: replace your neighbours by their average

The most popular form of VI is the mean-field approximation: assume the approximate posterior factorises completely, q(x)=\prod_i q_i(x_i), and optimise one factor at a time (Murphy 2012). The update for each factor has a memorable shape. Take the log of the unnormalised joint, average it over every other variable using their current q’s, and exponentiate. Only the variables that share a factor with x_j (its Markov blanket) matter; the rest fall into the normalising constant (Murphy 2012).

The name comes from what this does: each variable sees its neighbours replaced by their mean values (Murphy 2012). Murphy compares it with Gibbs sampling, which updates one variable at a time against sampled values of its neighbours; mean field passes means instead. A mean stands in for many samples, so it is usually more efficient, though sampling scales better in some very large models (Murphy 2012). Readers of the complexity track have met the idea under the same name: the mean-field approximation for epidemics replaces each node’s actual contacts by the average state of the population.

Nothing forces the factors to be Gaussian. Their form falls out of the model: a discrete variable gets a discrete q_j, a precision gets a Gamma, a mean gets a Gaussian (Murphy calls this free-form optimisation) (Murphy 2012).

Denoising an image, and a two-spin warning

Murphy’s showcase is cleaning a noisy black-and-white image with an Ising model (a Markov random field): each hidden pixel is \pm1, neighbouring pixels prefer to agree, and each observed pixel is a noisy reading of its hidden one (Murphy 2012). The mean-field update for pixel i turns out to be a hyperbolic tangent of two things added together: the weighted average of its neighbours’ current means, and the local evidence from its own noisy reading. Run in parallel sweeps over the image (his demonstration uses coupling 1, Gaussian noise with \sigma=2, 15 sweeps), the speckle clears. Murphy notes one practical detail: updating every pixel at once without damping (moving only part of the way to the new value each sweep) tends to leave checkerboard artefacts (Murphy 2012).

A two-pixel version shows both the mechanism and the overconfidence. The numbers are our own. Two spins prefer to agree (coupling 1); spin 1 has a little evidence for +1 (a field of 0.5); spin 2 has none.

Quantity Exact Mean field
P(\text{spin 1} = +1) 0.731 0.914
P(\text{spin 2} = +1) 0.676 0.839

Exactly, spin 2 has no evidence of its own, so summing it out leaves spin 1’s probability untouched at e^{0.5}/(e^{0.5}+e^{-0.5})=0.731. Mean field instead lets spin 1’s evidence flow to spin 2, then counts it again when spin 2’s mean flows back as if it were independent support. Four sweeps from zero give spin 1 means of 0.46, 0.73, 0.81, 0.82, climbing to a fixed point well past the truth. Both marginals end up too confident, and the approximation also understates how strongly the spins move together (implied E[x_1x_2]=0.56 against an exact 0.76). The arithmetic is in the appendix.

Structured mean field

Full independence is a strong assumption. Structured mean field keeps some dependence by grouping variables into blocks that are still tractable (Murphy 2012). Murphy’s example is the factorial HMM: several hidden Markov chains running side by side and jointly producing each observation. The chains are independent before you see the data but become coupled once you condition on a shared child. Exact inference costs O(TMK^{M+1}) for M chains of K states; approximating the posterior as a product of chains, each updated by its own forward–backward pass against an “approximate local evidence” that averages over the other chains, costs O(TMK^2I) with about I\approx10 iterations. That is the same cost as full factorisation, and much more accurate (Murphy 2012).

Variational Bayes: uncertainty about the parameters

So far the unknowns were latent variables with known parameters. Apply mean field to the parameters themselves and you get variational Bayes (VB) (Murphy 2012).

Murphy’s worked case, following MacKay, is the simplest one: a normal sample with unknown mean \mu and precision \lambda=1/\sigma^2, approximated as q(\mu)\,q(\lambda). You do not have to say what shape the factors take: the derivation produces a Gaussian for \mu and a Gamma for \lambda (Murphy 2012). The updates are coupled (the width of q(\mu) depends on the expected precision, and the Gamma’s rate depends on the expected squared deviations under q(\mu)), so they are iterated to a fixed point. Murphy’s figure shows the result is close to the exact posterior but more compact than it, and he states the general pattern: mean-field inference “often” underestimates posterior uncertainty (Murphy 2012).

The exact posterior is available for this model, which is why it is a good test. On eight readings of our own (mean 4.91, SD 0.74) with a nearly flat prior, VB reproduces the exact posterior mean of \mu (4.906) and the exact posterior mean of \lambda, but gives \mu a standard deviation of 0.253 against the exact 0.292: about 13% too narrow, because the factorisation throws away the dependence between \mu and \lambda (checked in the appendix). Murphy’s reason for doing VB on a model you could solve exactly is that the same recipe still works when a small change to the prior makes exact inference impossible (Murphy 2012).

Watch the bound. Murphy gives three reasons to compute \mathcal L(q) at every iteration (Murphy 2012): to judge convergence; to check the code, since the bound must rise at every update and a fall means a bug, just as with EM; and as an approximation to the marginal likelihood for model comparison. For Bayesian linear regression with learned hyper-parameters, Murphy finds VB’s model-selection answers close to empirical Bayes; with only five data points VB’s posterior over models is more diffuse, because it also carries uncertainty about the hyper-parameters (Murphy 2012).

VB-EM, and clusters that switch themselves off

Latent-variable models such as mixtures and HMMs have both kinds of unknown: one latent label per observation and shared parameters. Ordinary EM computes a posterior for the labels and a point estimate for the parameters. Variational Bayes EM (VBEM) approximates the joint posterior as q(\theta)\prod_i q(z_i) and alternates a variational E-step (labels, averaging over parameter uncertainty) with a variational M-step (updating the parameters’ posterior from expected sufficient statistics), at essentially EM’s cost (Murphy 2012). Standard EM is the special case in which q(\theta) is a single spike. Murphy calls VBEM “egalitarian”: parameters become first-class unknowns, like everything else (Murphy 2012).

Its most useful by-product is in choosing the number of clusters. Fit a Gaussian mixture with too many components and a Dirichlet prior on the weights with a very small concentration. In VBEM the effective weight of a component behaves roughly as though half a member were subtracted from its count, a penalty that hurts small clusters far more than large ones, like a regressive tax. Small clusters empty out over the iterations while popular ones grow (“rich get richer”). Murphy’s demonstration fits six Gaussians to the Old Faithful data with concentration 0.001; the data need two, and the other four are “killed off” (Murphy 2012). With a larger concentration the pruning does not happen (Murphy 2012). If you instead fit several K and compare bounds, add \log K! to each one: the bound describes one of the K! label-permuted copies of the posterior, not all of them (Murphy 2012).

Beyond mean field

Two further ideas complete Murphy’s chapter. Variational message passing (implemented in the VIBES software, a variational counterpart to the BUGS Gibbs sampler) automates the mean-field updates for any directed model with conjugate exponential-family pieces, sweeping over the graph much as Gibbs does (Murphy 2012). And local variational bounds approximate just one awkward term of the model rather than the whole posterior. Murphy’s lead example is Bayesian logistic regression, where this can do better than the Gaussian (Laplace) approximation (Murphy 2012).

Common pitfalls

WarningWhere this goes wrong
  • Reporting VI intervals as if they were exact. Mean field with reverse KL usually makes the posterior too narrow, and it understates correlations between unknowns. Treat its credible intervals as a lower bound on your real uncertainty, or check a few against MCMC. (Murphy 2012)
  • Missing a second mode. Reverse KL locks onto one peak and is silent about the others. Different starting values that converge to different answers are a symptom, not noise to average away. (Murphy 2012)
  • Ignoring a bound that goes down. The ELBO must rise at every coordinate update; a decrease is a bug in the derivation or the code. (Murphy 2012)
  • Reading the ELBO as the evidence. It is a lower bound on the log marginal likelihood, short of it by exactly the KL divergence you could not drive to zero. Comparing bounds across models compares approximations as well as models, and for mixtures needs the \log K! correction. (Murphy 2012)
  • Undamped parallel updates. Updating every variable at once can oscillate; Murphy’s image-denoising example shows checkerboard artefacts without damping. (Murphy 2012)
  • Treating pruned components as a discovery. VBEM’s automatic switching-off of clusters depends on the small Dirichlet concentration you chose; with a larger one the extra components survive. (Murphy 2012)
  • Calling the method Bayesian and stopping there. VI is Bayesian in its target but approximate in its answer, and the approximation errs in a known direction. Say which approximation you used.

Appendix · the mathematics

KL divergence. For distributions p and q, \mathrm{KL}(q\,\|\,p)=\sum_x q(x)\log\frac{q(x)}{p(x)}\;\ge\;0, with equality iff q=p (Gibbs’ inequality, from Jensen applied to the concave \log). Sums become integrals for densities.

The bound. Write \tilde p(x)=p(x,\mathcal D)=Z\,p^*(x) with Z=p(\mathcal D). Then \sum_x q(x)\log\frac{q(x)}{\tilde p(x)}=\mathrm{KL}(q\,\|\,p^*)-\log Z , so \mathcal L(q)\equiv\sum_x q(x)\log\frac{\tilde p(x)}{q(x)}=\log Z-\mathrm{KL}(q\,\|\,p^*)\le\log Z . Rearranged: \mathcal L(q)=\mathbb E_q[\log\tilde p(x)]+H(q) (negative energy plus entropy), or \mathcal L(q)=\mathbb E_q[\log p(\mathcal D\mid x)]-\mathrm{KL}(q(x)\,\|\,p(x)) (expected fit minus the divergence from the prior).

Mean-field update. With q=\prod_i q_i, collect the terms of \mathcal L involving q_j: \mathcal L(q_j)=\sum_{x_j}q_j(x_j)\log f_j(x_j)-\sum_{x_j}q_j(x_j)\log q_j(x_j)+\text{const},\qquad \log f_j(x_j)=\mathbb E_{-q_j}[\log\tilde p(x)], which is -\mathrm{KL}(q_j\,\|\,f_j) up to a constant. It is maximised by \log q_j(x_j)=\mathbb E_{-q_j}[\log\tilde p(x)]+\text{const}. Each such step cannot lower \mathcal L: coordinate ascent.

Ising update. For x_i\in\{-1,+1\} with \log\tilde p(x)=x_i\sum_{j\in\text{nbr}(i)}W_{ij}x_j+L_i(x_i)+\text{const}, averaging over the neighbours gives \mu_i=\tanh\!\Big(\sum_{j\in\text{nbr}(i)}W_{ij}\mu_j+\tfrac12\big(L_i(+1)-L_i(-1)\big)\Big), and the damped version is \mu_i^{t}=(1-\lambda)\mu_i^{t-1}+\lambda\tanh(\cdots).

The two-spin check. p(x_1,x_2)\propto\exp(x_1x_2+0.5x_1). Summing out x_2 gives 2\cosh(1)\,e^{0.5x_1}, so exactly P(x_1=+1)=e^{0.5}/(e^{0.5}+e^{-0.5})=0.731; enumerating the four states gives P(x_2=+1)=0.676 and E[x_1x_2]=0.762. Mean field iterates \mu_1=\tanh(\mu_2+0.5), \mu_2=\tanh(\mu_1) to \mu_1=0.827, \mu_2=0.679, i.e. P=(1+\mu)/2=0.914 and 0.839, with implied E[x_1x_2]=\mu_1\mu_2=0.562.

Correlated Gaussian. For p=\mathcal N(\mu,\Lambda^{-1}) in two dimensions, the reverse-KL factorised optimum is q=\mathcal N(x_1\mid m_1,\Lambda_{11}^{-1})\,\mathcal N(x_2\mid m_2,\Lambda_{22}^{-1}) with m_1=\mu_1-\Lambda_{11}^{-1}\Lambda_{12}(m_2-\mu_2) (and symmetrically), whose solution is m=\mu. With unit variances and correlation \rho, \Lambda_{11}^{-1}=1-\rho^2; at \rho=0.9 that is a variance of 0.19 (SD 0.44) against the true marginal variance of 1. The forward-KL factorised optimum is the product of the true marginals.

VB for a normal sample. Prior p(\mu,\lambda)=\mathcal N(\mu\mid\mu_0,(\kappa_0\lambda)^{-1})\, \mathrm{Ga}(\lambda\mid a_0,b_0), approximation q(\mu)q(\lambda). The optimal factors are q(\mu)=\mathcal N(\mu_N,\kappa_N^{-1}) and q(\lambda)=\mathrm{Ga}(a_N,b_N) with \mu_N=\frac{\kappa_0\mu_0+N\bar x}{\kappa_0+N},\quad \kappa_N=(\kappa_0+N)\,\frac{a_N}{b_N},\quad a_N=a_0+\frac{N+1}{2},\quad b_N=b_0+\tfrac12\,\mathbb E_{q(\mu)}\!\Big[\kappa_0(\mu-\mu_0)^2+\textstyle\sum_i(x_i-\mu)^2\Big], using \mathbb E[\mu^2]=\kappa_N^{-1}+\mu_N^2. Only \kappa_N and b_N need iterating. Our example: x=(4.2, 5.1, 3.8, 6.0, 4.9, 5.6, 4.4, 5.3), \mu_0=0, \kappa_0=a_0=b_0=0.01. At the fixed point \mu_N=4.906, E[\lambda]=1.951 and \mathrm{SD}_q(\mu)=\kappa_N^{-1/2}=0.253. The exact Normal–Gamma posterior has the same mean and the same E[\lambda], but its marginal for \mu is a Student-t with 2a_0+N degrees of freedom whose SD is 0.292.

Forward KL in the exponential family. Minimising \mathrm{KL}(p\,\|\,q) over Gaussians q matches moments: q takes p’s mean and variance. For the equal mixture of \mathcal N(\pm2,0.7^2) in the figure, that is mean 0 and SD \sqrt{0.49+4}=2.12.

::: {.lead-kernel}
**Variational inference (VI)** replaces an intractable posterior with the closest member of a tractable
family, where "closest" is measured by the **Kullback–Leibler divergence**. You cannot compute that distance
directly, but you can maximise a **lower bound** on the log evidence that is equivalent to it. The most common
version, **mean field**, pretends the unknowns are independent and updates each one against the *average* of
the others, which is fast but tends to understate uncertainty.
:::

## Why it matters

Bayesian inference ends in a posterior $p(x\mid\mathcal D)$, and for most interesting models nobody can
compute it exactly. Murphy lists the walls you hit: exact algorithms for discrete graphical models take time
exponential in the graph's treewidth; Gaussian models are cubic, still too slow with many variables; and
neither handles general continuous or mixed models [@murphy_ml]. The usual way round is
[MCMC](../02-inference/mcmc-in-depth.qmd), which is exact in the limit but can be slow, and is hard to judge
for convergence. Lambert points out the catch: to know whether a sampler has reached the posterior you would
need the posterior [@lambert_bayes].

Variational inference takes a different deal. Choose a family of simple distributions $q$, such as
independent Gaussians or a product of categorical distributions, and **search that family for the member
nearest the true posterior**. Inference becomes an **optimisation problem**, and Murphy's summary is that VI
"often gives us the speed benefits of MAP estimation but the statistical benefits of the Bayesian approach"
[@murphy_ml]. You get a whole distribution, not a point, at a cost close to fitting a point.

For a social scientist the most visible use is text. The [topic models](../04-predictive/text-as-data.qmd)
behind most computational text analysis were introduced by Blei, Ng and Jordan with a **variational EM**
algorithm [@jacobucci_ml]. In network analysis, the unknown block memberships of a
[stochastic block model](../complexity/statistical-network-models.qmd) (which students fall into which
friendship groups) can likewise be assigned by variational EM [@jacobucci_ml]. Large [hierarchical models](../02-inference/hierarchical-bayesian-models.qmd),
mixture models with many components and models with one latent variable per respondent are where VI earns
its place.

## The yardstick: KL divergence

To say "nearest" you need a distance between distributions. The standard choice is the
**Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence**. It is zero when the two distributions are identical, positive
otherwise, and **not symmetric**: the divergence of $q$ from $p$ is not the divergence of $p$ from $q$
[@lambert_bayes].

Lambert gives two pictures of what it measures [@lambert_bayes]:

- **Secret codes.** Morse code was tuned to English: the most common letter, *e*, gets the shortest symbol,
  a rare letter like *q* a long one. Use the English code to send Spanish and your messages get longer than
  they need to be. The KL divergence is that **informational penalty** for using a code built for one
  distribution on data from another. The penalty from English to Spanish differs from Spanish to English,
  which is why the measure is asymmetric. Lambert adds a Scrabble version: playing in Spanish with an English
  board's letter frequencies.
- **A betting game.** Urn $p$ holds one red ball and nine grey cubes. You set fair odds from $p$: you pay
  a friend £1 for a grey cube, they pay you £9 for a red ball, and on urn $p$ nobody wins on average. Now
  they draw from a second urn $q$ with an unknown mix. If $q$ matches $p$ the divergence is zero and the game
  stays fair; if $q$ holds more red balls you profit in the long run, and the divergence gauges how sure
  you can be of winning. When $q$ holds nothing but balls you are certain to win, and the divergence is
  infinite.

In the [model-comparison entry](../02-inference/bayesian-model-comparison.qmd#sec-why-log-score) the same
divergence explains why predictive accuracy is scored on the log scale. Here it does a different job: it is
the thing VI minimises.

## The trick: a bound you *can* compute

The obvious target, the divergence between the true posterior $p^*$ and our approximation $q$, needs
expectations under $p^*$, which is the very thing we cannot compute. VI reverses the order and minimises
$\mathrm{KL}(q\,\|\,p^*)$, which only needs expectations under the simple $q$ [@murphy_ml]. Even that has a
snag: evaluating $p^*(x)=p(x,\mathcal D)/p(\mathcal D)$ needs the normalising constant $p(\mathcal D)$, the
evidence, which is usually the hardest quantity of all.

The way out is to work with the **unnormalised** posterior $p(x,\mathcal D)$, which is easy to evaluate.
Murphy shows that the divergence from the unnormalised version is the true divergence minus the constant
$\log p(\mathcal D)$ [@murphy_ml]. Constants do not move the optimum, so minimising the computable quantity
minimises the one we wanted. Flip the sign and you get a **lower bound** on the log evidence,

$$
\mathcal L(q) \;=\; \log p(\mathcal D) \;-\; \mathrm{KL}(q\,\|\,p^*) \;\le\; \log p(\mathcal D),
$$

often called the **evidence lower bound (ELBO)**. Raising the bound and shrinking the divergence are the
same move, and the bound is tight exactly when $q$ equals the posterior [@murphy_ml]. If that looks
familiar, it should: the [EM algorithm](mixture-models-and-em.qmd) climbs the same bound, with an E-step that
can afford to set $q$ to the *exact* posterior over the latent labels. VI is what you do when it can't.

Murphy gives two readings of the objective [@murphy_ml]:

- **Fit versus prior.** Up to sign, it is the expected negative log-likelihood under $q$ *plus* the KL
  divergence from $q$ to the prior. Good approximations explain the data without straying further from the
  prior than the data demand, the same tension as a penalised regression.
- **Energy versus entropy.** It is an expected "energy" minus the entropy of $q$, which is why physicists
  call it the **variational free energy**. Squeezing $q$ onto the single best point lowers the energy but
  loses entropy; the optimum balances the two.

## Which way round? Zero-forcing and zero-avoiding

Because KL is asymmetric, the direction matters, and it decides how the approximation fails
[@murphy_ml].

- **Reverse KL**, $\mathrm{KL}(q\,\|\,p)$, the one VI uses, blows up wherever $q$ puts mass and $p$ has none.
  So $q$ is **zero-forcing**: it keeps out of regions the posterior rules out, and typically
  **under-estimates the support** of $p$. Faced with two peaks, a single-peaked $q$ **locks onto one of
  them**.
- **Forward KL**, $\mathrm{KL}(p\,\|\,q)$, blows up wherever $p$ has mass and $q$ has none. So $q$ is
  **zero-avoiding**: it stretches to **cover** all of $p$, and with two peaks it sits between them, centring
  its mass in a valley where the true density is low.

Murphy's verdict is that for a multimodal posterior the reverse direction is not only easier to compute but
"more sensible statistically": an approximation that describes one real mode is more useful than one centred
on a point the posterior thinks is implausible [@murphy_ml].

The second case in the figure is the one that matters for routine use. Approximate an elongated, correlated
2-D Gaussian by a product of two independent Gaussians. Reverse KL gets the **mean right** but the
approximation is **too compact**: its width in each direction is set by the posterior's narrowest
direction, not its marginal spread. Forward KL gives the product of the true marginals, which is **too
broad** [@murphy_ml]. Murphy's general lesson: minimising reverse KL with a factorised $q$ "often" (not
always) gives an approximation that is **overconfident** [@murphy_ml].

## Mean field: replace your neighbours by their average

The most popular form of VI is the **mean-field approximation**: assume the approximate posterior factorises
completely, $q(x)=\prod_i q_i(x_i)$, and optimise one factor at a time [@murphy_ml]. The update for each factor
has a memorable shape. Take the log of the unnormalised joint, **average it over every other variable** using
their current $q$'s, and exponentiate. Only the variables that share a factor with $x_j$ (its Markov blanket)
matter; the rest fall into the normalising constant [@murphy_ml].

The name comes from what this does: each variable sees its neighbours **replaced by their mean values**
[@murphy_ml]. Murphy compares it with [Gibbs sampling](../02-inference/mcmc-in-depth.qmd), which updates one
variable at a time against *sampled* values of its neighbours; mean field passes *means* instead. A mean
stands in for many samples, so it is usually more efficient, though sampling scales better in some very large
models [@murphy_ml]. Readers of the complexity track have met the idea under the same name: the
[mean-field approximation for epidemics](../complexity/spreading-on-networks.qmd#sec-mean-field) replaces each
node's actual contacts by the average state of the population.

Nothing forces the factors to be Gaussian. Their form **falls out of the model**: a discrete variable gets a
discrete $q_j$, a precision gets a Gamma, a mean gets a Gaussian (Murphy calls this free-form optimisation)
[@murphy_ml].

### Denoising an image, and a two-spin warning

Murphy's showcase is cleaning a noisy black-and-white image with an **Ising model** (a [Markov random field](markov-random-fields.qmd)): each hidden pixel is
$\pm1$, neighbouring pixels prefer to agree, and each observed pixel is a noisy reading of its hidden one
[@murphy_ml]. The mean-field update for pixel $i$ turns out to be a hyperbolic tangent of two things added
together: the weighted average of its neighbours' current means, and the local evidence from its own noisy
reading. Run in parallel sweeps over the image (his demonstration uses coupling 1, Gaussian noise with
$\sigma=2$, 15 sweeps), the speckle clears. Murphy notes one practical detail: updating every pixel at once
without **damping** (moving only part of the way to the new value each sweep) tends to leave
**checkerboard artefacts** [@murphy_ml].

A two-pixel version shows both the mechanism and the overconfidence. The numbers are our own. Two spins
prefer to agree (coupling 1); spin 1 has a little evidence for $+1$ (a field of 0.5); spin 2 has none.

| Quantity | Exact | Mean field |
|---|---|---|
| $P(\text{spin 1} = +1)$ | 0.731 | **0.914** |
| $P(\text{spin 2} = +1)$ | 0.676 | **0.839** |

Exactly, spin 2 has no evidence of its own, so summing it out leaves spin 1's probability untouched at
$e^{0.5}/(e^{0.5}+e^{-0.5})=0.731$. Mean field instead lets spin 1's evidence flow to spin 2, then counts
it again when spin 2's mean flows back as if it were independent support. Four sweeps from zero give spin 1
means of 0.46, 0.73, 0.81, 0.82, climbing to a fixed point well past the truth. Both marginals end up too
confident, and the approximation also understates how strongly the spins move together (implied
$E[x_1x_2]=0.56$ against an exact 0.76). The arithmetic is in the [appendix](#sec-vi-math).

### Structured mean field

Full independence is a strong assumption. **Structured mean field** keeps some dependence by grouping
variables into blocks that are still tractable [@murphy_ml]. Murphy's example is the **factorial HMM**:
several [hidden Markov chains](hidden-markov-models.qmd) running side by side and jointly producing each
observation. The chains are independent before you see the data but become coupled once you condition on a
shared child. Exact inference costs $O(TMK^{M+1})$ for $M$ chains of $K$ states; approximating the posterior
as a **product of chains**, each updated by its own forward–backward pass against an "approximate local
evidence" that averages over the other chains, costs $O(TMK^2I)$ with about $I\approx10$ iterations. That is
the same cost as full factorisation, and much more accurate [@murphy_ml].

## Variational Bayes: uncertainty about the parameters

So far the unknowns were latent variables with known parameters. Apply mean field to the **parameters**
themselves and you get **variational Bayes (VB)** [@murphy_ml].

Murphy's worked case, following MacKay, is the simplest one: a normal sample with unknown mean $\mu$ and
precision $\lambda=1/\sigma^2$, approximated as $q(\mu)\,q(\lambda)$. You do not have to say what shape the
factors take: the derivation produces a Gaussian for $\mu$ and a Gamma for $\lambda$ [@murphy_ml]. The
updates are coupled (the width of $q(\mu)$ depends on the expected precision, and the Gamma's rate depends on
the expected squared deviations under $q(\mu)$), so they are iterated to a fixed point. Murphy's figure shows
the result is close to the exact posterior but **more compact** than it, and he states the general pattern:
mean-field inference "often" **underestimates posterior uncertainty** [@murphy_ml].

The exact posterior is available for this model, which is why it is a good test. On eight readings of our own
(mean 4.91, SD 0.74) with a nearly flat prior, VB reproduces the exact posterior mean of $\mu$ (4.906) and
the exact posterior mean of $\lambda$, but gives $\mu$ a standard deviation of **0.253** against the exact
**0.292**: about 13% too narrow, because the factorisation throws away the dependence between $\mu$ and
$\lambda$ (checked in the [appendix](#sec-vi-math)). Murphy's reason for doing VB on a model you could solve
exactly is that the same recipe still works when a small change to the prior makes exact inference
impossible [@murphy_ml].

**Watch the bound.** Murphy gives three reasons to compute $\mathcal L(q)$ at every iteration
[@murphy_ml]: to judge **convergence**; to check the **code**, since the bound must rise at every update and
a fall means a bug, just as with EM; and as an approximation to the **marginal likelihood** for
[model comparison](../02-inference/bayesian-model-comparison.qmd). For Bayesian linear regression with
learned hyper-parameters, Murphy finds VB's model-selection answers close to empirical Bayes; with only
five data points VB's posterior over models is more diffuse, because it also carries uncertainty about the
hyper-parameters [@murphy_ml].

### VB-EM, and clusters that switch themselves off

Latent-variable models such as mixtures and HMMs have both kinds of unknown: one latent label per
observation and shared parameters. Ordinary [EM](mixture-models-and-em.qmd) computes a posterior for the
labels and a point estimate for the parameters. **Variational Bayes EM (VBEM)** approximates the joint
posterior as $q(\theta)\prod_i q(z_i)$ and alternates a variational E-step (labels, averaging over parameter
uncertainty) with a variational M-step (updating the parameters' posterior from expected sufficient
statistics), at essentially EM's cost [@murphy_ml]. Standard EM is the special case in which $q(\theta)$ is a
single spike. Murphy calls VBEM "egalitarian": parameters become first-class unknowns, like everything else
[@murphy_ml].

Its most useful by-product is in **choosing the number of clusters**. Fit a Gaussian mixture with too many
components and a Dirichlet prior on the weights with a very small concentration. In VBEM the effective
weight of a component behaves roughly as though half a member were subtracted from its count, a penalty that
hurts small clusters far more than large ones, like a **regressive tax**. Small clusters empty out over the
iterations while popular ones grow ("**rich get richer**"). Murphy's demonstration fits six Gaussians to the
Old Faithful data with concentration 0.001; the data need two, and the other four are "killed off"
[@murphy_ml]. With a larger concentration the pruning does not happen [@murphy_ml]. If you instead fit
several $K$ and compare bounds, add $\log K!$ to each one: the bound describes one of the $K!$ label-permuted
copies of the posterior, not all of them [@murphy_ml].

### Beyond mean field

Two further ideas complete Murphy's chapter. **Variational message passing** (implemented in the VIBES
software, a variational counterpart to the BUGS Gibbs sampler) automates the mean-field updates for any
directed model with conjugate exponential-family pieces, sweeping over the graph much as Gibbs does
[@murphy_ml]. And **local variational bounds** approximate just one awkward term of the model rather than the
whole posterior. Murphy's lead example is Bayesian logistic regression, where this can do better than the
Gaussian (Laplace) approximation [@murphy_ml].

## Common pitfalls

::: {.callout-warning title="Where this goes wrong"}
- **Reporting VI intervals as if they were exact.** Mean field with reverse KL usually makes the posterior
  too narrow, and it understates correlations between unknowns. Treat its credible intervals as a lower
  bound on your real uncertainty, or check a few against MCMC. [@murphy_ml]
- **Missing a second mode.** Reverse KL locks onto one peak and is silent about the others. Different
  starting values that converge to different answers are a symptom, not noise to average away. [@murphy_ml]
- **Ignoring a bound that goes down.** The ELBO must rise at every coordinate update; a decrease is a bug in
  the derivation or the code. [@murphy_ml]
- **Reading the ELBO as the evidence.** It is a *lower bound* on the log marginal likelihood, short of it by
  exactly the KL divergence you could not drive to zero. Comparing bounds across models compares
  approximations as well as models, and for mixtures needs the $\log K!$ correction. [@murphy_ml]
- **Undamped parallel updates.** Updating every variable at once can oscillate; Murphy's image-denoising
  example shows checkerboard artefacts without damping. [@murphy_ml]
- **Treating pruned components as a discovery.** VBEM's automatic switching-off of clusters depends on the
  small Dirichlet concentration you chose; with a larger one the extra components survive. [@murphy_ml]
- **Calling the method Bayesian and stopping there.** VI is Bayesian in its target but approximate in its
  answer, and the approximation errs in a known direction. Say which approximation you used.
:::

## Appendix · the mathematics {#sec-vi-math .unnumbered}

::: {.math-appendix}

**KL divergence.** For distributions $p$ and $q$,
$$
\mathrm{KL}(q\,\|\,p)=\sum_x q(x)\log\frac{q(x)}{p(x)}\;\ge\;0,
$$
with equality iff $q=p$ (Gibbs' inequality, from Jensen applied to the concave $\log$). Sums become integrals
for densities.

**The bound.** Write $\tilde p(x)=p(x,\mathcal D)=Z\,p^*(x)$ with $Z=p(\mathcal D)$. Then
$$
\sum_x q(x)\log\frac{q(x)}{\tilde p(x)}=\mathrm{KL}(q\,\|\,p^*)-\log Z ,
$$
so
$$
\mathcal L(q)\equiv\sum_x q(x)\log\frac{\tilde p(x)}{q(x)}=\log Z-\mathrm{KL}(q\,\|\,p^*)\le\log Z .
$$
Rearranged: $\mathcal L(q)=\mathbb E_q[\log\tilde p(x)]+H(q)$ (negative energy plus entropy), or
$\mathcal L(q)=\mathbb E_q[\log p(\mathcal D\mid x)]-\mathrm{KL}(q(x)\,\|\,p(x))$ (expected fit minus the
divergence from the prior).

**Mean-field update.** With $q=\prod_i q_i$, collect the terms of $\mathcal L$ involving $q_j$:
$$
\mathcal L(q_j)=\sum_{x_j}q_j(x_j)\log f_j(x_j)-\sum_{x_j}q_j(x_j)\log q_j(x_j)+\text{const},\qquad
\log f_j(x_j)=\mathbb E_{-q_j}[\log\tilde p(x)],
$$
which is $-\mathrm{KL}(q_j\,\|\,f_j)$ up to a constant. It is maximised by
$\log q_j(x_j)=\mathbb E_{-q_j}[\log\tilde p(x)]+\text{const}$. Each such step cannot lower $\mathcal L$:
coordinate ascent.

**Ising update.** For $x_i\in\{-1,+1\}$ with $\log\tilde p(x)=x_i\sum_{j\in\text{nbr}(i)}W_{ij}x_j+L_i(x_i)+\text{const}$,
averaging over the neighbours gives
$$
\mu_i=\tanh\!\Big(\sum_{j\in\text{nbr}(i)}W_{ij}\mu_j+\tfrac12\big(L_i(+1)-L_i(-1)\big)\Big),
$$
and the damped version is $\mu_i^{t}=(1-\lambda)\mu_i^{t-1}+\lambda\tanh(\cdots)$.

**The two-spin check.** $p(x_1,x_2)\propto\exp(x_1x_2+0.5x_1)$. Summing out $x_2$ gives
$2\cosh(1)\,e^{0.5x_1}$, so exactly $P(x_1=+1)=e^{0.5}/(e^{0.5}+e^{-0.5})=0.731$; enumerating the four states
gives $P(x_2=+1)=0.676$ and $E[x_1x_2]=0.762$. Mean field iterates $\mu_1=\tanh(\mu_2+0.5)$,
$\mu_2=\tanh(\mu_1)$ to $\mu_1=0.827$, $\mu_2=0.679$, i.e. $P=(1+\mu)/2=0.914$ and $0.839$, with implied
$E[x_1x_2]=\mu_1\mu_2=0.562$.

**Correlated Gaussian.** For $p=\mathcal N(\mu,\Lambda^{-1})$ in two dimensions, the reverse-KL factorised
optimum is $q=\mathcal N(x_1\mid m_1,\Lambda_{11}^{-1})\,\mathcal N(x_2\mid m_2,\Lambda_{22}^{-1})$ with
$m_1=\mu_1-\Lambda_{11}^{-1}\Lambda_{12}(m_2-\mu_2)$ (and symmetrically), whose solution is $m=\mu$. With unit
variances and correlation $\rho$, $\Lambda_{11}^{-1}=1-\rho^2$; at $\rho=0.9$ that is a variance of 0.19
(SD 0.44) against the true marginal variance of 1. The forward-KL factorised optimum is the product of the
true marginals.

**VB for a normal sample.** Prior $p(\mu,\lambda)=\mathcal N(\mu\mid\mu_0,(\kappa_0\lambda)^{-1})\,
\mathrm{Ga}(\lambda\mid a_0,b_0)$, approximation $q(\mu)q(\lambda)$. The optimal factors are
$q(\mu)=\mathcal N(\mu_N,\kappa_N^{-1})$ and $q(\lambda)=\mathrm{Ga}(a_N,b_N)$ with
$$
\mu_N=\frac{\kappa_0\mu_0+N\bar x}{\kappa_0+N},\quad
\kappa_N=(\kappa_0+N)\,\frac{a_N}{b_N},\quad
a_N=a_0+\frac{N+1}{2},\quad
b_N=b_0+\tfrac12\,\mathbb E_{q(\mu)}\!\Big[\kappa_0(\mu-\mu_0)^2+\textstyle\sum_i(x_i-\mu)^2\Big],
$$
using $\mathbb E[\mu^2]=\kappa_N^{-1}+\mu_N^2$. Only $\kappa_N$ and $b_N$ need iterating. Our example:
$x=(4.2, 5.1, 3.8, 6.0, 4.9, 5.6, 4.4, 5.3)$, $\mu_0=0$, $\kappa_0=a_0=b_0=0.01$. At the fixed point
$\mu_N=4.906$, $E[\lambda]=1.951$ and $\mathrm{SD}_q(\mu)=\kappa_N^{-1/2}=0.253$. The exact Normal–Gamma
posterior has the same mean and the same $E[\lambda]$, but its marginal for $\mu$ is a Student-$t$ with
$2a_0+N$ degrees of freedom whose SD is $0.292$.

**Forward KL in the exponential family.** Minimising $\mathrm{KL}(p\,\|\,q)$ over Gaussians $q$ matches
moments: $q$ takes $p$'s mean and variance. For the equal mixture of $\mathcal N(\pm2,0.7^2)$ in the figure,
that is mean 0 and SD $\sqrt{0.49+4}=2.12$.
